** If you want to go and shoot right now

Part 2 is an hour of setup, and it is worth it. But if the camera is in your hands
and you want to take pictures today, this will get you a long way:

1. Mode dial to *=A=*
2. *Photo Shooting menu → Image quality → NEF (RAW)*
3. *Photo Shooting menu → ISO sensitivity settings*: Auto ISO =On=, Maximum
   sensitivity =6400=, Minimum shutter speed =Auto=
4. Aperture to *f/5.6* for one subject, *f/8* for a scene
5. Focus mode =AF-S=, AF-area mode Single-point
6. Adjust the *diopter* (the wheel by the viewfinder) until the numbers along the
   bottom are sharp — thirty seconds, and everything looks better afterwards

Go and take two hundred photographs. Come back to Part 1 tonight.

** Who this is written for

Someone who has shot a Panasonic FZ200 and is now holding a Nikon D7200 with an
18–140mm lens. That is a specific situation and it has specific consequences,
good and bad. The next chapter is about exactly that, and it is the most
important chapter in the book for you. Do not skip it.

You are assumed to know nothing about DSLRs and nothing about editing. You are

** Every fact here was checked

The camera-specific claims in this guide — menu locations, custom setting
numbers, frame rates, buffer depths, magnifications, sync speeds — were verified
against Nikon's own /D7200 User Manual/ and /D7200 Menu Guide/, not against
memory and not against the internet.

*** What to buy, in order

Do not buy any of this until the course tells you that you need it. This list
exists so that when you feel a limit, you know whether money fixes it.

1. *A second EN-EL15 battery — $40–60.* Buy this now, before anything else. The
   D7200 is good on battery for viewfinder work and bad on it in live view and
   cold. Running flat mid-outing is the only gear failure that ends a day
   outright. Genuine Nikon or a reputable third party; avoid the $10 ones.

2. *A circular polarizer, 67 mm — $40–90.* Your 18–140mm takes 67mm filters.
   A polarizer cuts reflections off leaves, water and glass, and deepens blue
   sky at 90° to the sun. It is the only filter effect that darktable cannot
   reproduce, because it removes light that never reached the sensor. Directly
   useful for your zoo work: it cuts reflections off enclosure glass.
   Skip cheap ones — a bad polarizer costs you sharpness and adds a colour cast.

3. *A Speedlight — $150–300 used.* Only once Part 7 has convinced you that you
   want it. An SB-700 or SB-600 used is the sweet spot, and both work with the
   D7200's built-in Commander mode, which is a genuinely premium feature you
   already own.

4. *A second lens.* Deliberately last, and Book II has a chapter arguing about
   which. Short version: your next lens should be a fast prime for low light and
   people, not more zoom range. The 35mm f/1.8G DX is the standard answer and
   it is the right one.

Things you do *not* need, despite what the internet says: a monitor calibrator,
extension tubes, a focusing rail, ND filters, a second camera body, a battery
grip, or a "professional" bag.

* Book I — The Course

** Part 1 — Coming from the FZ200

You are not a beginner. You are a beginner *at this camera*. Those are very
different problems, and every generic "your first DSLR" guide solves the wrong
one.

This chapter is the translation layer. Read it before you touch a menu.

*** What you already know, and keep

The FZ200 taught you more than you probably credit it for. All of this transfers
intact:

- *Aperture, shutter speed and ISO.* Identical concepts, identical numbers,
  identical trade-offs. f/5.6 means the same thing on both cameras.
- *P / A / S / M.* Same four modes, same behaviour. Nikon calls Shutter Priority
  =S=; Panasonic called it =S= too. Nothing to relearn.
- *Raw files.* You already shot raw. The D7200's =.NEF= is the same idea with
  more room in it.
- *Composition and seeing.* Entirely camera-independent, and the hardest part of
  photography. You keep all of it.
- *Exposure compensation.* Same dial, same logic.

What you are actually learning is: a much larger sensor and what it lets you do,
a real autofocus system with far more control than you have ever had, an optical
viewfinder, and interchangeable lenses. That is a shorter list than "photography
from scratch."

*** What genuinely got better

**** The sensor is thirteen times larger

| Camera | Sensor        | Approx. area |
|--------+---------------+--------------|
| FZ200  | 1/2.3"        | ~28 mm²      |
| D7200  | APS-C (DX)    | ~367 mm²     |

That is roughly thirteen times the light-collecting area, or about 3.7 stops.
In practice: *ISO 3200 on the D7200 looks roughly like ISO 250–400 did on the
FZ200.* Low light stopped being a wall and became a slider.

This one fact is why the whole trade is worth it, and it is the answer to most
of the complaints in the next section.

**** You can finally blur a background

On the FZ200 at normal focal lengths, essentially everything was sharp whether
you wanted it or not. A small sensor gives enormous depth of field — convenient,
and creatively a straitjacket.

At 140mm f/5.6 on the D7200, with your subject a few metres from their
background, the background genuinely melts. This is the single biggest creative
gain of the change, and Part 6 is largely about exploiting it.

**** Raw files that survive being pushed

The FZ200's files broke if you lifted the shadows much. The D7200's sensor has
unusually good dynamic range even by DSLR standards, and 14-bit files take a
+2 or +3 stop shadow lift without falling apart. You will use this constantly.

**** Real autofocus

51 points, 15 of them cross-type, with genuine continuous tracking. The FZ200's
contrast-detect AF was fine for static subjects and hopeless on anything moving
toward you. Part 4 is about learning to actually drive this.

*** What genuinely got worse — read this before you get annoyed

I would rather you hear this from me now than discover it at the zoo.

**** You lost two thirds of your reach

This is the big one and it will sting.

| Camera            | Equivalent focal range | Long end |
|-------------------+------------------------+----------|
| FZ200             | 25–600 mm              | 600 mm   |
| D7200 + 18–140 mm | 27–210 mm              | 210 mm   |

Your wide end is essentially unchanged. Your long end went from 600 mm to
210 mm. A distant animal will appear *roughly three times smaller* in the frame
than you are used to.

There is no way to talk you out of this; it is simply true. What you can do:

1. *Use the 1.3× crop mode* (Part 2). It gets you to about 273 mm equivalent
   for free, and it makes the camera faster while doing it. It is the closest
   thing to a fix that costs nothing.
2. *Crop in editing.* You have 24 megapixels. The FZ200 had 12. A half-frame
   crop of a D7200 file still has more pixels than a full FZ200 frame, and they
   are far better pixels. Cropping is not cheating.
3. *Get closer.* Genuinely — most of the reach you think you need is a
   fieldcraft problem. See Chapter 6.5, on the zoo.
4. *Eventually, a longer lens.* A used 55–200mm or 70–300mm DX gets you to
   300–450 mm equivalent. Book II discusses this. Do not buy it yet.

**** You lost the constant f/2.8

The FZ200's headline feature was f/2.8 at every focal length, including 600 mm.
That was remarkable and you will miss it. Your 18–140mm is f/3.5 at the wide end
and f/5.6 at the long end — about two stops darker at full zoom.

The consolation is real, though: two stops darker on a sensor that is nearly
four stops cleaner still leaves you ahead. Shooting 140mm f/5.6 at ISO 3200 on
the D7200 beats 600mm f/2.8 at ISO 400 on the FZ200 for image quality. You just
have to be willing to raise the ISO, which brings us to the mental adjustment
that matters most:

#+begin_quote
*Stop being frightened of ISO.* On the FZ200, ISO 800 was a disaster and you
learned to avoid it. That reflex is now actively harming your photographs. On
the D7200, ISO 3200 is a perfectly good file, and a sharp ISO 6400 shot beats a
blurred ISO 400 one every single time. Part 3 has the numbers.
#+end_quote

**** You lost close focus

The FZ200 could focus to about a centimetre. Your 18–140mm cannot focus closer
than *0.45 m* (about 18 inches) from the sensor plane, at any focal length.

Its maximum reproduction ratio is *0.23×*, which means the smallest subject that
can fill the frame's width is about *10 cm* — a large flower head, a mug, a
fist. Good for flowers. Useless for insects.

If you were fond of the FZ200's 1cm macro mode, that capability is genuinely
gone until you buy something. Chapter 6.4 covers what you *can*
do, which is more than you would expect.

**** You lost frames per second, the articulating screen, and video autofocus

- The FZ200 shot 12 fps. Your D7200 shoots 5–7 depending on settings (Part 2).
- The FZ200's screen flipped out. The D7200's is fixed. Low and overhead angles
  are now genuinely awkward.
- The FZ200's video autofocus was usable. The D7200's is poor. If video matters
  to you, manual focus is the only real answer.

*** The thing that is neither better nor worse, but will feel wrong for a week

**** The optical viewfinder

On the FZ200 you looked at a small screen showing the *processed image*. Change
the exposure and the view got brighter or darker. What you saw was what you got.

The D7200's viewfinder is a mirror and a prism. You are looking at *the actual
scene through the lens*, in real light. It is bigger, clearer, lag-free, works
in bright sun, and never blacks out except at the moment of exposure.

It also *does not preview your exposure*. Set a wildly wrong exposure and the
viewfinder looks exactly the same. This is the single most disorienting part of
the transition, and it is why Part 3 spends so long on the meter, the histogram
and the highlight warning — those replace the reassurance the EVF used to give
you for free.

If you want the old behaviour back for a shot, press =Lv=. Live view puts a
real-time, exposure-accurate image on the rear screen, exactly like the FZ200.
It is slower and the autofocus is worse, but for a tripod landscape or a
close-up it is the better tool. You have not lost the EVF; you have gained a
second way of working alongside it.

*** Gate

Before Part 2, you should be able to say out loud:

- Why the D7200 beats the FZ200 in low light, and by roughly how much
- Why it does not beat it for reach, and what your three options are
- Why the viewfinder does not get darker when you underexpose

** Part 2 — Setting the camera up, once

Do this once, properly, and then stop thinking about menus. That is the entire
goal of this part.

Everything below was checked against Nikon's /D7200 Menu Guide/. Where a setting
lives in a numbered Custom Setting, the number given is the number on *your*
camera.

*** How the menu system is organised

Press =MENU=. Seven tabs run down the left side:

| Tab             | What lives there                              |
|-----------------+-----------------------------------------------|
| Playback        | Reviewing, deleting, slideshows               |
| Photo Shooting  | Image quality, ISO, white balance, bracketing |
| Movie Shooting  | Video settings only                           |
| Custom Settings | The a/b/c/d/e/f/g behaviour switches          |
| Setup           | Format, language, firmware, user settings     |
| Retouch         | In-camera edits                               |
| My Menu         | Your own shortlist — set this up last          |

The Custom Settings tab is the one that matters and the one everyone gets wrong.
It is divided into lettered groups:

| Group | Covers                        |
|-------+-------------------------------|
| =a=   | Autofocus                     |
| =b=   | Metering and exposure         |
| =c=   | Timers and AE lock            |
| =d=   | Shooting and display          |
| =e=   | Bracketing and flash          |
| =f=   | Controls (button assignments) |
| =g=   | Movie controls                |

#+begin_quote
*Why other guides get this wrong.* Nikon renumbers these between models. Advice
copied from a D7100, D750 or D7500 will cite numbers that mean something else on
your camera. On the D7200, =d7= is the viewfinder grid; on a D7500 it is =d6=.
If a setting is not where a website says it is, the website is describing a
different camera. Book II has the full verified map.
#+end_quote

*** Step 1 — The essentials

**** Format the card

*Setup menu → Format memory card → Slot 1 → Yes.*

Do this now, and do it after every import to the computer. Formatting in-camera
rebuilds the folder structure properly; deleting files on the computer does not.

Never format a card you have not backed up. There is no undo.

**** Put your name in every file

*Setup menu → Copyright information.* Fill in *Artist* and *Copyright*, then set
*Attach copyright information* to =On=.

Every file from now on carries your name in its metadata. It takes two minutes,
and it is the only copyright step that is genuinely automatic.

There is also *Setup menu → Image comment* if you want a free-text note attached
to every frame. Optional.

**** Set the clock properly

*Setup menu → Time zone and date.* Get this right, including the time zone. Every
file is stamped with it and your editing software sorts by it. Fixing thousands
of wrong timestamps later is miserable.

**** Set the diopter — this is not optional

To the right of the viewfinder is a small knurled wheel. It focuses *the
viewfinder display for your eyes*. It has nothing to do with focusing the lens.

1. Take the lens cap off and point the camera at something bright and plain.
2. Look through the viewfinder at the *row of numbers along the bottom*, not at
   the scene.
3. Turn the wheel until those numbers are as crisp as they get.

If you skip this, everything will look slightly soft in the viewfinder forever
and you will blame your lens. It is the most commonly skipped setup step there
is.

*** Step 2 — File format

**** Shoot raw

*Photo Shooting menu → Image quality → NEF (RAW).*

Not RAW+JPEG. Raw only.

Many beginner guides tell you to shoot RAW+JPEG "for safety." That advice made
sense when raw converters were expensive and awkward. You have darktable
installed. The JPEG doubles your storage and gives you a second file to manage
that you will never open.

The one real exception: if you need to hand someone a photo *straight off the
card* with no computer involved. That is rare, and you can switch to RAW+JPEG
Fine for a day when it comes up.

**** Set the raw type and bit depth

*Photo Shooting menu → NEF (RAW) recording:*

- *Type* → =Lossless compressed=
- *NEF (RAW) bit depth* → =14-bit=

Lossless compressed is exactly what it sounds like: smaller files, zero quality
loss. There is no reason to use uncompressed.

14-bit gives finer tonal gradation than 12-bit, which matters when you lift
shadows hard — which you will. The cost is speed, and it is a real cost:

| Setting         | File size | Frames/sec (DX) | Buffer | Burst  |
|-----------------+-----------+-----------------+--------+--------|
| 14-bit lossless | ~28 MB    | 5 fps           | 18     | ~3.6 s |
| 12-bit lossless | ~22 MB    | 6 fps           | 27     | ~4.5 s |

Stay on 14-bit as your default. Drop to 12-bit deliberately on a day you know is
about action — a fast zoo visit, kids running. Part 6 covers when.

#+begin_quote
*Watch out —* You will read everywhere that the D7200 shoots "6 fps."
It does — at 12-bit, or in the crop mode. At the 14-bit setting recommended
above, DX is *5 fps*. Nikon's own capacity table confirms it. Nobody mentions
this because nobody checks.
#+end_quote

**** Colour space

*Photo Shooting menu → Color space → sRGB.*

Since you shoot raw, this only affects the embedded preview, so it barely
matters. sRGB is the safe default. Ignore anyone who says Adobe RGB is "better" —
it is only better inside a colour-managed workflow you do not have yet, and it
makes files look flat everywhere else.

*** Step 3 — In-camera processing, and why most of it does nothing

Here is a rule that saves a great deal of confusion:

#+begin_quote
*Because you shoot raw, most in-camera image processing does not touch your
actual file.* Picture Controls, High ISO NR, Active D-Lighting, Vignette Control
and Auto Distortion Control all bake into JPEGs. On a raw file they only change
the preview you see on the back of the camera.
#+end_quote

That has one important consequence, and it is worth remembering:

*The histogram and highlight warning in playback are computed from that JPEG
preview, not from your raw data.* Your raw file always holds a little more
highlight room than the blinkies suggest — roughly a third to two-thirds of a
stop. You will use that fact in Part 3.

Set these once:

| Setting                 | Value    | Why                                        |
|-------------------------+----------+--------------------------------------------|
| High ISO NR             | =Normal= | Preview only; real denoise happens later    |
| Long exposure NR        | =Off=    | See below                                   |
| Active D-Lighting       | =Off=    | Preview only, and it skews the meter        |
| Auto distortion control | =On=     | Harmless; corrects preview geometry          |
| Vignette control        | =Normal= | Preview only                                |

*Long exposure NR* deserves explaining. When on, after any exposure of about one
second or longer the camera takes *a second exposure of equal length with the
shutter closed* and subtracts it to remove hot pixels. A 30-second exposure
becomes a 60-second wait during which the camera is unusable.

Leave it off. Turn it on deliberately for a single critical long exposure, or
when a test frame shows bright coloured speckles. Always leave it off for
time-lapse, where it would punch gaps in your sequence.

**** Picture Control

*Photo Shooting menu → Set Picture Control → Neutral.*

Preview-only again — but the preview is what you judge exposure from, and
=Standard= adds contrast that makes the histogram look worse than your file
really is. =Neutral= gives a flatter, more honest preview.

One genuinely useful trick: set Picture Control to =Monochrome= on a day you are
working on light and shadow. The optical viewfinder is unaffected, but the rear
screen preview goes black and white, which makes judging luminance far easier
without colour distracting you. Your raw file stays in full colour and you can
undo the decision at any point, forever.

*** Step 4 — Auto ISO, the setting that makes this camera easy

This is the most important setting in the chapter, and the one most beginners
never find.

*Photo Shooting menu → ISO sensitivity settings:*

| Option                       | Set to |
|------------------------------+--------|
| ISO sensitivity              | =100=  |
| Auto ISO sensitivity control | =On=   |
| Maximum sensitivity          | =6400= |
| Minimum shutter speed        | =Auto= |

**** What this actually does

With Auto ISO on, you choose aperture and shutter speed, and the camera chooses
ISO to make the exposure correct. It floats ISO up in dim light and back down to
100 in bright light, without you thinking about it.

This means you can shoot in *Manual mode* — full control of both aperture and
shutter — while the camera handles the one variable you care least about. That is
the best way to work with this camera, and it is how most of Part 6's recipes are
written.

**** The hidden setting nobody tells you about

Highlight *Minimum shutter speed*, choose =Auto=, and then *press the right arrow
(▶) again.*

A slider appears, running from *Slower* to *Faster*.

With =Auto=, the camera picks a minimum shutter speed based on your focal
length — roughly the old 1/focal-length rule, recomputed live as you zoom. That
is already clever. The slider biases it: nudge it toward *Faster* and the camera
holds a quicker shutter than it otherwise would, at the cost of higher ISO.

*Set it two steps toward Faster.*

On a 24-megapixel sensor the classic 1/focal-length rule is too optimistic. It
dates from film, where nobody inspected grain at 100%. Two steps faster buys you
noticeably more sharp frames, and the extra noise is trivial on this sensor.

This single setting will improve your keeper rate more than anything else in this
chapter.

**** Why 6400 and not lower

Because a sharp ISO 6400 photograph beats a blurred ISO 800 one every time, and
the D7200 at 6400 is genuinely good.

You are coming from a camera where high ISO meant ruin. It does not mean that any
more. If 6400 feels frightening, set it there anyway and go look at the files.
The fear is FZ200 muscle memory and it will cost you photographs until you
unlearn it.

*** Step 5 — Back-button focus

The second change that will genuinely improve your photography. It feels strange
for about a week.

**** The problem it solves

By default, half-pressing the shutter does two things at once: it focuses and it
meters. So you cannot recompose without refocusing, cannot lock focus and wait
for a moment to happen, and have to keep switching focus modes between still and
moving subjects.

Back-button focus separates them. The shutter button only takes the picture. A
button under your right thumb does the focusing.

**** The setup — two settings

1. *Custom Settings → a Autofocus → a4 AF activation → =AF-ON only=*

   The shutter button no longer focuses at all.

2. *Custom Settings → f Controls → f4 Assign AE-L/AF-L button → Press → =AF-ON=*

   The =AE-L/AF-L= button, right where your thumb sits, now focuses.

**** Why it is worth the awkward week

Leave the camera in =AF-C= permanently and you get both behaviours from one
button:

- *Tap and release* the back button → focus locks. Behaves like single-shot AF.
  Recompose freely; focus will not shift.
- *Hold* the back button → focus tracks continuously. Behaves like continuous AF.
  Perfect for a child running toward you.

One button, both modes, decided by your thumb in the moment rather than by a menu
before the moment. You will stop thinking about it within a fortnight and then
wonder how you managed before.

Give it two weeks before judging. Everyone fumbles at first.

*** Step 6 — The two custom banks: U1 and U2

The mode dial has =U1= and =U2= positions. These store *complete camera
configurations* — mode, aperture, shutter, ISO settings, focus mode, metering,
drive mode and most custom settings — and recall them instantly.

Think of them as two other cameras you can switch to in half a second.

**** Saving one

Configure the camera exactly as you want it, then:

*Setup menu → Save user settings → Save to U1 → Save settings.*

**** The two banks to start with

*U1 — People and action.* For anything alive and moving.

| Setting         | Value                |
|-----------------+----------------------|
| Mode            | =M=                  |
| Shutter         | 1/250 s              |
| Aperture        | f/5.6                |
| ISO             | Auto, max 6400       |
| Focus mode      | =AF-C=               |
| AF-area mode    | Dynamic-area 9-point |
| Metering        | Matrix               |
| Drive           | =CL= (continuous low) |
| Picture Control | Neutral              |

*U2 — Tripod work.* Landscape, night, close-up. Deliberate and slow.

| Setting        | Value                            |
|----------------+----------------------------------|
| Mode           | =A=                              |
| Aperture       | f/8                              |
| ISO            | 100, *Auto ISO off*              |
| Focus mode     | =AF-S= single point, or manual    |
| Metering       | Matrix                           |
| Drive          | Self-timer, 2 s                   |
| Exposure delay | =d4= set to 1 s                   |
| VR             | *Off* — switch on the lens barrel  |

VR is a *physical switch on the lens*, so it is not stored in the bank. Turning
it off on a tripod is a habit you have to build yourself.

#+begin_quote

** Part 3 — Exposure

Exposure is the single most fundamental thing in photography, and it is not
complicated. It is one idea and three controls.

*The idea:* a photograph is made by letting a measured amount of light fall on
the sensor. Too little and the picture is dark and noisy. Too much and the bright
parts turn to featureless white. "Exposure" is the amount of light you let in.

*The three controls:* how wide the hole is (*aperture*), how long it stays open
(*shutter speed*), and how strongly the signal is amplified afterwards (*ISO*).

Here is the part that matters, and the reason this takes a whole chapter rather
than a paragraph:

#+begin_quote
*Any of the three can make the picture brighter. But each one also does
something else — something visible — and those side effects are the actual
creative controls of photography. You do not choose settings to get a correct
exposure. You choose the side effects you want, and then balance the rest.*
#+end_quote

Learn the side effects and you have learned exposure.

*** Stops: the unit that makes it all one system

A *stop* is a doubling or halving of light. That is the whole definition.

One stop brighter = twice the light. One stop darker = half the light. Because
all three controls are measured in stops, they are interchangeable: if you take
one stop away with the aperture, you can put one stop back with the shutter, and
the brightness is unchanged.

That interchangeability is the entire game.

| Control  | One stop *more* light | One stop *less* light |
|----------+-----------------------+-----------------------|
| Shutter  | Twice as long         | Half as long          |
| ISO      | Double the number     | Halve the number      |
| Aperture | See below — it is odd  | See below              |

Shutter and ISO are easy: 1/125 → 1/60 is one stop. ISO 400 → 800 is one stop.

Aperture numbers look arbitrary because they are ratios, not multiples. The full
stop sequence is worth memorising, because it turns up constantly:

: f/1.4   f/2   f/2.8   f/4   f/5.6   f/8   f/11   f/16   f/22

Each step right is one stop *less* light. Your lens covers roughly f/3.5 to f/22
at the wide end.

Your camera moves in *thirds* of a stop by default, so you will see intermediate
values like f/6.3 and f/7.1. Those are just fine-tuning between the full stops.

*** Aperture — and what it costs you

The aperture is an adjustable hole inside the lens. Wide open lets in a lot of
light; stopped down lets in little.

*The side effect is depth of field:* how much of the scene, front to back, is
acceptably sharp.

| Aperture      | Light | Depth of field | Typical use                          |
|---------------+-------+----------------+--------------------------------------|
| f/3.5 – f/5.6 | Most  | Shallow        | Subject isolation, low light, people |
| f/8 – f/11    | Some  | Deep           | Landscapes, groups, general sharpness |
| f/16 – f/22   | Least | Deepest        | Only when you truly need front-to-back |

**** Your lens has a variable maximum aperture

This is important and it will catch you out. The "f/3.5–5.6" in your lens's name
means its widest available aperture *changes as you zoom*:

| Focal length | Widest aperture available |
|--------------+---------------------------|
| 18 mm        | f/3.5                     |
| 24 mm        | f/4                       |
| 35 mm        | f/4.5                     |
| 50 mm        | f/5                       |
| 70 mm        | f/5.3                     |
| 105–140 mm   | f/5.6                     |

#+begin_quote
*Tip —* Nikon publishes only the two end points, f/3.5 and f/5.6; the intermediate
figures above are close approximations of where the steps fall. You do not have to
take my word for any of it — set the camera to =A= mode, turn the aperture to its
widest, and zoom slowly. The number in the viewfinder changes as you go, and that
is the real answer for your particular lens.
#+end_quote

So zooming from 18mm to 140mm costs you about *1⅓ stops of light* before you
have changed a single setting. In dim conditions the camera will silently raise
ISO to compensate as you zoom in. That is not a fault; it is what a variable
aperture zoom does. It is also exactly what you gave up when you left the
FZ200's constant f/2.8 behind.

**** Two warnings about the extremes

*Do not live at f/22.* Very small apertures cause *diffraction* — light bending
around the aperture blades, softening the entire image. On a 24-megapixel DX
sensor this becomes visible past about f/11 and is obvious by f/16. Stopping
down to f/22 gets you more depth of field and a mushier picture overall. It is
usually a bad trade.

*Your lens is sharpest around f/8.* Nearly all lenses are sharpest two or three
stops down from wide open. For your 18–140mm, f/8 is the sweet spot across the
range. When sharpness is the priority and depth of field is not fighting you,
f/8 is the default answer.

*** Shutter speed — and what it costs you

How long the shutter stays open. Your camera runs from *30 seconds to 1/8000
second*, plus two long-exposure modes.

*The side effect is motion:* whether moving things are frozen or blurred, and
whether your own handshake shows.

| Shutter        | Effect                                              |
|----------------+-----------------------------------------------------|
| 1/2000 & faster | Freezes almost anything — birds, splashing water     |
| 1/500 – 1/1000 | Freezes running people, dogs, cars at street speed   |
| 1/250          | Freezes walking people, most casual movement         |
| 1/125          | Safe general handheld speed at moderate focal lengths |
| 1/60 – 1/30    | Handheld only with care; walking people will blur     |
| 1/15 – 1/2     | Tripod. Deliberate blur — water, crowds               |
| 1 s and longer | Tripod only. Light trails, night, smooth water        |

**** Two kinds of blur, and they have different cures

This distinction confuses everyone at first, so be precise about it:

- *Subject blur* — the thing you photographed moved. Cured only by a faster
  shutter speed.
- *Camera shake* — you moved. Cured by a faster shutter speed, by VR, by
  bracing, or by a tripod.

VR helps with the second and does nothing at all for the first. If your photo of
a running child is blurred, image stabilisation will not save you. Only a faster
shutter will.

**** How slow can you hand-hold?

The traditional rule is *1 / focal length*, using the equivalent focal length. At
140 mm (210 mm equivalent) that means about 1/200 s.

Nikon rates your lens's VR at *4.0 stops* (the CIPA standard figure, measured at
the telephoto end). Treat that as a laboratory best case. In real use, with real
hands, expect *two to three stops* — so around 1/30 s at full zoom on a good day,
with a subject that is holding still.

Because you set Auto ISO's minimum shutter speed to =Auto= biased two steps
faster in Part 2, the camera already handles this for you. That is why that
setting mattered.

**** Bulb and Time

Turn the shutter speed past 30 seconds in =M= mode and you reach two more
settings:

- *Bulb* — the shutter stays open as long as the release is held down. Needs your
  corded remote (holding the camera's own button would shake it).
- *Time* — press once to open, press again to close. *No need to hold anything.*

Almost every guide mentions Bulb and forgets Time exists. Time is usually the
better of the two: nothing to hold, nothing to keep still, no cramping thumb on a
four-minute exposure.

*** ISO — and what it costs you

ISO is how strongly the sensor's signal is amplified. Your camera runs from *100
to 25600*, and all of it is native — no trickery, no interpolation.

*The side effect is noise:* speckle and grain, worst in the shadows.

There are two extended settings above 25600, labelled *Hi 1* (≈51200) and *Hi 2*
(≈102400). Know one thing about them: **they are black-and-white JPEG only.** You
cannot shoot raw at those settings. They exist for emergencies and you will
almost certainly never use them.

**** The ISO advice that actually matters for you

| ISO           | Verdict on the D7200                          |
|---------------+-----------------------------------------------|
| 100 – 400     | Pristine. No thought required                 |
| 800 – 1600    | Excellent. Genuinely no visible penalty        |
| 3200          | Very good. Use without hesitation              |
| 6400          | Good. Slight grain, cleans up well in darktable |
| 12800         | Usable when you need it. Visible noise         |
| 25600         | Emergency. Small prints and web only           |

Coming from the FZ200, your instinct is to keep ISO as low as possible. *Break
that instinct.* On the FZ200 that reflex was correct; on the D7200 it costs you
photographs, because you will choose a shutter speed too slow to be sharp in
order to protect an ISO number that did not need protecting.

The correct order of priorities is:

1. Get the shutter speed you need for the subject.
2. Get the aperture you need for the depth of field.
3. Let ISO be whatever it has to be.

**** The D7200 is close to ISO-invariant, which is a gift

This camera has an unusual and useful property: brightening a dark file in
editing produces *almost the same result* as having raised the ISO when you shot
it.

The practical consequence: if you accidentally underexpose by a stop or two at
ISO 1600, you can lift it in darktable and lose remarkably little. It makes the
camera forgiving of mistakes in exactly the situations where mistakes are most
likely.

This is not licence to underexpose on purpose. More light is still better light.
But it does mean an error is rarely fatal.

*** The four modes, and the honest recommendation

The mode dial has =P=, =S=, =A=, =M= (plus =Auto=, scene modes, and your two
custom banks).

| Mode | You set                  | Camera sets   | Good for                    |
|------+--------------------------+---------------+-----------------------------|
| =P=  | Nothing (can shift)      | Both          | Handing the camera to someone |
| =S=  | Shutter speed            | Aperture      | When motion is the priority |
| =A=  | Aperture                 | Shutter speed | When depth of field is the priority |
| =M=  | Both                     | Nothing       | When you want consistency    |

**** What most people should use most of the time: A

*Aperture Priority* is the default working mode for most photography. You pick
the aperture — which is the creative decision most of the time — and the camera
handles the shutter. Add exposure compensation when you disagree with it.

Start here. It is not a beginner's crutch; a great many professionals shoot this
way permanently.

**** The mode this camera is best at: M with Auto ISO

Here is the combination that makes the D7200 genuinely pleasant, and the reason
Part 2 set up Auto ISO so carefully:

*Set Manual mode. Choose your shutter speed. Choose your aperture. Let Auto ISO
handle the exposure.*

You have now locked the two things with visible side effects — motion and depth
of field — and handed the camera the one variable whose side effect you care
least about. The exposure still comes out correct, because ISO floats.

This is not the same as old-fashioned manual shooting, where you had to meter
everything yourself. It is better than =A= mode, because in =A= mode the camera
can hand you a shutter speed too slow for your subject without warning you.

*And exposure compensation still works.* This surprises people. In =M= with Auto
ISO, the exposure compensation dial biases the ISO the camera chooses. So you
keep full manual control of shutter and aperture, and still have a brightness
dial. It is the best of every world.

Your =U1= bank is set up exactly this way.

**** When to actually use full manual, with ISO fixed too

Only when you want *consistency* rather than correctness:

- Studio or flash work, where the light does not change
- Panoramas, where every frame must match exactly
- Time-lapse, where drifting exposure causes flicker
- Astrophotography
- Scenes where the meter is being fooled and you would rather set it once

*** Metering: how the camera guesses, and when it guesses wrong

The camera measures the light using a 2016-pixel RGB sensor and gives you an
exposure suggestion. You choose *how* it measures by holding the metering button
and turning the main command dial.

| Mode                | What it measures                          | Use when                     |
|---------------------+-------------------------------------------+------------------------------|
| *Matrix*            | The whole frame, compared against a database | Almost always — your default |
| *Center-weighted*   | The whole frame, ~75% weight to an 8mm circle | Backlit subject filling the centre |
| *Spot*              | A 3.5mm circle (2.5% of frame) at the active AF point | One small thing must be right |

Two details worth knowing:

- *Spot metering follows your focus point.* Move the AF point and the spot moves
  with it. That makes it far more useful than a fixed centre spot — you can meter
  a face wherever it sits in the frame.
- *The centre-weighted circle is adjustable* via Custom Setting =b4= (6, 8, 10 or
  13 mm). Leave it at 8 mm.

The D7200 has *three* metering modes. It does not have highlight-weighted
metering — that arrived on the D7500 and the higher-end bodies. If you read about
it, it is not on your camera.

**** Why the meter gets snow wrong

This is the one thing you must understand about metering, and it explains most
"wrong" exposures beginners get.

*The meter assumes everything it looks at averages to a middle grey.* That is a
reasonable guess for an average scene, and it is badly wrong for scenes that are
mostly very light or very dark.

- Photograph a *snowy field*. The meter sees an overwhelmingly bright scene,
  concludes you must be overexposing, and darkens it. Your snow comes out grey.
- Photograph a *black cat on a dark sofa*. The meter sees a dark scene, decides
  you are underexposing, and brightens it. Your black cat comes out grey.

The meter is not broken. It is doing exactly what it was designed to do, and the
design has this known blind spot. Which is what exposure compensation is for.

*** Exposure compensation

Press the =+/−= button next to the shutter release and turn the main command
dial. Range is *−5 to +5 EV* in third-stop steps.

Positive makes the picture brighter. Negative makes it darker.

| Scene                            | Try         |
|----------------------------------+-------------|
| Snow, white sand, bright beach   | +1 to +1.7  |
| White wall, high-key subject     | +0.7 to +1.3 |
| Backlit person, no flash         | +1 to +1.7  |
| Black animal, dark clothing      | −0.7 to −1.3 |
| Sunset, to keep colours saturated | −0.3 to −0.7 |
| Stage lighting, spotlit subject  | −1 to −2    |

The rule underneath the table: *if the scene is mostly bright, add exposure. If
the scene is mostly dark, subtract it.* This feels backwards for about a month
and then becomes automatic.

#+begin_quote
*Watch out —* Exposure compensation is *sticky*. It stays where you left it when
you turn the camera off. Everyone eventually shoots an entire afternoon at +1.7
because of a snow
scene the previous weekend. Check the display at the start of every session. The
indicator is easy to miss.
#+end_quote

There is also *Custom Setting =b3= Easy exposure compensation*, which lets you
set compensation by turning a dial with no button press. Convenient once you are
fluent, and an excellent way to change it accidentally before then. Leave it off
for now.

*** Reading the result: the histogram and the blinkies

Because your viewfinder is optical, it cannot show you whether the exposure
worked. These two tools replace the reassurance the FZ200's EVF gave you for
free. Learn to trust them and you have solved the transition.

**** Turning them on

*Playback menu → Playback display options*, and tick:

- *Highlights* (the flashing overexposure warning)
- *RGB histogram*

Then in playback, press *up* or *down* on the multi-selector to cycle through the
display modes until you find them.

**** The histogram

A graph of brightness. Pure black at the left edge, pure white at the right,
everything else in between. Height shows how many pixels sit at that brightness.

There is *no correct shape*. A photo of a black cat should be piled left. A snow
scene should be piled right. Both are correct. What you are looking for is not a
shape but a specific failure:

*A hard spike jammed against either edge means information has been destroyed.*
Against the right edge, highlights have clipped to pure white and there is
nothing there to recover. Against the left, shadows have crushed to pure black.

Highlight clipping is the one that matters. A blown sky is gone permanently; no
software recovers it. Crushed shadows can usually be lifted, especially on this
camera.

*The RGB histogram is the one to use.* It shows red, green and blue separately.
A single channel can clip while the overall brightness histogram looks perfectly
healthy — which happens constantly with saturated reds and with blue skies. The
plain brightness histogram will not warn you.

**** The blinkies

With *Highlights* enabled, any area that has clipped to pure white flashes black
and white in playback. This is faster to read than the histogram and you will use
it more.

The judgement is not "make the blinking stop." It is *"is anything blinking that
I care about?"*

- A specular highlight on chrome, or the sun itself: let it blink. It is supposed
  to be white.
- A bride's dress, a cloud with structure, a face: that is a problem. Reduce
  exposure and reshoot.

Remember what Part 2 said: these warnings are computed from the JPEG preview, so
your raw file holds a little more highlight room than the blinkies suggest —
roughly a third to two-thirds of a stop. When something important is just barely
blinking, you have probably got away with it.

**** Expose to the right, honestly

You will encounter the advice "expose to the right" (ETTR): deliberately make the
image as bright as possible without clipping, because more light means a cleaner
file, then darken it in editing.

*It is technically correct and I do not recommend it as a habit for you.*

The gain is real but small on a camera this good and this close to ISO-invariant.
The cost is that you must check the histogram after every frame and you will
sometimes clip a highlight you cannot recover. The trade is bad for a beginner.

What to do instead: *expose so that nothing important clips, and do not
deliberately underexpose out of fear.* That gets you most of the benefit with
none of the risk. Save real ETTR for a tripod landscape where you have time to
check.

** Part 4 — Focus

Focus is where the D7200 is enormously more capable than the FZ200, and
enormously more confusing. It is the part of this camera most people never
really learn, and it is worth the effort, because *missed focus is the single
most common reason a photograph fails.*

An out-of-focus photo cannot be fixed. Not in darktable, not anywhere. Exposure
is recoverable, colour is recoverable, even a moderate amount of camera shake can
be disguised. Focus cannot.

*** Why this is harder than it was on your old camera

Two reasons, and they are both consequences of the upgrade.

*Your depth of field is now much thinner.* On the FZ200's tiny sensor, almost
everything was in focus whether you wanted it or not — focus errors were
invisible because the depth of field forgave them. At 140 mm f/5.6 with a person
three metres away, your sharp zone is a few centimetres deep. Focus on the nose
and the eyes are soft.

*You now have 51 focus points and seven ways to use them.* The FZ200 essentially
decided for you. The D7200 hands you the controls, which is better and also means
you can now get it wrong in ways that were previously impossible.

All 51 points are sensitive down to *−3 EV* — roughly the light of a candlelit
room. This camera focuses in the dark considerably better than you will expect,
and better than most cameras of its generation.

*** The two settings that are always confused

This is the thing to get straight before anything else. There are *two separate
settings*, they do different jobs, and almost every beginner conflates them.

| Setting          | Question it answers                              |
|------------------+--------------------------------------------------|
| *Focus mode*     | *When* does the camera focus? Once, or continuously? |
| *AF-area mode*   | *Where* does it look? One point, a cluster, or everywhere? |

You set both with the same physical control, which is why they get muddled:

*Hold down the AF-mode button* — the small button in the centre of the focus
selector switch on the front of the camera, beside the lens mount — and then:

- Turn the *main* command dial (rear) → changes *focus mode*
- Turn the *sub*-command dial (front) → changes *AF-area mode*

Both appear in the viewfinder and on the top LCD while you hold the button.
Practise this until your fingers do it without your eyes leaving the viewfinder.

That same switch has an *M* position. That is the master switch for manual focus;
your lens has its own *A/M* switch too, and both need to be set for autofocus to
work.

*** Focus mode: when the camera focuses

**** AF-S — Single servo

Focus locks when you focus, and stays locked. If the subject moves afterwards,
the camera does not follow.

For: landscapes, still life, buildings, flowers, anything that stays put.

**** AF-C — Continuous servo

The camera keeps refocusing for as long as you hold the focus button. It tracks
subjects moving toward or away from you.

For: children, animals, anything moving.

**** AF-A — Auto servo

The camera guesses which of the above you wanted based on whether it detects
movement.

*Do not use this.* It is the default and it is a trap. When it guesses wrong you
get an unpredictable failure with no obvious cause, which is the worst kind of
failure to learn from. Choose deliberately.

**** With back-button focus, this decision mostly disappears

Recall from Part 2: with back-button focus set up, leave the camera in *AF-C
permanently*.

- *Tap and release* the back button → focus locks and stays locked. You have
  AF-S behaviour.
- *Hold* the back button → focus tracks continuously. You have AF-C behaviour.

One physical setting, both behaviours, chosen by your thumb in the moment. This
is the main reason back-button focus is worth the awkward first fortnight.

*** AF-area mode: where the camera looks

Seven selectable settings, grouped into five named modes. Which are available
depends on the focus mode.

**** Single-point AF

You choose exactly one of the 51 points. The camera focuses on precisely that
and nothing else.

*Available in both AF-S and AF-C.* This is your default for anything that is not
moving fast. Maximum precision, maximum control, zero surprises. Most of your
photography should happen here.

**** Dynamic-area AF — 9, 21 or 51 points

*AF-C only.* You still choose the primary point, but if the subject briefly
slips off it, surrounding points help out.

| Points | Use for                                                    |
|--------+------------------------------------------------------------|
| 9      | Predictable movement — a person walking, a car on a road    |
| 21     | Less predictable — a dog running, a larger bird             |
| 51     | Erratic movement you cannot track precisely                 |

Nine is the right default. More points is not better: with 51, the camera has
more freedom to grab a high-contrast background instead of your subject.

**** 3D-tracking

*AF-C only.* Put the point on your subject, hold focus, and the camera follows it
across the frame using colour and distance information, handing off between
points as it goes.

Brilliant when the subject is *visually distinct* from its background — a red
coat against grass, a child in a bright jumper. Poor when subject and background
are similar in colour or the background is busy. It is the mode that feels like
magic when it works and is baffling when it does not.

**** Group-area AF

*Available in both AF-S and AF-C.* Uses a small cluster of five points as one
unit, and tends to prefer the *closest* subject within that cluster.

Genuinely useful and underrated. Because it favours the nearest thing, it is very
good at grabbing a face rather than the wall behind it. Good for portraits where
the subject shifts slightly, and for birds against a cluttered background.

**** Auto-area AF

The camera picks the subject entirely on its own.

Use it when handing the camera to someone who does not know how to use it.
Otherwise, do not — you have not gone to the trouble of learning this system in
order to let it guess.

**** Quick reference

| Subject                         | Focus mode | AF-area mode         |
|---------------------------------+------------+----------------------|
| Landscape on a tripod           | =AF-S=     | Single-point         |
| Portrait, person still          | =AF-S=     | Single-point on the near eye |
| Portrait, person shifting about | =AF-C=     | Group-area           |
| Child running toward you        | =AF-C=     | Dynamic-area 9 or 21 |
| Child running across the frame  | =AF-C=     | 3D-tracking          |
| Zoo animal, mostly still        | =AF-C=     | Single-point         |
| Bird in flight                  | =AF-C=     | Dynamic-area 21      |
| Flowers, close up               | =AF-S=     | Single-point, or manual |
| Street, unpredictable           | =AF-C=     | Single-point or Group-area |

*** Moving the focus point

Use the multi-selector — the round rocker on the back. Press the centre button to
snap back to the middle point.

**** The lock switch that will confuse you at least once

Below and right of the multi-selector is a small switch with a dot and an *L*.
Set to *L*, the focus point is locked and *will not move*.

Every D7200 owner has spent a frustrated minute jabbing at the multi-selector
before realising. Now you know. Keep it on the dot.

**** Two settings that make point selection faster

*Custom Setting =a7= Number of focus points → =AF11=.*

This reduces the *selectable* points from 51 to 11 — the camera still uses all 51
for tracking, you just cycle through fewer when moving the point manually. Getting
across the frame takes a couple of clicks instead of a dozen. For most people
this is a straight improvement. Try it; go back to 51 if you miss the precision.

*Custom Setting =a8= Store points by orientation → =Focus point=.*

The camera remembers a *separate* focus point for horizontal and for vertical
shooting. Rotate the camera and your point is where you left it last time you
were in that orientation. If you shoot portraits, this is excellent.

**** Focus point illumination

*Custom Setting =a5= Focus point illumination.* Controls whether the active point
lights up red in the viewfinder. Leave on =Auto=.

#+begin_quote
*Watch out —* This is =a5=, not =a3=. On the D7200, =a3= is Focus tracking with
lock-on, a completely different setting discussed below. Guides that say =a3= for
focus point illumination are describing another camera.
#+end_quote

*** Focus priority: the setting that decides whether the shutter fires

Two settings, and they control something important: whether the camera will take
a picture that is not in focus.

**** a1 — AF-C priority selection

*Custom Settings → a Autofocus → a1.*

- *Release* — the shutter fires the instant you press it, focused or not.
- *Focus* — the shutter will not fire until focus is confirmed.

For action, set *Release*. You would rather have a slightly soft frame of the
decisive moment than a perfectly sharp frame of the moment after it. In a burst,
the first frame is sometimes soft and frames two and three catch up.

**** a2 — AF-S priority selection

- *Focus* — will not fire unless focused. **Leave it here.**

In AF-S you are photographing something that is not going anywhere. There is no
reason to accept an out-of-focus frame. If the shutter refuses to fire, that is
the camera correctly telling you it has not achieved focus — usually because you
are inside the minimum focus distance or the subject has no contrast.

**** a3 — Focus tracking with lock-on

*Custom Settings → a Autofocus → a3.* Runs from *1 (Short)* to *5 (Long)*, plus
Off.

This controls how long the camera *ignores a sudden change in distance* before
refocusing. A tree passes between you and a running dog: do you want the camera
to leap to the tree, or hold on the dog?

| Setting     | Behaviour                    | Use for                              |
|-------------+------------------------------+--------------------------------------|
| 4–5 (Long)  | Stubbornly holds the subject | Subjects behind clutter — fences, branches, crowds |
| 3 (Normal)  | Balanced                     | Default; leave it here                |
| 1–2 (Short) | Refocuses quickly            | Deliberately switching between subjects |

*For the zoo, set this to 4 or 5.* You will constantly be shooting through
fences and past foreground clutter, and you want the camera to stay on the
animal rather than snapping to the wire. This is one of the most useful settings
on the camera for your specific situation and almost nobody adjusts it.

#+begin_quote
*Watch out —* This setting is partly ignored in some AF-area modes. Nikon's manual
states that *2*, *1 (Short)* and *Off* all behave as *3 (Normal)* when 3D-tracking
or Auto-area AF is selected. Only the *4* and *5* settings do anything different
in those modes. If you want lock-on behaviour you can actually control, use
Single-point, Dynamic-area or Group-area.
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
*Watch out —* Some guides call =a3= "Blocked shot AF response." That is Nikon's
name for the equivalent setting on the D5 and D500. On your camera it is called
*Focus tracking with lock-on*. Same idea, different label.
#+end_quote

*** The AF-assist illuminator

In dim light the camera fires a small orange lamp to help autofocus. It is fussy
about when it will do so — *both* of these must be true:

1. Focus mode is =AF-S=
2. AF-area mode is Auto-area AF, *or* it is another mode and the *centre* focus
   point is selected

Its range is *0.5 to 3.0 m*. Outside that, it does nothing. If you have wondered
why the lamp sometimes refuses to fire, it is almost always because you have
moved the focus point off centre.

To turn it off — for a concert, a ceremony, or anywhere it would be rude:
*Custom Settings → a Autofocus → a9 Built-in AF-assist illuminator → Off.*

#+begin_quote
*Watch out —* That is =a9=. Guides citing =a3= for this are, again, describing a
different camera.
#+end_quote

*** When autofocus cannot work

Autofocus needs *contrast* — an edge, a difference in brightness. It will fail,
reliably and predictably, on:

- Blank surfaces: clear sky, a white wall, a smooth painted door
- Very low light
- Subjects behind glass or a fence, where it focuses on the wrong plane
- Repeating patterns like brickwork, where it locks on the wrong repeat
- Strongly backlit subjects
- Anything closer than 0.45 m

None of these are faults. They are the physics of the system. Recognise them and
switch to manual rather than fighting the camera.

*** Manual focus, properly

Set the lens switch to *M* and the camera's focus-mode switch to *M*, then turn
the focus ring.

**** Method 1 — the electronic rangefinder

Even in manual focus, the camera still knows what is sharp. Look in the bottom
left of the viewfinder:

| Indicator      | Meaning                                        |
|----------------+------------------------------------------------|
| ● steady       | In focus at the selected point                  |
| ◀ arrow        | Focused *in front of* the subject — turn one way |
| ▶ arrow        | Focused *behind* the subject — turn the other    |
| ◀▶ both        | Camera cannot tell                              |

Free, fast, works in the viewfinder, and hardly anyone knows it is there.

**** Method 2 — live view zoom, the precision tool

For anything critical on a tripod, this is the most accurate focusing method the
camera has. More accurate than autofocus.

1. Press =Lv= to enter live view.
2. Move the focus box onto your subject with the multi-selector.
3. Press the magnify button (=+=) repeatedly to zoom in, up to *about 19×*.
4. Turn the focus ring until the detail snaps sharp.
5. Shoot with the 2-second self-timer or your corded remote.

At 19× you are examining actual pixels. Nothing else on this camera is that
precise.

#+begin_quote
*Watch out —* The maximum live view magnification on the D7200 is *about 19×*,
per Nikon's manual. You will see "23×" quoted in a great many places — that is the
D750 and D810 figure, not yours.
#+end_quote

The D7200 does *not* have focus peaking. If you have seen that on a mirrorless
camera, it is not available here. Live view zoom is the substitute and it is
genuinely better for static precision, just slower.

*** Depth of field: the practical version

Three things control how much of your scene is sharp:

1. *Aperture.* Wider = shallower. This is the one you usually reach for.
2. *Focus distance.* The closer you focus, the shallower it gets — dramatically.
   This is why close-ups are so hard.
3. *Focal length.* Longer = shallower-looking, mostly because it magnifies the
   background blur.

And one rule that explains more than any of them:

#+begin_quote
*Depth of field extends roughly twice as far behind your focus point as in front
of it.* When you need a group of people sharp, focus on the front row, not the
middle.
#+end_quote

**** Hyperfocal focusing: the landscape trick

When you want everything from close foreground to distant horizon sharp, do not
focus on infinity — you waste all the depth of field that extends behind it.

Focus instead at the *hyperfocal distance*: the closest point you can focus at
while the horizon still comes out sharp. Everything from half that distance to
infinity is then acceptably sharp.

Verified figures for your lens on DX:

| Focal length | Aperture | Focus at    | Sharp from  |
|--------------+----------+-------------+-------------|
| 18 mm        | f/8      | 2.0 m (7 ft)  | 1.0 m to ∞  |
| 18 mm        | f/11     | 1.5 m (5 ft)  | 0.75 m to ∞ |
| 24 mm        | f/8      | 3.6 m (12 ft) | 1.8 m to ∞  |
| 35 mm        | f/8      | 7.7 m (25 ft) | 3.8 m to ∞  |
| 50 mm        | f/8      | 15.7 m (51 ft) | 7.8 m to ∞ |
| 70 mm        | f/8      | 30.7 m (101 ft) | 15 m to ∞ |

The practical version, when you have no table: *at 18mm f/8, focus about two
metres in.* That covers most wide landscapes. Verify with live view zoom — check
the nearest thing you care about, then the horizon.

The old advice to "focus one third into the scene" is a rough approximation of
this. It is close enough at wide angles and increasingly wrong as you zoom in.

*** AF Fine-Tune — probably ignore this

*Setup menu → AF fine-tune* lets you correct a lens that consistently focuses
slightly in front of (front-focus) or behind (back-focus) the target.

Two honest points:

1. *You almost certainly do not need it.* Most bodies and lenses are fine. Most
   perceived focus errors are actually subject movement, shallow depth of field,
   or focusing on the wrong thing.
2. *Do not touch it until you have proven a problem exists*, with the camera on a
   tripod, a flat target at 45°, in good light, over many frames. A miscalibrated
   fine-tune is worse than none.

If manual focus in live view is consistently sharper than autofocus on static
subjects at the same settings, you may have a real issue. Otherwise, leave it
alone.

** Part 5 — Light, colour and composition

Everything so far has been about operating the camera. This part is about the
photograph, and it is the part that actually separates good pictures from
correctly exposed ones.

A technically perfect photograph of nothing interesting, in bad light, badly
arranged, is still a bad photograph. Nobody has ever looked at a picture and
admired the autofocus.

The good news: this part transfers completely. Nothing here depends on owning a
D7200, and none of it will be obsolete when you change camera.

*** Light

**** The thing nobody tells beginners

*You are not photographing objects. You are photographing light bouncing off
objects.*

That sounds like a slogan. It is actually a working instruction, and it changes
what you do. Beginners find a subject and then take a picture of it. Experienced
photographers find good light and then look for something to put in it.

The same tree on the same day is a different photograph at seven in the morning
and at one in the afternoon. Not slightly different — different in kind. If you
learn to see light before subject, you will improve faster than by any other
route.

**** Quality: hard light and soft light

The most useful single distinction in photography.

*Hard light* comes from a small source relative to the subject — the bare sun, a
naked bulb, an unmodified flash. It produces:

- Sharp-edged, dark shadows
- High contrast
- Strong texture — every wrinkle, crack and flake is emphasised

*Soft light* comes from a large source — an overcast sky, a big window, light
bounced off a wall. It produces:

- Soft-edged, open shadows
- Lower contrast
- Smoothed texture, flattering to skin

Neither is better. Hard light is brutal on faces and magnificent on rusted metal,
peeling paint and dramatic landscapes. Soft light flatters people and can make
landscapes look flat and dull.

The rule that governs it: *the larger the light source appears from where the
subject is standing, the softer the light.* The sun is enormous and very far
away, so it appears small, so it is hard. Put a cloud in front of it and the
cloud becomes the source — huge, and therefore soft. This is also why a window
softens as your subject steps closer to it.

**** Direction: where it comes from

| Direction  | What it does                                                   |
|------------+----------------------------------------------------------------|
| *Front*    | Flattens. Even, safe, dull. Hides texture and shape             |
| *Side*     | Sculpts. Reveals texture and three-dimensionality. The workhorse |
| *Back*     | Separates. Rim-lights edges, creates glow, silhouettes          |
| *Top*      | Midday sun. Dark eye sockets, shadow under the nose. Avoid on faces |
| *Low*      | Sunrise and sunset. Long shadows, warm, dramatic                |

If you take one thing from this section: *side light is where the interest is.*
Front light is what beginners default to because it is what the camera meters
easiest, and it is the least interesting choice available.

**** The times of day, honestly

*Golden hour* — roughly the hour after sunrise and before sunset. The sun is low,
so its light travels through more atmosphere, which scatters blue and leaves
warm orange. Low angle means long shadows and side light for free. It is
genuinely as good as its reputation.

*Blue hour* — roughly twenty to forty minutes after sunset or before sunrise. The
sky is a deep saturated blue and artificial lights balance against it. This is
when cities look best. Most people pack up at sunset and miss it entirely. Stay
another half hour.

*Overcast* — the whole sky becomes a giant softbox. Excellent for portraits,
flowers and woodland; the light is soft and even and colours saturate nicely. Poor
for grand landscapes, because a white featureless sky is dead space. Compose to
exclude the sky and overcast days become very good days.

*Midday sun* — the light everyone tells you to avoid. It is genuinely bad for
faces: overhead light means dark eye sockets and a shadow under the nose. But it
is not useless. Harsh light makes strong graphic shapes, hard-edged shadows and
high contrast. Photograph the *shadows themselves*, look for reflected light in
shade, or go somewhere with strong architecture. Do not simply put the camera
away for six hours of every day.

*** Colour, and white balance

Light has a colour, measured in kelvin. Low numbers are warm and orange; high
numbers are cool and blue. (This is backwards from how "warm" and "cool" usually
work, and everyone finds it confusing at first.)

| Source                    | Approx. temperature |
|---------------------------+---------------------|
| Candle                    | 1900 K              |
| Household bulb / warm LED | 2700–3200 K         |
| Sunrise / sunset          | 3000–4000 K         |
| Midday sun                | 5200–5500 K         |
| Overcast                  | 6000–7000 K         |
| Open shade                | 7000–8000 K         |

White balance tells the camera what to treat as neutral. Set it to match the
light and whites come out white.

**** Since you shoot raw, this matters less than you think

White balance is *not baked into a raw file.* You can change it completely in
darktable afterwards, with no quality loss at all. Getting it wrong in camera
costs you nothing permanent.

So do not agonise over it. But do not ignore it either, for one reason: *the
preview on the back of the camera is what you judge your photograph from.* A
badly wrong white balance makes it harder to assess what you have.

**** What to actually do

The =WB= button is on the back. Hold it and turn the main command dial.

*Leave it on =AUTO= most of the time.* The D7200's auto white balance is good,
and you can override it later at no cost.

Switch off Auto deliberately in these cases:

- *Sunset and golden hour.* Auto WB sees warm light and "corrects" it, removing
  the exact thing you got up early for. Set =Direct sunlight= or =Cloudy= to keep
  the warmth.
- *Panoramas and time-lapse.* Every frame must match. Auto WB will drift between
  frames and give you a striped sky. Set a fixed value.
- *Mixed artificial light indoors.* Auto tends to wander. Pick one and stick with
  it.

For anything critical you can set an exact kelvin value: hold =WB=, turn the main
dial to =K=, then turn the sub-command dial to pick the temperature.

**** A useful creative trick

White balance is not only a correction, it is a mood control. Setting a warmer
value than the actual light makes an image feel warm and nostalgic; a cooler
value makes it feel cold and clinical. Since it costs nothing in raw, experiment
freely in darktable rather than in camera.

*** Focal length and perspective

This section corrects the single most common misunderstanding in photography, and
getting it right will visibly improve your pictures of people.

**** Zooming is not the same as walking

Most beginners treat the zoom ring as a way to make things bigger — stand still,
zoom in to get closer. That works, but it misses what the zoom ring is actually
for.

#+begin_quote
*Perspective is controlled entirely by where you stand. Focal length only decides
how much of that perspective fits in the frame.*
#+end_quote

Read that twice. It is the whole idea.

If you stand in one spot and zoom from 18 mm to 140 mm, the relationships between
near and far objects do not change at all — you are simply cropping. If you *walk
closer or further away*, those relationships change dramatically, whether you
touch the zoom or not.

So the real sequence is: *decide how you want near and far to relate, walk to the
spot that produces it, and then zoom to frame.* Not the other way round.

**** What each end of your lens does

*Wide (18–24 mm) exaggerates depth.* Close things loom enormous, distant things
shrink away. Space feels vast. Excellent for landscapes with strong foreground,
for small rooms, and for environmental pictures where the setting is part of the
story.

The catch: to fill the frame with anything at 18 mm you must be extremely close,
and that exaggeration applies to your subject too.

*Telephoto (70–140 mm) compresses depth.* Near and far appear stacked together.
Distant hills pile up in layers, backgrounds seem to sit right behind the
subject, and clutter simplifies because you are only seeing a narrow slice of the
world.

This is also what makes telephoto flattering for faces, and why background blur
looks so much stronger — you are magnifying the blur along with everything else.

**** The specific rule for photographing people

*Never photograph a face at 18–24 mm.*

To fill the frame with a head at 18 mm you have to be about half a metre away, and
at that distance the nose is significantly closer to the lens than the ears. The
nose is rendered large and the ears small. The result is a caricature, and your
subject will not know why they dislike the photo — only that they do.

*Stand at least two metres away from a face, then zoom to frame.* On your lens
that means:

| Framing                | Use            | Stand about |
|------------------------+----------------+-------------|
| Head and shoulders     | 85–140 mm      | 2.5–4 m     |
| Waist up               | 50–85 mm       | 2–3 m       |
| Full body              | 35–50 mm       | 3–5 m       |
| Person in their setting | 18–35 mm       | 1.5–3 m     |

Those distances assume you are holding the camera *vertically*, which is normal
for portraits. Shooting horizontally, you need to be roughly a third further back
for the same framing.

This one habit will improve your pictures of people more than any gear you could
buy.

*** Composition

Composition is arranging what is in the frame so a viewer's eye goes where you
want it to go. That is the entire job.

Most composition advice is presented as rules. They are not rules; they are
*observations about what tends to work*, useful as a starting point and
absolutely made to be broken once you can see why.

**** The tools worth knowing

*Rule of thirds.* Divide the frame in three, horizontally and vertically. Place
important things on the lines or their intersections. It works because dead-centre
is static and slightly off-centre creates a little tension.

Turn the grid on: *Custom Settings → d Shooting/display → d7 Viewfinder grid
display → On.* (=d7=. Not =d3=, whatever you have read.)

*Leading lines.* Roads, fences, rivers, shadows, railings — anything linear pulls
the eye along it. Point them at your subject.

*Frames within frames.* Shoot through a doorway, an arch, overhanging branches.
It adds depth and directs attention inward.

*Negative space.* Leave a lot of empty around a small subject. Emptiness is not
wasted; it gives the subject room and creates calm or isolation.

*Layers.* A distinct foreground, middle ground and background turn a flat
rectangle into a sense of depth. This is the main tool for making landscapes feel
three-dimensional.

*Symmetry.* Dead-centre and perfectly balanced. Formal, confrontational, strong.
Requires precision — near-symmetry looks like a mistake.

**** The one that matters more than all of them: watch the background

If you take a single composition lesson from this guide, take this one.

*Beginners look at their subject. The background is what ruins the photograph.*

A lamppost growing out of someone's head. A bright white patch in the corner
dragging the eye away. A dustbin behind a beautiful portrait. Your eye edited
these out while you were shooting, because you were concentrating on the subject.
The camera does not edit anything out.

Build this habit: *before you press the shutter, look deliberately at the
background and at all four edges of the frame.* It takes one second and it will
save more photographs than any other single practice.

When the background is bad, you have four fixes:

1. *Move.* One step left often solves it completely.
2. *Change height.* Crouching puts a subject against sky instead of clutter.
3. *Open the aperture and zoom in.* Blur the background into irrelevance.
4. *Move the subject*, if it is a person and they are willing.

**** Getting level, and getting low

*Level.* A tilted horizon looks like a mistake in almost every photograph.
Turn on the *virtual horizon*: press the =info= button while composing to cycle
display modes until the level indicator appears. You can also assign it to a
button. Straightening in darktable costs you resolution at the edges — better to
get it right.

*Height.* Almost everyone photographs everything from standing eye level, because
that is where their eyes already are. It is the most boring available viewpoint
and it is why most snapshots look like snapshots.

Crouch. Kneel. Put the camera on the ground. Photograph a child from *their*
height rather than looming over them — the difference is enormous and costs
nothing. Photograph a flower from beside it rather than above it.

Changing your height is the cheapest, fastest improvement available to you, and
the fixed rear screen on the D7200 makes it slightly awkward, which is precisely
why most people skip it.

framing them from wherever you happen to be standing.

Repeat another day at 140 mm. Entirely different world, same lesson.

** Part 6 — The subjects you actually shoot

Six chapters: one for each subject you told me you photograph, plus one on motion,

*** Chapter 6.1 — People: family, children, portraits

The most rewarding thing you will photograph and the one where small technical
choices show up most obviously.

**** The four things that matter, in order

1. *Focus on the near eye.* Not the face — the eye, and specifically the one
   closer to the camera. At f/5.6 and a couple of metres, the depth of field is
   thin enough that focusing on the far eye leaves the near one soft, and it
   reads as a mistake even to people who cannot say why.

2. *Stand far enough away.* At least two metres from a face, then zoom to frame.
   Part 5 explains why. This is free and it matters more than your lens.

3. *Find the light before you pose anyone.* Move the person to good light rather
   than photographing them where they happen to be standing. Thirty seconds of
   looking around beats any amount of editing.

4. *Watch the background.* Faces are what you look at; backgrounds are what ruin
   the photograph.

Everything else is refinement.

**** Getting background blur out of a kit lens

Your 18–140 mm is not a fast lens. At the long end it opens to f/5.6, and you
will read endlessly that you need f/1.8 for "proper" portraits. You do not — you
need to understand the three levers, only one of which is aperture.

Background blur increases when you:

1. *Zoom in.* Use 105–140 mm rather than 50 mm. This is the biggest lever you
   have and it costs nothing.
2. *Increase the distance between subject and background.* Move your subject away
   from the wall. Ten metres of separation at 140 mm f/5.6 melts a background
   completely.
3. *Open the aperture.* f/5.6 is as wide as you get at the long end. It is
   enough.

A person at 140 mm f/5.6, standing three metres from you and ten metres from a
hedge, gives you thoroughly blurred background. The people who insist you need
f/1.8 are usually shooting at 50 mm with the subject a metre from a wall, where
nothing would help.

**** Light for faces, in order of how easy it is

*A window, indoors.* The single best free light source there is. Put your subject
beside it, not facing it head-on — turn them so the light comes across the face at
roughly 45°. Closer to the window means softer light. Turn the room lights off,
or you will mix colour temperatures and make the file harder to edit.

*Open shade, outdoors.* On a sunny day, put your subject just inside the edge of
shade — under a tree, in a doorway, beside a building — *facing out toward the
open sky.* They get soft, even light and no squinting. This is the workhorse
technique for outdoor portraits and it takes ten seconds to set up.

*Overcast.* The whole sky is a softbox. Easy and flattering. The one catch is
that light comes from directly above, so eye sockets can go dark. Put your
subject under something that blocks overhead light — a porch, a tree — so light
arrives more from the side.

*Golden hour backlight.* Sun low and behind your subject, rimming their hair.
Beautiful and the trickiest to expose: the meter sees the bright background and
underexposes the face. Add *+1 to +1.7 exposure compensation*, or spot meter on
the face. Use a lens hood or you will get washed-out flare.

*Direct midday sun.* The hardest. Harsh shadows, dark eye sockets, squinting. If
you have no choice, turn them so the sun is behind or to the side and expose for
the face — never have them face into it.

**** The classic lighting patterns

With one light source, these are the shapes you can make. Move the subject's head,
not the light.

| Pattern       | Light position               | Look                              |
|---------------+------------------------------+-----------------------------------|
| *Loop*        | 30–45° to the side, slightly above | Small nose shadow toward the mouth corner. Most common, most flattering |
| *Rembrandt*   | Further to the side, higher  | Nose and cheek shadows join, leaving a triangle of light on the far cheek. Moody |
| *Butterfly*   | Straight on, high            | Small symmetrical shadow under the nose. Glamorous, emphasises cheekbones |
| *Split*       | 90° to the side              | Half the face lit, half dark. Bold, dramatic, rarely flattering |

You do not need to memorise these. Know they exist, then watch the nose shadow as
you turn someone's head and you will find them yourself.

**** Photographing children

Different discipline entirely from adult portraiture.

*Get down to their height.* This is the whole game. A photograph taken from adult
standing height looking down at a child is a photograph about an adult looking
down at a child. Kneel, sit, lie on the floor. Every single time.

*Raise your shutter speed.* Children move faster and more unpredictably than you
expect. 1/250 s is a minimum; 1/500 s is safer. Let Auto ISO handle the cost.

*Use =AF-C= and hold the back button.* Do not lock focus — they will have moved.
Group-area or Dynamic-area 9 are both good.

*Shoot in bursts, but short ones.* =CL= at three or four frames per second, in
bursts of three or four. Expressions change between frames and the good one is
rarely the one you would have picked.

*Do not say "smile."* It produces the fake face every family album is full of.
Instead give them something to *do* — a game, a question, something to hold, a
task. Photograph them doing it. The real expressions arrive in the gaps.

*Let them ignore you.* The best photographs of children happen about twenty
minutes after they have stopped caring that you have a camera. Be boring first.

**** Groups and family gatherings

*Stop down to f/8.* At f/5.6 with people in two rows, the back row goes soft.
f/8 gives you the depth to cover a group; f/11 for a big one.

*Focus on the front row.* Depth of field extends roughly twice as far behind the
focus point as in front. Focusing on the front row uses that correctly; focusing
on the middle wastes it.

*Take more frames than feels necessary.* In any group of six, someone is blinking
in any given frame. Five or six frames of the same arrangement is not excessive —
it is the only way you get one with everybody's eyes open.

*Arrange for height, not for height order.* Staggered heads read better than a
straight line. Get people close together — closer than feels natural. Gaps
between people look like gaps.

**** Directing someone who feels awkward

Almost everybody dislikes being photographed. The technical part is easy; this
part is the actual skill.

- *Talk continuously.* Silence makes people self-conscious. Narrate what you are
  doing, even if it is boring.
- *Give hands a job.* Hands are where awkwardness shows. In a pocket with the
  thumb out, holding a cup, resting on something. Anything but hanging.
- *Angle the body, turn the face.* Square-on to the camera is confrontational and
  makes people look wider. Turn shoulders slightly away and bring the face back.
- *Chin forward and slightly down.* Feels absurd, defines the jawline, works.
- *The exhale trick.* Ask them to breathe out fully and let their face relax.
  Shoot in the pause afterwards. Reliably produces the most natural expression of
  the session.
- *Show them a good one.* Early on, find a frame that works and show them the
  back of the camera. Confidence transforms everything that follows.

*** Chapter 6.2 — Landscape and travel

The genre where your tripod and your patience matter more than your lens, and
where the D7200's sensor genuinely shines.

**** The honest limitation first

*18 mm on DX is not very wide.* It gives you the same field of view as 27 mm on
full frame. Landscape photographers talk endlessly about 14 mm and 16 mm lenses,
which on your camera would be genuinely sweeping. Yours is moderately wide.

This is less of a problem than it sounds, for two reasons. Beginners
systematically over-use wide angles, producing photographs where everything is
tiny and far away and nothing is interesting. And your telephoto end — which most
landscape beginners never use — is arguably the more valuable half. More on that
below.

**** The tripod workflow

You own a tripod and a corded remote. That combination unlocks the whole genre.
Use them properly:

1. Turn the dial to =U2=.
2. *Turn VR off on the lens.* On a tripod, stabilisation can hunt for motion that
   is not there and introduce blur. This is a physical switch and it is not stored
   in the bank — you have to remember.
3. Set up and level the camera using the virtual horizon (=info= button to cycle
   display modes).
4. Compose, taking a proper look at all four edges.
5. *Focus in live view*: press =Lv=, zoom to 19×, focus manually on your chosen
   point. This is more accurate than autofocus.
6. Fire with the corded remote, or the 2-second self-timer.
7. Check the histogram and the blinkies. Reshoot if the sky has clipped.

Step 5 is the one people skip and it is the one that makes the difference between
"quite sharp" and "actually sharp."

**** Aperture and where to focus

*f/8 to f/11.* f/8 is your lens's sharpest aperture; f/11 buys extra depth of
field at a slight cost. Going to f/16 or f/22 gets you more depth and a visibly
softer image everywhere thanks to diffraction. It is almost never the right
trade.

*Focus at the hyperfocal distance*, not at infinity. At 18 mm f/8, that is about
two metres in — everything from one metre to the horizon comes out sharp. The
table is in Part 4 and again in Book II.

**** Composition for landscape

*Find a foreground.* This is the difference between a landscape photograph and a
holiday snap. A wide view with nothing in front of it is a flat postcard. Put
something in the near frame — a rock, flowers, a fence, texture in the sand — and
get *low and close* to it. At 18 mm, something half a metre away looms large while
the mountain stays fully visible. That is your depth.

*Decide where the horizon goes.* Never dead centre unless you are being
deliberately symmetrical. Dramatic sky? Give it two thirds. Boring white sky? Give
it a sliver, or crop it out entirely and photograph the land.

*Use the telephoto end.* This is the advice most likely to improve your landscapes
quickly. At 70–140 mm you compress distant ridges into stacked layers, isolate a
single lit tree against a dark hillside, and pull order out of chaos. Wide angles
include everything; telephoto *selects*, and selection is what composition is.

**** Long exposure, since you have the gear

Moving water and cloud turn to silk over a long exposure. You have the tripod and
the remote, so this costs you nothing but time.

Without an ND filter you are limited to low light — dawn, dusk, deep shade, or
after sunset. At f/11 and ISO 100 at blue hour you can easily reach several
seconds.

- Past 30 seconds, use *Time* mode: press the remote once to open, once to close.
  Better than Bulb, which requires holding it down.
- *Focus first*, then switch the lens to =M= so nothing shifts between frames.
- Turn *Long exposure NR* on only if a test frame shows coloured hot pixels.
  Remember it doubles the wait.

**** The one filter worth owning

A *circular polarizer* (67 mm for your lens) is the only filter that does
something darktable cannot. Rotating it:

- Cuts reflections off leaves and wet rock, so foliage goes deep saturated green
- Cuts glare off water so you can see beneath the surface
- Deepens blue sky — strongest at 90° to the sun, and does nothing at all when
  pointed straight at or away from it

It costs you about 1.5–2 stops of light, which on a tripod is free.

Two warnings: at 18 mm on a wide sky it can produce an obviously uneven band of
darker blue across the frame, because the polarising effect varies with angle. And
it kills the reflections in a mirror-still lake, which you may have wanted. It is
a tool, not a permanent fixture.

**** Panoramas

Your 18 mm not being wide enough has a free solution: shoot several frames and
stitch them.

1. Tripod, levelled properly. If the head is not level, the horizon will bow.
2. *Manual mode, fixed ISO, fixed white balance, manual focus.* Everything locked.
   Auto anything will drift between frames and produce visible banding.
3. Turn the camera *vertical* — more height, more pixels.
4. Use around 35–50 mm rather than 18 mm. Wide angles distort and stitch badly.
5. Overlap each frame by about a third.
6. Pan smoothly, six to ten frames.

Stitch in Hugin (free) or GIMP. The result has far more resolution and a wider
view than any single frame, which turns your kit zoom into something considerably
more capable than it looks.

**** Travel: what changes

- *One lens is a feature.* You have one. It covers 27–210 mm equivalent, which is
  most of what travel needs. Not choosing lenses means more time looking.
- *Photograph early.* The light is better and the crowds are absent. The hour
  after sunrise at any famous place is a different world from ten o'clock.
- *Photograph the ordinary things.* Doorways, market stalls, hands, signage,
  breakfast. In ten years these will mean more than another frame of the
  cathedral, of which better versions exist on a thousand postcards.
- *Back up every night.* Copy the card to a laptop or a phone. Cards are stolen
  and lost. See Part 8.
- *The battery.* This is where not owning a spare will hurt most. Buy one before
  you travel.

*** Chapter 6.3 — Street and everyday

Photographing ordinary life as you move through it. The genre with the least gear
and the most skill.

**** What makes it hard

Not the camera. The two hard parts are *seeing something worth photographing in a
scene that looks like nothing*, and *being ready before the moment ends*. Both are
practice, not equipment.

**** Be ready before you need to be

The most common street failure is fumbling with settings while the moment
evaporates. Solve it by having the camera already correct:

- Turn the dial to =U1= as you leave the house
- Shutter 1/250 s minimum, aperture f/8, Auto ISO to 6400
- Camera on, lens cap off, strap over your shoulder rather than round your neck

#+begin_quote
*Tip —* Set the camera to =Q= (quiet) drive mode for street work. It does not
make the camera silent, but it slows the mirror and splits the sound so it is
markedly less conspicuous. The difference in how people react is real.
#+end_quote

**** Zone focusing: the technique that beats autofocus

Autofocus takes a fraction of a second. Sometimes that fraction is the photograph.

Zone focusing removes it entirely. At f/8 and a moderately wide focal length,
depth of field is deep enough that a broad band of distance is already sharp — so
you pre-focus on that band and never focus again.

1. Set 24–35 mm, f/8, and =AF-S=
2. Focus once on something about three metres away
3. Switch the lens to =M= to lock it
4. Anything roughly 2–5 m away is now sharp

You can now raise the camera and shoot instantly, with zero lag. This is how a
great deal of classic street photography was made, and it works exactly as well
now as it did then.

#+begin_quote
*Tip —* Zone focusing pairs beautifully with shooting from chest height without
raising the camera to your eye. Because you are not focusing and not composing
through the viewfinder, you can take a photograph in complete silence of movement.
Your framing will be wrong at first; it gets good surprisingly fast.
#+end_quote

**** What to actually look for

- *Light first.* A shaft of sun between buildings, a lit doorway, a pool of light
  on a pavement. Find the light, then wait for someone to walk into it. This is
  the single most productive street technique there is.
- *Gesture over face.* A hand reaching, a shoulder turning, someone mid-stride.
  Gesture carries emotion, and it does not require anyone's face to be identifiable.
- *Layers.* Something near, something middle, something far, all doing something.
  Hard to pull off, extremely satisfying when it works.
- *Wait somewhere good.* Find a strong background — a bright wall, an interesting
  doorway, a graphic shadow — and simply wait for the frame to complete itself.
  Far more productive than wandering.

**** Manners and the law

In the US, the UK and most of Europe, photographing people in public places is
legal. That does not make every photograph decent.

- If someone objects, stop and be gracious. You lose nothing.
- A nod and a smile defuses almost everything. If you look furtive, people react
  to the furtiveness.
- Be careful photographing children you do not know, and around schools. Legal is
  not the same as sensible.
- Some places that feel public are private — shopping centres, stations, museums.
  Staff can ask you to stop and you should comply.

*** Chapter 6.4 — Flowers and close-up

You said flowers, and flowers are the ideal close-up subject for the lens you
own — which is fortunate, because that lens has real limits and flowers sit
comfortably inside them.

**** What your lens can and cannot do

| Specification              | Value                     |
|----------------------------+---------------------------|
| Minimum focus distance     | 0.45 m from the sensor plane |
| Maximum reproduction ratio | *0.23×*                   |
| Smallest subject that fills the frame width | *about 10 cm* |

Ten centimetres is a large flower head, a rose, a mug, your fist. That is
genuinely useful for flowers and completely useless for insects.

#+begin_quote
*Watch out —* You will see larger magnification figures quoted for this lens —
0.31× turns up often. Nikon's own specification is *0.23×* (1:4.3). The difference
matters: it is what separates expecting insect photography from expecting flower
photography.
#+end_quote

**** Getting maximum magnification

1. Zoom to *140 mm*
2. Turn the focus ring to its *closest* setting and leave it there
3. Now *move the whole camera* forward and back until the subject snaps sharp

That third step is the technique. At close distances, rocking the camera is far
more precise than turning the focus ring, because the ring moves the focus plane
in large jumps while your body moves it in fractions of a millimetre.

#+begin_quote
*Tip —* Do not use autofocus at minimum distance. It hunts endlessly, because at
that magnification any tiny movement throws it out. Manual focus plus rocking is
faster and more reliable.
#+end_quote

**** Depth of field is the whole problem

At 140 mm and minimum focus distance, your depth of field is a few millimetres,
even stopped down. A flower that looks flat to your eye is deep to your lens.

- Use *f/8 to f/11*. Enough depth to hold a flower head, still short of
  diffraction.
- *Align the sensor with the subject.* If the flower is roughly flat, get the back
  of the camera parallel to it and the whole thing lands in the sharp zone at
  once. This is worth more than two stops of aperture.
- Decide *what specifically must be sharp* — usually the stamen or the nearest
  petal edge — and put the focus exactly there. In a close-up, "the flower is in
  focus" is not precise enough.

**** Focus stacking, since you have a tripod

When one frame cannot hold the whole subject, take several at different focus
distances and merge them.

1. Tripod, no wind, =M= mode, manual focus, fixed white balance
2. Focus on the nearest point you want sharp. Shoot.
3. Nudge focus very slightly further. Shoot. Repeat until past the furthest point.
4. Eight to fifteen frames for a flower

Merge in GIMP, or in Hugin's =enfuse=. Both free. If you see blurred bands in the
result, your steps were too big.

#+begin_quote
*Tip —* Focus stacking and wind are completely incompatible. If a single leaf is
moving, stop and come back on a still morning. Early morning is also when
flowers are freshest and the light is soft — the same trip solves both problems.
#+end_quote

**** Light for flowers

*Overcast is ideal.* Soft, even, saturated colour, no blown highlights on white
petals. The day everyone else stays home is the best flower day of the week.

*Backlight for translucent petals.* Put the sun behind a poppy or a leaf and the
petal glows from within, veins and all. Expose for the petal — usually around
−0.7 compensation to keep it from blowing out. This is the most striking flower
technique there is and it costs nothing.

*Diffuse harsh sun.* If you are stuck in bright sunshine, hold anything
translucent between sun and flower — a white shirt, a carrier bag, a sheet of
paper. Instant softbox.

*Block the wind with your own body.* Frequently the difference between a sharp
frame and a blurred one.

**** Backgrounds are where close-ups are won

A flower is easy. A flower against a bright chaotic background is a mess.

- *Get low and shoot slightly upward* to put sky or distant shade behind it
  rather than the ground
- *Maximise the distance* between flower and background — a metre is
  transformative
- 140 mm at f/5.6 with a distant background gives a completely clean wash of
  colour

#+begin_quote
*Tip —* Carry a piece of A4 card, black on one side and white on the other, in
your bag. Held half a metre behind a flower it gives you an instant clean
background anywhere. Costs nothing, weighs nothing, works every time.
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
*Tip —* A small spray bottle of water makes convincing dew on petals. Slightly
cheating, entirely standard practice, looks wonderful. Spray from above and let it
settle rather than blasting the flower.
#+end_quote

**** If you want to go closer

Two cheap routes, when flowers stop being enough:

- *Extension tubes*, about $30–60 for a Nikon-fit set with electronic contacts.
  Hollow rings between body and lens; no glass, so no quality loss. They reduce
  minimum focus distance and increase magnification substantially. You lose
  infinity focus and some light.
- *A close-up lens* (a screw-on magnifying filter, 67 mm), about $25–50. Simpler,
  keeps infinity focus, costs a little image quality at the edges.

Neither is needed for flowers. Both open up insects. Do not buy either until the
0.23× limit has actually frustrated you.

*** Chapter 6.5 — The zoo, and animals on the street

You told me you photograph animals at the zoo and in the street. That is a
genuinely different discipline from wildlife photography, and no general guide
covers it, because guides are written by people photographing eagles in Alaska.

Zoo photography has its own problems: barriers, glass, terrible light, and
backgrounds full of concrete and other visitors. Every one of them is solvable.

**** Set the camera up for it before you arrive

| Setting             | Value               | Why                                    |
|---------------------+---------------------+----------------------------------------|
| Image area          | *1.3× (18×12)*      | 273 mm equivalent, and wider AF coverage |
| NEF bit depth       | 12-bit lossless     | Together with the crop: *7 fps, 44-frame buffer* |
| Mode                | =M= with Auto ISO   | Lock shutter and aperture               |
| Shutter             | 1/500 s             | Animals move suddenly and without warning |
| Aperture            | f/5.6 (wide open at 140 mm) | Blur the enclosure behind them  |
| ISO                 | Auto, max 6400      | Zoo houses are dark                     |
| Focus mode          | =AF-C=, back button | —                                      |
| AF-area             | Single-point, or Dynamic 9 | —                               |
| *=a3= lock-on*      | *4 or 5 (Long)*     | *Stops the camera jumping to the fence* |
| Drive               | =CL=                | Short bursts for expressions           |

That =a3= setting is the one nobody adjusts and the one that matters most here.
Set high, the camera stubbornly holds focus on the animal when wire, branches or
a passing head cross the frame. Set low, it leaps to the obstruction constantly.
This single change will noticeably raise your keeper rate at a zoo.

**** Shooting through wire and fences — the trick that feels like magic

You can make a wire fence disappear entirely. It is not editing; it is optics.

1. Get the *front of the lens as close to the wire as physically possible* —
   touching it, if allowed
2. Zoom *long* — 140 mm
3. Aperture *wide open* — f/5.6
4. Focus on the *animal*, not the wire

The wire is now so far inside your minimum focus distance and so utterly out of
focus that it dissolves into nothing. Frames come out looking like there was no
barrier at all.

#+begin_quote
*Tip —* Autofocus will often insist on grabbing the wire regardless. Two fixes:
set =a3= lock-on to 5, or simply focus manually. Manual focus through a fence is
easier than fighting the camera for it.
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
*Tip —* Position yourself so the wire is in *shade* rather than direct sun. Sunlit
wire is bright enough to leave a milky haze across the frame even when completely
out of focus. Shaded wire vanishes far more cleanly.
#+end_quote

**** Shooting through glass

Harder than wire, and still very doable.

1. *Put the lens right against the glass*, or as close as you can. A *collapsible
   rubber hood* (a cheap screw-on accessory, 67 mm) is ideal — it cushions the
   contact and blocks side light. Your lens's own HB-32 hood is rigid plastic and
   less suited to pressing against glass.
2. *Shoot perpendicular to the glass.* At an angle you get distortion and doubled
   reflections.
3. *Turn the flash off.* Always. It will bounce straight back into the lens, and
   at a zoo it is also rude to the animal.
4. *Wear dark clothing.* You are the brightest thing behind you, and you will
   photograph your own reflection. A white shirt is a portable reflection machine.
5. *Use your hand or a jacket* to shade the glass around the lens.

#+begin_quote
*Tip —* A circular polarizer is genuinely excellent here. Rotating it kills
reflections on glass in a way nothing in darktable can replicate, because the
reflection is real light that would otherwise be recorded. This is the strongest
argument for buying one, and it is the one on your list at number two.
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
*Watch out —* Zoo glass is often thick, scratched and slightly green. Expect a
colour cast and reduced contrast. Both are fixable in darktable: correct the
white balance off something neutral, then add contrast back. Do not fight it in
camera.
#+end_quote

**** Dealing with the light

Most zoos open around ten and close around five, which is exactly the worst light
of the day,
and indoor houses are dim and lit with mixed artificial sources.

- *Go at opening time.* Better light, fewer people, and — the real reason —
  animals are far more active in the morning. By afternoon most are asleep.
- *Overcast days are better than sunny ones* for the zoo. Soft light, no harsh
  shadows through wire, no blown highlights on pale fur.
- *Indoors, do not be afraid of ISO 6400.* Part 3 said this and it applies here
  more than anywhere. A sharp noisy frame of an animal doing something beats a
  clean blurred one.
- *Never use flash.* Rude, often banned, useless through glass, and it distresses
  the animal.

**** Backgrounds are the real problem

Technically zoo photography is easy. Making a photograph that does not obviously
look like a zoo is the hard part, and it is entirely a background problem: fences,
painted concrete, feeding troughs, signage, visitors in bright coats.

- *Wait for the animal to move* to a spot with a clean background. Patience beats
  every other technique here. Ten minutes at one enclosure beats ten enclosures.
- *Shoot at 140 mm, wide open, from as far back as you can* — maximum background
  blur.
- *Change your height.* Crouching often replaces a concrete wall with foliage or
  sky.
- *Go tight.* A frame-filling head-and-shoulders portrait of an animal has no
  room left for the enclosure. This is the reliable solution.
- *Focus on the eye.* Same rule as human portraits, same reason.

**** Animals in the street

Cats on walls, dogs in parks, pigeons, urban foxes, ducks. Opportunistic, so the
camera has to be ready.

- *Get down to their eye level.* The single biggest improvement, exactly as with
  children. A photograph of a cat taken from standing height is a photograph of
  the top of a cat.
- *1/500 s minimum.* Animals move faster than they appear to.
- *Focus on the near eye*, always.
- *Approach low and slow*, and stop before they get uncomfortable. Photographs of
  relaxed animals look different from photographs of wary ones.
- *Let them come to you.* Sit down and wait. Cats in particular will approach a
  stationary person and ignore an advancing one.

#+begin_quote
*Tip —* For dogs and cats, a sudden soft noise just before you shoot gets ears up
and eyes toward the camera. It works once, occasionally twice, then never again —
so have the frame composed and focused before you make it.
#+end_quote

*** Chapter 6.6 — Motion: freezing it and using it

**** Freezing motion

How fast a shutter you need depends on the subject, but also on *direction* —
something crossing your frame needs a faster shutter than the same thing coming
toward you, because it travels further across the sensor.

| Subject                       | Across the frame | Toward you |
|-------------------------------+------------------+------------|
| Person walking                | 1/250            | 1/125      |
| Child running, dog playing    | 1/500            | 1/250      |
| Cyclist, jogger               | 1/500            | 1/250      |
| Car at town speed             | 1/1000           | 1/500      |
| Bird in flight                | 1/2000           | 1/1000     |
| Splashing water, droplets     | 1/2000+          | —          |

#+begin_quote
*Tip —* When in doubt, go faster and let Auto ISO pay for it. Noise is fixable in
darktable. Motion blur is not fixable anywhere.
#+end_quote

**** Panning: sharp subject, streaked background

The technique that makes movement look like movement.

1. Shutter *1/30 s* for a cyclist or runner, *1/60 s* for a car
2. =AF-C=, Dynamic-area 9, back-button focus held down
3. VR *on*
4. Stand with feet apart, twist your upper body to where the subject will appear
5. Track it smoothly, *rotating from the hips*, not the wrists
6. Fire a burst *while still rotating*
7. *Follow through* after the shutter, like a golf swing

Expect a keeper rate around one in ten at first. That is normal — even
professionals shoot a lot of frames for one good pan. The follow-through is the
part beginners skip and the part that makes it work.

**** Deliberate blur

Blur is not always a failure. On a tripod:

- *Water* — 1/4 s makes it feel like flowing water; several seconds turns it to
  mist. Both are valid; they say different things.
- *Cloud* — 30 seconds or more streaks movement across the sky.
- *Crowds* — a few seconds turns people into ghosts and leaves architecture sharp.

Handheld, *intentional camera movement* — panning vertically along a tree trunk at
1/8 s, for example — produces abstracts. Mostly it produces rubbish, occasionally
something lovely. Cheap to try.

**** Drive modes and the buffer

Set with the dial under the mode dial (hold the release button to turn it).

| Mode  | Behaviour                                                    |
|-------+--------------------------------------------------------------|
| =S=   | One frame per press. Landscapes, portraits, deliberate work   |
| =CL=  | Continuous low, 1–6 fps, set via =d2=. Your default for action |
| =CH=  | Continuous high — 5 fps at 14-bit DX, 6 at 12-bit, 7 in crop  |
| =Q=   | Quiet — see below. Street, ceremonies                          |
| Timer | Self-timer, configured via =c3=                               |
| =Mup= | Mirror up — two presses. Maximum vibration control on tripod  |

#+begin_quote
*Tip —* Use =CL= rather than =CH= for most action. Six frames per second of a
child's face gives you six nearly identical expressions and a full buffer. Three
frames per second across a longer window gives you more genuinely different
moments to choose from.
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
*Watch out —* *You cannot shoot a burst with flash.* In both =CL= and =CH=, Nikon's
manual is explicit: only one picture is taken if the flash fires. The flash needs
time to recycle, so continuous mode simply stops after one frame. If you are using
fill flash at a party, you are shooting single frames whether you meant to or not.
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
*Tip —* =Q= mode is more useful than "quieter." The mirror does not clack back down
while you keep the shutter button fully pressed — so you take the picture, then
choose when the loudest part of the noise happens, by easing off the button a
moment later. Hold it down through a quiet moment in a ceremony and release when
someone coughs. The beep is also disabled in =Q=, regardless of =d1=.
#+end_quote

*Buffer discipline.* At 14-bit DX you get eighteen frames before the camera slows
to card speed — under four seconds. Fire in short bursts of three or four and let
it recover. Holding the button down means you will be waiting, buffer full, at
the exact moment something happens.

For a day of action, switch to *12-bit lossless in 1.3× crop*: 7 fps and 44
frames, which is over six seconds of continuous shooting.

** Part 7 — Flash

Flash has a bad reputation, and it earned it honestly: almost everyone's first
experience of flash is a harsh, flat, red-eyed picture that looks worse than the
dim one it replaced.

That reputation comes from one specific mistake — using flash as the *only* light,
pointed straight at the subject from the camera. Used properly, flash is not a
way of lighting a dark room. It is a way of *adding a controlled amount of light
to light that is already there.*

*** How flash works on this camera

**** i-TTL — the automatic system

When you take a flash picture, the camera fires an almost invisible *pre-flash*
first, measures how much came back, calculates the right power, and then fires the
real flash — all in a few thousandths of a second. Nikon calls this i-TTL and it
works well.

You do not need to calculate anything. You need to know how to *adjust* what it
decides, which is flash compensation.

**** Flash compensation, and its asymmetric range

*Press the flash button and rotate the sub-command dial* (the front one, under
your index finger).

The range is *−3 EV to +1 EV* in third-stop steps.

Note that it is asymmetric: you can cut flash power by three stops but only add
one. That tells you something about how Nikon expects you to use it — mostly
turning it down.

#+begin_quote
*Watch out —* Flash compensation is *not reset when you turn the camera off.*
Like exposure compensation, it is sticky. Check it at the start of a session.
#+end_quote

**** Sync speed: the 1/250 ceiling

Your camera's shutter is two curtains that travel across the sensor. Below 1/250 s
the sensor is fully uncovered at some instant, so a flash can light all of it.

*Above 1/250 s the curtains form a moving slit* — there is never a moment when the
whole sensor is exposed at once. A normal flash would light only the strip that
happened to be open, leaving a black band across your picture.

So *1/250 s is your maximum shutter speed with flash.* The camera enforces it.

There is a way around it — *Auto FP high-speed sync*, via *Custom Settings → e
Bracketing/flash → e1 Flash sync speed → 1/320 s (Auto FP)* — which pulses the
flash continuously as the slit travels, allowing sync all the way to 1/8000 s. It
requires a compatible Speedlight and costs a lot of flash power. Not available
with the built-in flash. File it away for later.

**** Flash modes

*Press the flash button and rotate the main command dial* (the rear one).

| Mode                   | What it does                                  | Available in |
|------------------------+-----------------------------------------------+--------------|
| *Fill flash*           | Fires every shot. Your default                | All          |
| *Red-eye reduction*    | Lamp lights first. Slow and annoying          | Most         |
| *Slow sync*            | Lets shutter go slow to capture the background | P, A         |
| *Rear-curtain sync*    | Fires at the *end* of the exposure            | S, M         |
| *Slow rear-curtain*    | Both of the above                             | P, A         |
| *Off*                  | No flash                                      | All          |

#+begin_quote
*Watch out —* Rear-curtain sync is a *flash mode*, not a menu item. It is not
=e1= — that is Flash sync speed. And it is only offered in =S= and =M=; in =P= and
=A= you get the slow rear-curtain variant instead.
#+end_quote

*Why rear-curtain matters:* with a slow shutter and a moving subject, front-curtain
sync freezes the subject at the *start* and then records motion blur *ahead* of
them — so the light trails run in front, which looks wrong. Rear-curtain fires at
the end, leaving the trails *behind* the subject, which looks right. If you ever
photograph a moving car or a dancer with a slow shutter, this is the setting.

*** The built-in flash, honestly

Your pop-up flash has a guide number of about *12 metres at ISO 100*, a minimum
range of *0.6 m*, and it sits about four centimetres above the lens.

That position is the problem. Light from directly above the lens produces flat,
shadowless, characterless illumination — the "passport photo" look — because there
are no shadows to describe shape.

So here is the honest assessment:

*The built-in flash is bad as a main light and genuinely good as a fill light.*

#+begin_quote
*Watch out —* Take the lens hood off before using the pop-up flash, or it will
cast a shadow across the bottom of your frame. Nikon's manual also notes the
built-in flash cannot cover the close-focus range of the zoom — at minimum focus
distance the lens barrel itself blocks it.
#+end_quote

**** Fill flash: the one thing the pop-up is excellent at

This is the technique worth learning, and it is genuinely useful.

*The situation:* bright sunshine, a person, harsh shadows under their eyes and
nose. Or: a person in shade against a bright background, where exposing for the
background makes them a silhouette.

*The fix:* a small amount of flash to lift the shadows, while the sun stays the
main light.

1. Mode =A=, aperture f/5.6–f/8
2. Pop the flash up
3. Set flash compensation to *−1.0*
4. Shoot

You are not lighting the person with the flash. You are adding just enough to open
the shadows. Done right, nobody can tell flash was used — which is exactly the
goal.

#+begin_quote
*Tip —* If it looks like flash, dial it further down. −1.0 is a starting point;
−1.7 is often better. The correct amount of fill flash is the largest amount
nobody notices.
#+end_quote

**** Diffusing the pop-up

The pop-up is a small, hard light source. Make it bigger and it gets softer. A
piece of white tissue, a folded paper napkin, or a translucent film canister
taped over it all work.

You lose a stop or two of power, which the pop-up cannot really spare, but for
close subjects it noticeably improves the quality. Costs nothing to try.

**** Dragging the shutter: fixing the black background

*The problem:* you photograph someone indoors at night. They come out lit and the
room behind them is pitch black. Everything looks like a police photograph.

*The cause:* flash is quick and close. At 1/250 s, the ambient light in the room
contributes essentially nothing — only the flash registers, and flash falls off
sharply with distance, so the background gets none.

*The fix:* slow the shutter so ambient light has time to build up in the
background, while the flash still freezes your subject.

1. Mode =M=
2. Set aperture and ISO so the *room without flash* is roughly correctly exposed —
   typically 1/30 s, f/4, ISO 1600
3. Now add flash, at about −1.0 compensation
4. Shoot

The flash lights the subject; the slow shutter lights the room. The result looks
like a photograph taken in a room rather than in a void.

#+begin_quote
*Tip —* 1/30 s is slow enough that a moving subject will show some ghosting
around the flash-frozen image. Usually this looks like life. If you want it clean,
raise ISO instead so you can use 1/60 s.
#+end_quote

*** When you get a Speedlight

Everything below needs an external flash unit. You said you "sort of" have one —
if you find out what it is, the specifics may change, but the principles hold.

Good used options that work fully with your camera: *SB-700*, *SB-600*, *SB-900*,
*SB-800*. Roughly $130–320 used. All support i-TTL and all can be controlled
wirelessly by your camera's built-in Commander mode.

**** Bounce flash — the single biggest jump in quality available to you

A Speedlight's head tilts and swivels. This is the entire reason to own one.

*Do not point it at your subject.* Point it at a white ceiling or wall. The
ceiling becomes the light source — enormous, and therefore soft. The light now
comes from above and to the side, producing gentle, natural shadows that describe
the face instead of erasing it.

The difference between direct flash and bounced flash is not subtle. It is the
difference between a snapshot and a photograph.

- *Ceiling bounce:* tilt straight up. Pull out the little white bounce card to
  kick a catchlight into the eyes.
- *Wall bounce:* swivel 45–90° toward a side wall. Gives directional, sculpted
  light. Usually better than ceiling bounce.
- *Watch the colour.* Bouncing off a green wall gives you a green subject. Bounce
  off white or neutral surfaces only.
- *Expect to use more power*, since the light travels much further. Raise ISO if
  the flash struggles.

**** Commander mode — a premium feature you already own

The D7200's built-in flash can *wirelessly trigger and control* off-camera
Speedlights, adjusting their power from the camera menu. This was dropped from the
D7500, so you have something its successor does not.

*Custom Settings → e Bracketing/flash → e3 Flash cntrl for built-in flash →
Commander mode.*

Then:

- Set *Built-in flash* to =−−=. It still fires the control pulses but contributes
  no light to the exposure.
- Set *Group A* to =TTL=.
- Note the *Channel* (default 3) and set the same channel on the Speedlight, in
  remote/slave mode, group A.

Now put the flash on a stand — or have someone hold it — at 45° to your subject
and slightly above eye level. You have off-camera lighting, which is where
portraiture genuinely begins.

#+begin_quote
*Watch out —* Commander mode is *optical*. The remote flash must be able to see
the pulses from your camera's pop-up. It works well indoors, where walls bounce
the signal around. It is unreliable outdoors in bright sun and at distance. If a
flash refuses to fire, check the little sensor window on its side is facing the
camera.
#+end_quote

**** Two lights, briefly

Once you have two flashes, put them in *Group A* and *Group B* and set them
independently.

- *Group A* = key light, TTL at 0
- *Group B* = fill light, TTL at −1.0 to −2.0

The difference in power between them is the *lighting ratio*, and it controls how
dramatic the picture is: one stop apart is natural, two stops is moody, three is
theatrical.

** Part 8 — After the shutter

Pressing the shutter is halfway. A raw file is not a photograph yet — it is the
ingredients.

This part is the workflow: getting files off the card, deciding which ones are
worth anything, developing them, and making sure you do not lose them. It does
*not* re-teach darktable; your =DARKTABLE-README.org= does that properly. This is
the scaffolding around it.

*** Why your raw files look worse than the camera's preview

This confuses everyone and it is worth clearing up immediately.

The picture you saw on the back of the camera was a *JPEG* the camera made for
you — with contrast, saturation and sharpening applied. Your raw file has none of
that. Open it in darktable and it will look flatter, duller and softer than what
you remember seeing.

*Nothing is wrong.* That flat file contains considerably more information than the
punchy preview did. Applying contrast is easy; recovering a highlight the camera's
JPEG engine threw away is impossible. You are starting from the ingredients rather
than from someone else's cooking.

*** The folder structure

Keep this simple and keep it on disk. Folders are the thing you can still
understand in ten years, on any computer, with any software.

#+begin_example
Photos/
├── 2026/
│   ├── 2026-08-21 Zoo/
│   ├── 2026-08-29 Family lunch/
│   └── 2026-09-04 Coast walk/
└── 2027/
#+end_example

One folder per outing. Date first in =YYYY-MM-DD= form so it sorts correctly,
then a couple of words you will recognise later.

#+begin_quote
*Tip —* Name the folder for what you will search for, not what you were doing.
"Zoo" is better than "Saturday." "Sarah birthday" is better than "party." You are
writing for yourself in five years, who will remember the event and not the date.
#+end_quote

*Your raw files never move out of these folders and never get edited.* darktable
works non-destructively: it stores your edits as a separate sidecar file
(=.NEF.xmp=) sitting next to the raw, and the raw itself is never modified. You
can always go back to the untouched original, forever.

*** Getting files off the card

1. *Use a card reader, not the camera.* Faster, and it does not drain your
   battery — the one you have only one of.
2. *Copy, do not move.* Copy into a new dated folder, and only then verify.
3. *Check the files opened properly* before you do anything else.
4. *Then format the card in the camera* — not on the computer. Camera formatting
   rebuilds the folder structure properly.

#+begin_quote
*Watch out —* Never format a card until the files are in *two* places. Card
corruption is rare but it is total, and it always happens to the one shoot you
cared about.
#+end_quote

*** Culling: the skill nobody teaches

You will come back from a zoo trip with 400 frames. Editing 400 frames is
miserable and pointless. The skill is deciding quickly which few deserve your
attention.

**** How to do it

*First pass — reject, do not select.* Go through fast and mark only the
definitively broken ones: missed focus, eyes closed, badly clipped, accidental
frames. Do not deliberate. Two seconds each.

*Second pass — pick.* Now go through what is left and mark the ones that are
genuinely good. Be harsh. From 400 frames, twenty picks is generous.

*Third pass — the near-duplicates.* You shot six frames of the same expression.
Pick *one*. This is the pass people skip, and it is the one that turns a set into
an edit.

#+begin_quote
*Tip —* A brutal cull is the single fastest way to look like a better
photographer. Nobody judges you on the photos you did not show them. Ten strong
pictures from a day is an excellent day; forty mediocre ones is a worse day, even
though it contains the same ten.
#+end_quote

**** What to actually look for

In order:

1. *Is it sharp where it needed to be?* Check at 100%, on the eye or the critical
   detail. Unsharp is unfixable — reject and move on.
2. *Did anything important clip?* Blown highlights with no detail.
3. *Is the background clean?* Or is there a bin, a pole, a stranger's arm?
4. *Is there a subject?* Can you say in one sentence what the picture is of? If
   not, it is probably a photograph of "some stuff," and those never improve.
5. *Only then:* does it make you feel anything?

Points 1–4 are technical and you can judge them immediately. Point 5 needs
distance — which is why the next tip matters.

#+begin_quote
*Tip —* Do not cull on the day you shot. You are still attached to how much
effort a frame cost you, and effort is invisible to everyone else. Wait a day.
Your judgement improves enormously overnight.
#+end_quote

*** The first edit: a sane order of operations

Your darktable guide gives you one excellent rule, and I am going to repeat it
because it is the difference between learning the software and abandoning it:

#+begin_quote
*For your first two weeks, use only the Quick Access Panel and the crop tool.*
#+end_quote

Do not browse the module list. Get fluent with eight sliders first.

Within that, work in this order. Order matters, because early decisions change
what the later ones need to do.

1. *Crop and straighten.* Decide the frame first. Everything after depends on
   what is actually in the picture, and a level horizon changes how you read the
   rest.

2. *White balance.* Set the overall colour cast before judging anything about
   tone. Get the neutrals neutral, then warm or cool it to taste.

3. *Exposure.* Overall brightness. Get this roughly right before touching
   anything local.

4. *Highlights and shadows.* Pull back blown skies, open up dark foregrounds.
   This is where the D7200's dynamic range pays you back — you can lift shadows
   hard.

5. *Contrast and local contrast.* Add the punch that the raw file lacks. Local
   contrast is darktable's version of what Lightroom calls Clarity, and it lives
   in the Quick Access Panel.

6. *Colour.* Saturation and vibrance, gently. This is where beginners
   overcook — see below.

7. *Sharpening and noise.* Last, always. Sharpening early amplifies problems you
   then have to fix.

#+begin_quote
*Tip —* Set up the *auto-apply denoise preset* described in section 6 of your
darktable guide before you do anything else. It reads the D7200's measured noise
profile and applies it automatically to every high-ISO file you import, forever.
Five minutes, once, and step 7 stops being your problem. It is the highest-value
setup step in either guide.
#+end_quote

Do the same for lens corrections, scoped to your 18–140mm. Distortion and
vignetting then correct themselves on import.

**** The beginner mistakes, named

Everyone makes all four. Knowing their names helps you catch yourself.

- *Oversaturation.* The colours look great while you are dragging the slider and
  garish an hour later. Push it until it looks right, then pull it back by a
  third.
- *Too much local contrast.* Produces bright halos along high-contrast edges — a
  glow outline around trees against sky. Once you have seen it you cannot unsee
  it.
- *Crushed blacks.* Dragging shadows down until dark areas become featureless
  black. Some deep black is good; a whole region of nothing is not.
- *Oversharpening.* Gritty, crunchy edges and speckled skies. Sharpening cannot
  add detail that is not there; it can only make edges harsher.

#+begin_quote
*Tip —* Walk away and come back. Edit an image, leave it for an hour, then look
again. Your eye adapts as you work, so the file drifts further and further while
seeming fine at each step. A fresh look catches it every time.
#+end_quote

*** When to open GIMP

Almost never, and that is the correct amount. Your =GIMP-README.org= makes the same
point, and it is worth restating:

*Do everything you can in darktable.* It is non-destructive, it is faster, and
your raw file stays untouched.

Open GIMP only for jobs darktable genuinely cannot do:

- Removing a *large* object — a bin, a person, a power line across a sky
- Combining two frames — a focus stack, an exposure blend, swapping a head in a
  group shot
- Serious skin retouching
- Compositing anything

That is roughly a once-a-month tool. If you find yourself opening it weekly, you
are probably doing something in GIMP that darktable would do better.

*** Exporting

Your finished photograph needs to leave darktable as a JPEG. Different
destinations want different settings.

|--------------------+--------+--------------+-----------+---------|
| Web, social, email | JPEG   | sRGB         | 2048 px   | 90      |
| Sending to family  | JPEG   | sRGB         | 2048 px   | 90      |

#+begin_quote
*Tip —* Export to a =_export= subfolder inside each shoot folder, not to your

JPEGs all in one place, ready to be backed up as a unit.
#+end_quote

**** What your 24 megapixels are worth in print

| Print size    | Resolution | Verdict                    |
|---------------+------------+----------------------------|
| Up to 13×20"  | 300 ppi    | Excellent, no upscaling     |
| Up to 20×30"  | 200 ppi    | Very good at normal viewing distance |
| Up to 26×40"  | 150 ppi    | Fine for a big print seen from a few feet |

You have more resolution than you will use. This is also why cropping is not
cheating: throwing away half the width *and* half the height still leaves you a
6-megapixel file, which prints beautifully at 8×10".

*** Backup: the part everyone skips until it is too late

Hard drives fail. Not "might fail" — fail, with certainty, on a timescale of
years. So do SSDs and so do memory cards.

The standard is the *3-2-1 rule*:

| Rule            | Meaning                                          |
|-----------------+--------------------------------------------------|
| *3* copies      | The working copy plus two backups                 |
| *2* media types | Not both on identical drives in the same machine  |
| *1* offsite     | Somewhere a fire or a burglary would not reach    |

A workable version that costs very little:

1. *Working copy* — your computer's drive
2. *Local backup* — an external USB drive. On Windows, File History, or just
   drag the whole =Photos= folder across monthly
3. *Offsite* — a cloud backup service, or a second external drive kept at a
   relative's house and swapped every few months

#+begin_quote
*Watch out —* A backup you have never restored from is not a backup; it is a
hope. Once, pick a random folder, delete it from your working copy, and restore it
from your backup. Do it now, while nothing is at stake.
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
*Tip —* Your raw files and their =.xmp= sidecars are what must survive. Exported
JPEGs can always be regenerated. If storage is tight, back up the raws and
sidecars religiously and the exports casually.
#+end_quote

Take one outing from card to finished picture, doing every step deliberately.

1. Copy the card into a dated folder
2. Verify the files open, then format the card in camera
3. Wait a day
4. Cull: reject the broken, pick the good, kill the near-duplicates
5. Set up the auto-denoise preset if you have not already
6. Edit *three* images, using only the Quick Access Panel and crop, in the order
   above
7. Export at 2048 px sRGB
8. Copy the whole folder to your backup drive

Then look at the three finished pictures next to the untouched raws. That
difference is what editing is.

- You have a folder structure and you use it every time
- You can cull 300 frames down to a handful without agonising
- You can take a raw file to a finished JPEG using the Quick Access Panel

** Part 9 — Getting better

Technique plateaus. Most people reach a competent level in a year and then stay
there for a decade, taking the same photograph repeatedly at gradually higher
resolution.

This part is about not doing that. It is short, because there is not much to say
and all of it is uncomfortable.

*** The habit that matters most

Every week or two, sit down with what you have shot and ask four questions of
each picture. Not "do I like it" — that tells you nothing you can act on.

1. *Is it sharp where it should be?* And if not, *why* — subject movement, camera
   shake, missed focus, or diffraction? Each has a different fix, and guessing
   wrong means practising the wrong thing.
2. *Is the exposure right?* Did anything important clip? Could you have exposed
   brighter and got a cleaner file?
3. *Is there a clear subject, and is the frame free of things that fight it?*
4. *What is the one thing I would change?*

That fourth question is the whole exercise. "The background was cluttered." "I
should have knelt." "1/125 was not fast enough for a running child." Each answer
is a specific, correctable thing — and correctable things are the only things you
can practise.

#+begin_quote
*Tip —* Keep the answers somewhere. A text file is fine. After three months you
will notice the *same* answer recurring, and that recurring answer is precisely
what you should be working on. It is almost never what you thought it was.
#+end_quote

*** Look at your worst photographs, not your best

Everyone reviews their good frames. It feels nice and teaches nothing.

Your failures are where the information is. A photograph that nearly worked and
did not is a diagnosis waiting to happen. Go through the rejects occasionally and
ask what specifically went wrong. That is the fastest feedback loop available to
you and it costs nothing.

*** Constraints, not gear

When you feel stuck, the instinct is to buy something. It never works — new gear
produces a fortnight of enthusiasm and no improvement.

Constraints work far better, because they force you out of the defaults you have
stopped noticing:

- *One focal length for a month.* Tape the zoom ring at 35 mm. You will start
  moving your feet and seeing compositions rather than framing them from wherever
  you happen to be.
- *Thirty-six frames.* Give yourself exactly one "roll," turn image review off,
  and do not look at the back of the camera all day. You will think far harder
  before each shot.
- *One location, one hour.* Do not leave a small area. The first twenty frames
  are obvious, the next twenty are a struggle, and everything worth having is in
  the twenty after that.
- *One subject, fifty ways.* Angle, light, distance, depth of field. Exhausts your
  reflexes and forces invention.
- *Black and white only.* Set Picture Control to Monochrome so the preview matches.
  Removes colour as a crutch and makes you look at light and shape.

*** Projects beat outings

A single afternoon produces single photographs. A *project* — one subject, one
place, one idea, returned to repeatedly over weeks — produces a body of work, and
teaches you things a one-off never can, because you are forced past your first
obvious idea.

Make it narrow and accessible. "My neighbourhood" is too broad to be a project.
"The same tree, once a week, for a year" is a project. So is "hands of people I
know," or "what the light does in my kitchen across a day."

The test of a good project is that you get bored of your first three ideas and
have to find a fourth. That fourth idea is where the interesting work lives.

*** Learn to look at photographs

Most people who want to take better photographs spend all their time taking
photographs and none looking at them. That is like wanting to write without
reading.

Find photographers whose work you admire and look properly — not scrolling, but
sitting with one image for a minute. Ask where the light is coming from. Ask where
the photographer was standing, and how far away, and roughly what focal length.
Ask what they left *out* of the frame.

You will start reverse-engineering pictures automatically, and then you will start
seeing the same opportunities in front of you.

** Part 10 — The features you have not used yet

Everything so far covers ninety per cent of what you will do. This part covers the
rest of the camera: the features that sit in the menus unused, and which of them
are worth your attention.

Read it when you are curious, not in order.

*** Bracketing: insurance for scenes you cannot expose in one frame

Bracketing takes several frames at different exposures with one setup, so that at
least one is right — and so you can blend them later if none is.

Use it when the scene has more range than the sensor can hold: an interior with a
bright window, a sunset over a dark landscape, a backlit subject.

**** How to set it

Available in *P, S, A and M modes only.*

1. *Custom Settings → e Bracketing/flash → e6 Auto bracketing set → =AE only=*
2. *Hold the =BKT= button* (front of the camera, near the lens mount) and turn the
   *main* command dial to choose the *number of shots* — start with =3F=
3. Still holding =BKT=, turn the *sub*-command dial to choose the *increment* —
   start with =1.0= (one stop)
4. Shoot three frames. On a tripod, use =CH= so all three fire in one burst

You now have one frame at the metered exposure, one a stop darker, one a stop
brighter. Blend them in GIMP, or simply pick whichever worked.

To turn it off, hold =BKT= and turn the main dial back to zero. The =BKT=
indicator stays in the viewfinder until you do, which is the point — you will not
forget it is on.

**** What else it can bracket

=e6= also offers *Flash only*, *WB bracketing* and *ADL bracketing*.

#+begin_quote
*Watch out —* White balance bracketing is *not available when shooting NEF (RAW)*.
That is not a limitation worth mourning: you can set any white balance you like on
a raw file afterwards, so bracketing it would be pointless anyway.
#+end_quote

*** Interval timer and time-lapse — two different things

The D7200 has *two separate features* with confusingly similar purposes, in two
different menus. Almost every guide conflates them.

| Feature                  | Menu           | What you get                    |
|--------------------------+----------------+---------------------------------|
| *Interval timer shooting* | Photo Shooting | A folder full of individual raw stills |
| *Time-lapse photography* | Movie Shooting | A finished silent movie, made in-camera |

**** Which to use

*Interval timer* is the one you want for quality work. You get full raw files, so
you can develop them properly, correct exposure drift, and assemble a video
afterwards at whatever resolution you like. It is also how you shoot star trails —
a long sequence of stills to stack later.

*Time-lapse photography* is the convenient one. The camera assembles the movie
itself and you get a finished file with no computer work at all. The cost is that
you get only the movie — no raws, no second chances.

Start with *time-lapse* to see whether you enjoy it, then move to *interval timer*
when you want control.

**** Settings that matter

- *Interval* must be *longer than your shutter speed*, with a second or two spare
  for the camera to write the file. A 20-second exposure needs at least a
  25-second interval.
- *Exposure smoothing* to =On= reduces the flickering you get when the camera
  changes exposure between frames. Essential for sunsets.
- *Long exposure NR* to *Off*, always. It would double every frame's duration and
  punch holes in your sequence.
- *Manual mode, fixed white balance, manual focus.* Anything automatic will drift
  and produce visible flicker.

#+begin_quote
*Watch out —* Exposure smoothing has a catch that trips people up: in =M= mode it
*only works if Auto ISO is on*. In fully manual with fixed ISO the camera has no
lever to smooth with, so the setting does nothing. If you want smoothing in =M=,
leave Auto ISO enabled.
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
*Tip —* Roughly 250 frames gives you ten seconds of finished video at 25 fps. At a
5-second interval that is about 21 minutes of standing around. Bring a chair, and
watch your battery — this is the single most battery-hungry thing the camera does.
#+end_quote

*** Multiple exposure

Combines two or three frames into a single file, in camera.

*Photo Shooting menu → Multiple exposure:*

- *Multiple exposure mode* → =On (single photo)= for one, =On (series)= to keep going
- *Number of shots* → 2 or 3
- *Auto gain* → =On= (each frame is exposed at a fraction of full so the result is
  not overexposed)

Useful for ghostly portraits, or blending a texture into a face. It is a genuine
creative tool rather than a gimmick, but only if you plan the shot — the good ones
are conceived before the shutter, not discovered afterwards.

#+begin_quote
*Watch out —* Descriptions of an "Overlay mode" with Add, Average, Lighten and
Darken options are describing a D810 or D850. Your camera offers only *Auto gain*,
on or off. It is also limited to *three frames maximum*, works on raw files only,
and cannot be used in live view.
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
*Tip —* You can do all of this far more flexibly in GIMP, with layers you can
adjust afterwards. The reason to do it in camera is that it makes you commit,
which is a different and sometimes better creative discipline.
#+end_quote

*** Video, honestly

The D7200 shoots decent-looking video and is awkward to shoot video *with*. If
video matters to you, this camera is not the reason you will succeed at it.

**** What it can do

| Setting          | Available                              |
|------------------+----------------------------------------|
| Resolution       | 1920×1080 (Full HD) maximum            |
| Frame rates (DX) | 30p, 25p, 24p                          |
| Frame rates (1.3×) | *60p, 50p*, plus the above           |
| Maximum clip     | 29 min 59 s                            |
| Audio            | Built-in stereo mic, or external via 3.5 mm |
| Headphone socket | Yes                                    |

#+begin_quote
*Watch out —* *1080/60p and 50p are only available in the 1.3× crop.* Select DX
image area and the camera silently resets you to 30p or 25p. If you want slow
motion, you must shoot in the crop.
#+end_quote

**** The honest limitations

- *Autofocus during recording is poor.* Contrast-detect, slow, and it hunts
  audibly. Almost everyone focuses manually.
- *No focus peaking*, so manual focus means zooming in before you record and not
  changing distance much.
- *Rolling shutter.* Pan quickly and vertical lines lean — the "jello" effect.
- *The screen does not articulate*, so anything low or high is guesswork.

**** If you shoot video anyway

- *Mode =M=*, and set the shutter to roughly *double your frame rate* — 1/50 s for
  25p, 1/60 s for 30p. This is the "180-degree rule" and it makes motion look
  natural rather than stuttery.
- Because the shutter is locked slow, bright daylight will overexpose. This is the
  one situation where you genuinely want an ND filter.
- *Manual focus*, set before you roll.
- *Fixed white balance.* Auto WB drifting mid-shot is very visible.
- Use a tripod. Handheld DSLR video without stabilisation looks like handheld DSLR
  video.

*** Preset manual white balance — the grey card method

Part 5 said auto white balance is fine because raw lets you fix it later. That is
true. There is one situation where measuring it properly is worth the trouble:
*mixed or strange artificial light*, where there is no correct single value and
you will otherwise fight the file in darktable.

1. Put a *grey card* — or any genuinely neutral white surface — in the light your
   subject is in
2. Hold the =WB= button and turn the main dial to =PRE=
3. Release, then *hold =WB= again until =PRE= flashes* in the display
4. Fill the frame with the card and press the shutter
5. =Good= appears if it worked

The camera now knows exactly what neutral looks like under that light. This is
also the fastest way to fix a zoo aquarium's green cast — measure through the
glass and the cast disappears at source.

*** Using both card slots

You have one card, so this is for later. When you get a second:

*Photo Shooting menu → Role played by card in Slot 2:*

| Option                    | What happens                                       |
|---------------------------+----------------------------------------------------|
| *Overflow*                | Slot 2 is used only once Slot 1 is full. Maximum capacity |
| *Backup*                  | Every picture is written to both cards. Maximum safety |
| *RAW Slot 1 – JPEG Slot 2* | Only applies when shooting RAW+JPEG                |

*Use =Backup= for anything you cannot reshoot* — a family occasion, a trip. It is
the only protection against a card failing that exists at the moment of capture.
Everything else in Part 8 protects you only after you get home.

Both slots are UHS-I. There is no fast slot and slow slot.

#+begin_quote
*Tip —* The camera writes at the speed of the slower card in Backup mode, and the
D7200's internal bus tops out around 30 MB/s anyway. That means an expensive
95 MB/s card buys you nothing over a decent 45 MB/s one, and UHS-II cards buy you
nothing at all. Spend the difference on a second card instead.
#+end_quote

*** Old manual lenses, and why your camera can use them

Your D7200 has two things many cheaper Nikons lack: a *built-in autofocus motor*
and an *aperture indexing tab*. Together they open up decades of inexpensive
second-hand glass.

- *AF and AF-D lenses* ("screw-drive") autofocus normally on your body. They will
  not autofocus on a D3500 or D5600 at all. Excellent primes go cheap because so
  many buyers cannot use them.
- *AI and AI-S manual focus lenses* will mount and *meter correctly*, which is
  unusual and genuinely useful.

**** Registering a non-CPU lens

For AI/AI-S lenses to meter, tell the camera what they are:

*Setup menu → Non-CPU lens data:*

1. Choose a lens number, 1–9
2. Enter the *focal length* and *maximum aperture*
3. Use =A= or =M= mode, and set aperture with the ring on the lens

The meter, and i-TTL flash, now work properly. Focus using the green dot
rangefinder in the viewfinder (Part 4).

This is a niche pleasure rather than a necessity. But a manual 50mm f/1.8 AI-S
costs very little, is beautifully made, and will teach you more about aperture and
focus in a month than any amount of reading.

*** Battery, care and maintenance

**** Battery

You own one EN-EL15. The D7200 is good for a large number of viewfinder frames on
a charge and much worse in live view, in video, and in the cold.

- *Buy a second one.* It is first on your list for a reason.
- *Cold kills batteries temporarily, not permanently.* A battery that dies at
  −5 °C will often recover most of its charge once warm. Keep the spare in an
  inside pocket, against your body.
- *Setup menu → Battery info* shows the charge, the number of shots on this
  charge, and the battery's *age* as a five-step meter. Worth checking on a
  second-hand battery.
- Store batteries around half charged if they will sit unused for months.

**** Sensor dust

Dust arrives on the sensor eventually. It shows as dark specks, most visible in
plain bright areas at small apertures.

1. Check for it: photograph a blank sky or white wall at f/16 and look at the file
2. *Setup menu → Clean image sensor → Clean now*
3. Still there? *Setup menu → Lock mirror up for cleaning*, then a *hand blower* —
   never compressed air, never a finger
4. Stubborn spots need a wet clean. Have it done professionally the first time

Dust is not a fault and not an emergency. A few specks are trivial to remove in
darktable's =retouch= module.

**** General care

- *Change lenses with the body pointing down*, quickly, out of wind
- *Never touch the mirror.* It is front-silvered and marks permanently
- *A UV filter is not protection*, it is an extra air-glass surface that costs you
  contrast. Use the lens hood instead — it protects the front element better and
  improves the image
- *Keep the hood on.* It stops flare and it is the cheapest insurance against
  knocks there is
- *Firmware:* check *Setup menu → Firmware version* against Nikon's download page
  once a year. Newer firmware is what lets the camera use AF-P lenses

#+begin_quote
*Watch out —* Moving a cold camera into a warm room causes condensation, including
*inside* the lens. Coming in from the cold, put the camera in a sealed bag *before*
you go indoors and let it warm up slowly. This is the one environmental mistake
that can actually damage the camera.
#+end_quote

*** The Retouch menu

The camera can process files by itself: crop, resize, convert raw to JPEG, apply
filters, even merge two raws.

*It is almost entirely pointless for you.* You have darktable, which does all of
it better and non-destructively.

One genuine use: *NEF (RAW) processing* lets you make a JPEG from a raw file
without a computer — useful if someone at an event wants a picture immediately and
you shoot raw only. Everything else in that menu is a solution to a problem you do
not have.

*Field shortcut:* at 18 mm f/8, focus about two metres in. That covers most wide

*** The rules worth remembering

- Depth of field extends roughly *twice as far behind* the focus point as in front
- Wider aperture → shallower
- Closer focus → shallower, dramatically so
- Longer focal length → shallower-looking, mostly by magnifying background blur
- Diffraction becomes visible past about *f/11* on 24 MP DX, obvious by f/16

*** Common problems

| Photos slightly soft everywhere  | Shutter too slow, or VR on a tripod | Raise shutter; turn VR off on tripod |

*Tip —* The flash indicator flashing for three seconds after a shot is the

*** Sensor cleaning

1. *Setup menu → Clean image sensor → Clean now.* Try this first.
2. Still dusty? *Setup menu → Lock mirror up for cleaning*, then use a hand blower

3. Persistent spots need a proper wet clean. Get it done professionally the first
   time so you can see how it is done.

Dust is easiest to see in a plain bright area at a small aperture. Photograph a
blank sky at f/16 to check.

** J — Glossary

- *Aperture* — the adjustable opening in the lens. Controls light and depth of field.
- *AF-C / AF-S* — continuous and single autofocus modes.
- *AF-area mode* — *where* the camera looks to focus. Separate from focus mode.
- *Back-button focus* — moving autofocus off the shutter button onto a rear button.
- *Blinkies* — the flashing overexposure warning in playback.
- *Bokeh* — the visual quality of out-of-focus areas, not merely their quantity.
- *Bulb* — shutter stays open while the release is held.
- *Chromatic aberration* — colour fringing on high-contrast edges.
- *Clipping* — pixels pushed to pure white or pure black, losing all detail.
- *Commander mode* — using the built-in flash to wirelessly control off-camera flashes.
- *Crop factor* — 1.5× on DX. Multiply focal length to get the full-frame equivalent.
- *Depth of field* — the zone, front to back, that is acceptably sharp.
- *Diffraction* — softening caused by very small apertures. Visible past ~f/11 here.
- *DX* — Nikon's APS-C sensor format. Your camera.
- *Dynamic range* — the span from darkest to brightest a sensor can record at once.
- *ETTR* — "expose to the right." Brightening to just short of clipping.
- *EV* — exposure value. One EV is one stop.
- *EXIF* — the settings and metadata embedded in every file.
- *Fill flash* — a small amount of flash to open shadows, not to be the main light.
- *Guide number* — a measure of flash power.
- *Histogram* — a graph of brightness across the image.
- *Hyperfocal distance* — the focus distance giving maximum depth of field to infinity.
- *i-TTL* — Nikon's automatic flash metering system.
- *ISO* — how strongly the sensor signal is amplified. Higher means brighter and
noisier.
- *ISO-invariant* — a sensor where brightening later ≈ raising ISO at capture.
Yours nearly is.
- *Live view* — using the rear screen as a real-time, exposure-accurate preview.
- *NEF* — Nikon's raw file format.
- *Raw* — unprocessed sensor data. Maximum editing latitude.
- *Reciprocal rule* — hand-hold at roughly 1/(equivalent focal length) or faster.
- *Stop* — a doubling or halving of light. The universal unit of exposure.
- *Sync speed* — the fastest shutter usable with flash. 1/250 s here.
- *VR* — Vibration Reduction. Corrects camera shake only, never subject movement.
- *White balance* — telling the camera what colour of light it is looking at.
