How to read this document

This is a primer, not an argument. It does not tell you where to live, does not rank communities against each other, and does not push a thesis about housing policy. It describes what is actually there: how many frum families, which mosdos, which kollelim, what housing costs, what people do for a living, and what the local pressures are.

On hashkafa. Communities here are described by what they contain, not by how close they sit to some assumed center. Yeshivish, chassidish, Sephardi, Modern Orthodox, Chabad, and mixed communities are all treated the same way. Where a community is dominated by one tzibbur, that is stated as a fact about the community, not as a compliment or a complaint.

On confidence. Numbers in this field rot fast and are collected badly. Three markers are used:

Marker Meaning
(v) Verified against a named source, with the date it reflects
(est.) My estimate from indirect evidence — treat as a ballpark, not a figure
(comp.) Computed — a verified rate applied to a figure stated in the same place
(self-rep.) Reported by the community itself, usually in relocation marketing. Directionally useful, systematically optimistic

Where a claim carries no marker, it is a general structural statement rather than a measurement.

On layout. Parts 1–3 are cross-cutting: what makes a community work, how kollel functions as an institution, and how people earn — §3.5 on tuition and §3.6 on property and income tax are the two that decide most of the cost difference between communities. Part 4 profiles the US by region, Part 5 covers Eretz Yisroel, and the Addendum covers Canada, the UK, Europe, Australia, South Africa, and Latin America. Parts 6–7 are the comparison tables and a checklist for evaluating a community yourself. Appendix A is the full Ichud kollel directory, Appendix D the shul and community-contact listings.

If you read only three things. §3.5 (tuition — the largest cost difference between communities, and a function of state law rather than of schools), §3.6 (property and income tax — the second tuition bill, and the one no brochure mentions), and §6.1 (what housing actually costs, by segment). For Eretz Yisroel, §5.8 on the draft law and the funding cuts, because every number in §5.7 currently depends on it.

On what’s missing. The two best structured datasets available — the OU’s Savitsky community directory and the Ichud HaKollelim kollel registry — both have a coverage bias. The OU directory is built from communities that want newcomers, so it over-represents small and growing places and under-represents Lakewood, Brooklyn, Monsey, and the chassidishe centers, which have no relocation-marketing problem. The Ichud registry covers BMG-affiliated kollelim and therefore misses Ner Yisroel, Chofetz Chaim/RSA, YU, Chabad, Sephardi, and independent kollelim entirely, along with the entire Israeli kollel world. Both gaps are filled here from other sources, but the sections built on those datasets are denser than the sections that aren’t, and that reflects the data, not the communities.

Part 1 — The shape of the map

1.1 What actually makes a place livable

A frum community is an infrastructure stack. Each layer has a minimum viable size, and the layers fail in a predictable order as a community shrinks.

Tier 1 — Non-negotiable. A daily minyan (three tefillos, not just Shabbos). A mikvah in usable proximity — ideally within an eruv, otherwise a short drive. Kosher food that does not require a two-hour round trip. A chevra kadisha or a working arrangement with one.

Tier 2 — Required for raising children. Boys’ elementary and girls’ elementary within a reasonable bus ride. A mesivta and a Bais Yaakov / girls’ high school, or an accepted dormitory pipeline out of town. This is the single most common reason families leave small communities: the community can carry them through eighth grade and then can’t.

Tier 3 — Required for a community to sustain itself. An eruv. A kollel or beis medrash with real learning during the day. Hatzolah or an equivalent. Bikur cholim. Tomchei Shabbos. A vaad hakashrus or a rav whose hashgacha is accepted. A shidduch network that doesn’t require every date to start with a flight.

Tier 4 — Quality of life. Restaurants. Multiple shuls, so that a machlokes or a bad fit doesn’t mean leaving town. Camps. Seminary and post-high-school options. Simcha halls. Senior care and a kosher nursing facility.

A community with Tiers 1–2 and a thin Tier 3 is a real community that will feel small. A community with Tier 1 only is a place frum people live, not a place a frum family builds.

1.2 “In-town” and “out-of-town” — what the words actually mean

The terms are geographic in form and economic in substance. “In-town” means the New York–New Jersey corridor plus Lakewood, and by extension Baltimore, Monsey, and the Five Towns. Everything else is “out-of-town.”

What the distinction actually encodes:

  In-town Out-of-town
Housing Scarce, expensive, appreciating fast Cheaper per square foot, more land, slower appreciation
Mosdos Deep choice; multiple options per grade per hashkafa One or two options; fit is not guaranteed
Tuition High sticker, limited state aid in NY/NJ Often dramatically lower, frequently voucher- or ESA-subsidized
Parnassa Dense frum business ecosystem; short job search Thinner frum ecosystem; general economy matters more
Anonymity High. You can be a private person Low. Everyone knows, and you will be asked to do things
Communal load Someone else is running it You are running it, or it doesn’t happen
Shidduchim Local pool You are flying, or your children are boarding

The last two rows are the ones people underweight when they move, and the ones that decide whether the move holds.

1.3 The frum household cost structure

Any comparison of communities that only compares home prices is misleading, and most of them do exactly that. Four things dominate, and the sticker price of the house is only one.

A note on why most published price figures are useless here. For any given community you can find three “median home prices” that differ by a factor of two, because they measure different things:

  • Median listing or sale price (Redfin, Realtor) includes condos, co-ops, small starter homes, and the non-frum parts of the ZIP code. Lakewood’s August 2026 median listing price was $379K (v) while new 5-bedroom construction there was selling at $1.1–1.2 million (v). Both numbers are true. Only one describes what a frum family buys.
  • Zillow’s typical home value (ZHVI) smooths the whole housing stock, so it sits above the listing median and below the family-home price. Lakewood’s was $733,729 in October 2025 (v).
  • Price per square foot strips out the size difference — but only if it is measured on the same property type. A neighborhood-wide $/ft² blends condos, co-ops and two-to-four-family buildings in with houses, and frum families buy houses.
  • In a condo-heavy neighborhood the two published figures are wrong in opposite directions. The all-types median price understates houses, because it is full of apartments. The all-types $/ft² overstates the house rate, because small units cost more per foot. Monsey is the proof: the published all-types rate is $580/ft², but actual Monsey houses run 4,700–5,000 ft² and sell around $1,019,957 on average (v) — roughly $215/ft². The published rate is nearly triple the real one for houses.
  • Therefore: never divide a median price by a median $/ft² to infer a house size. Those are medians of two different distributions, both contaminated, and the quotient is meaningless. An earlier draft of this document did exactly that and concluded Monsey’s typical house was 1,720 ft². It is closer to 5,000.
  • Get the house range instead. Monsey houses: $475,000–$2,999,988 (v). Kew Gardens Hills houses: $750,000–$2,999,000 (v), against an all-types median of $350–505K that is measuring the co-op stock. Boca Raton: all-types $675–685K, houses $1,399,000 (v). The range for the right property type beats any single blended number.

And always ask what share of the stock is even houses. Detached single-family homes are 34.46% of Lakewood’s housing units (v) — the rest is townhouses, duplexes, apartments and 3,500-plus units of 55-plus senior housing, including Leisure Village’s 2,433 attached homes at a $182,168 median (v). A “Lakewood median” of $399–429K is a fact about that two-thirds, not about the house a frum family buys, which is $750–950K existing and $1.1–1.2M newly built (v). This is why §6.1 lists as many housing segments as a given community actually has, rather than one number for all of them.

So: always ask how big. A $400,000 house in South Bend is roughly 3,000 square feet at $133/ft². A $400,000 house in West Rogers Park is roughly 2,000 square feet at $200/ft². A $400,000 house in Monsey is about 690 square feet at $580/ft², which is to say it is an apartment. Same number, three completely different lives.

The four cost drivers, in order of how much they actually vary:

1. Tuition — but not the way sticker prices suggest. Usually the largest single line, and it varies by state school-choice law rather than by the schools themselves. Do not multiply a per-child band by your number of children. Sibling discounts are standard, and many schools cap total family tuition at 12–18% of AGI regardless of how many children attend (v); the voucher states pay per child instead, and Indiana does not means-test at all (v). The upshot is that the community-to-community tuition gap is modest at a kollel or single-earner income and large at a professional one — see §3.5 and the two-income worked example in §3.6.

2. Property tax. Routinely $10,000–$20,000 a year in the frum communities of New Jersey and New York, and it never stops — you pay it after the mortgage is gone. It is close to a second tuition bill, it is almost never mentioned in relocation marketing, and it does not track house prices. Cleveland Heights has some of the cheapest frum housing in America and an effective rate around 3.00% (v), among the highest anywhere in the country. See §3.6.

3. State income tax. Between zero and 13.3% of everything you earn, depending on which side of a state line you live on (v). Nine states levy none. Texas and Florida trade it away for higher property tax; California and New Jersey charge both.

4. The housing proximity premium. Frum families cannot arbitrage distance. You must be inside the eruv and within walking distance of a shul, which means you are bidding against every other frum family for the same few streets. This is why frum-neighborhood prices decouple upward from their metro’s median, and why a metro-level figure understates what you will actually pay.

Then the recurring items that don’t vary as much but aren’t small:

  • Food. Kosher meat, chalav Yisroel, and Pesach add a recurring premium. Communities where kosher is stocked in the mainstream supermarket (Kroger, Costco, Trader Joe’s) are meaningfully cheaper than communities where everything ships in.
  • Yom tov and simchos. Non-negotiable, poorly forecast, and larger in communities with strong social expectations.
  • The Israel year and seminary. For most families, one to two years per child at roughly $25,000–$35,000 (est.), typically hitting when tuition is also at peak.
  • Health insurance, if you are not covered through an employer — a large American number that the kollel conversation routinely omits.

The way to actually compare two communities is to price the same family in both: purchase price of a house you would genuinely live in at the size you need, plus annual property tax on it, plus state income tax on your expected income, plus post-subsidy tuition for your actual number of children. Section 6.1 gives the inputs. Section 3.6 works an example.

1.4 Why frum housing demand never loosens

Growth is endogenous. Frum communities grow primarily from births, not from moves. Lakewood’s birth rate is among the highest of any American municipality, and Kiryas Joel’s population roughly doubled in a decade — from 33,507 in the 2020 census to about 43,863 by 2024 (v, US Census), with growth continuing at high single digits annually. This means demand for housing and school seats is baked in years ahead of supply, everywhere, permanently.

Turnover is low. Frum families with four to eight children do not downsize and move every seven years like the general market. Housing stock in frum neighborhoods therefore trades far less often than the surrounding metro, which tightens supply beyond what population figures alone suggest.

Part 2 — Kollel

2.1 The categories

“Kollel” covers several genuinely different institutions that happen to share a name. Conflating them produces bad decisions.

The learning kollel. A kollel whose purpose is Torah study, full stop. Beth Medrash Govoha in Lakewood is the archetype and by far the largest — roughly 7,000–10,000 talmidim depending on how the count is drawn (v; published figures vary by source and by whether the mesivta and beis medrash divisions are included), making it the largest yeshiva outside Eretz Yisroel. Similar institutions exist at Ner Yisroel, Philadelphia, Chaim Berlin, Torah Vodaath, the Mir’s American branch, and dozens of smaller batei medrash. The yungerman’s obligation is to learn; there is no communal job attached.

The community kollel. A kollel planted in a community that has frum families but not enough Torah infrastructure. The yungerleit learn a full seder, and in exchange for the stipend they carry a communal load: night chavrusas with baalei batim, shiurim, a morning kollel for retirees, learning programs for boys, sometimes teaching in the local school. This is a job, and the community is a stakeholder. It is the model the rest of this section is mostly about.

The kollel horaah / dayanus kollel. Semicha and beyond — yoreh yoreh, yadin yadin, choshen mishpat. Aimed at producing rabbanim and dayanim. Detroit’s Kollel Hora’ah Bais Yehuda, the Choshen Mishpat kollelim in Detroit and Passaic, and several of Baltimore’s kollelim work in this space. Length is measured in years, and the exit is a specific job.

The kiruv kollel. Outreach-first. The learning is real, but the mandate is to reach Jews who are not yet frum. The Chofetz Chaim / Rabbinical Seminary of America network, Torah Links (Lakewood-affiliated), Atlanta Scholars Kollel, Tampa, Orlando, and the Jewish Study Network in Palo Alto operate in this space. Different skill set, different personality fit, different metrics.

The night kollel / baalebatishe kollel. For men who work. Usually a small stipend or none.

The retiree kollel. A growing category in relocation-oriented communities — South Bend, San Antonio, Dunwoody, Silver Spring, Portland, Providence, and Oak Park all list one. Not a parnassa; a reason for older people to move.

2.2 Ichud HaKollelim — the network, and what it tells you

Ichud HaKollelim is an arm of Beth Medrash Govoha. It became a formal entity in 2022 after fourteen years as BMG’s Community Development Office; the model traces to the first community kollel, founded in Toronto in 1970. It reports 125 kollelim and over 1,500 yungerleit (v, ichudhakollelim.org), with offices in both the US and Eretz Yisroel. It does three things: helps communities establish and run kollelim, supports kollel leadership and community integration, and matches yungerleit to placements — including relocation and acclimation help.

For anyone actually weighing a kollel position, this is the front door.

Its published directory lists 112 kollelim across 66 cities. The full list is in Appendix A. Four things it shows:

  1. Concentration. Chicago and Los Angeles each host seven listed kollelim; Baltimore, Cleveland, and Detroit six each. These are not “out-of-town communities that have a kollel” — they are Torah centers with internal variety, including chassidishe and Sephardi kollelim. Chicago has both, plus the Veitzener Kollel.
  2. Reach. Savannah, Tucson, San Antonio, Norfolk, Rochester, Jacksonville, Indianapolis, and South Bend each carry one. A few dozen frum families can support a kollel, because the economics are a handful of stipends against a donor base, not a market.
  3. The in-town listings are thin by design. Flatbush has one listing, Monsey three, Passaic three. That is a fact about what Ichud tracks, not about how much learning happens in those places.
  4. Directory quirks, so you don’t misread it: “Kollel Zichron Aryeh Leib – The Manhattan Beach Community Kollel” is filed under Manhattan but sits in Brooklyn; “Kollel Saint-Louis” is in France, not Missouri (St. Louis, MO has its own separate listing); “Blue Ridge Kollel” is filed under Waterbury. The taxonomy is loose.

2.3 The other networks

The OU’s community directory classifies each community’s local kollel by network, which is a useful proxy for a community’s center of gravity. Its categories: Lakewood, Ner Yisroel, Chofetz Chaim, YU, Torah MiTzion, and unaffiliated.

Reading across roughly sixty US community profiles:

  • Lakewood-affiliated is the most common single label, and it appears in communities across the whole spectrum — including places whose shuls are Modern Orthodox. Denver, Boston, Pittsburgh, Houston, Southfield, Scranton, Northeast Philadelphia, South Bend, Bensalem, Orlando, Chesterfield, Oak Park, and Highland Park all list it.
  • Ner Yisroel in Atlanta, Columbus, Cincinnati, Indianapolis, Dunwoody, Lincolnwood.
  • Chofetz Chaim / RSA in Tampa, Cherry Hill, Rochester, Manalapan, Buffalo Grove, and behind Orlando Torah Academy and St. Louis’s boys’ yeshiva.
  • YU / Torah MiTzion in Northbrook, Buffalo Grove, Lincolnwood, Silver Spring.
  • Unaffiliated is common and does not mean weak: Memphis, Elizabeth, West Orange, Livingston, Providence, Norfolk, Richmond, Portland, San Antonio, San Diego, and Jacksonville all fall here.

Several communities list multiple networks. Lincolnwood lists Lakewood, Ner Yisroel, Torah MiTzion, YU, and unaffiliated simultaneously; Cherry Hill lists Chofetz Chaim and Lakewood; Manalapan lists Chofetz Chaim, Lakewood, and Ner Yisroel. That is a direct signal about how wide a community’s tent actually is.

Not covered by either directory: Chabad’s kollel network, the Israeli kollel world (enormous — Part 5), most Sephardi kollelim, and the many kollelim attached to individual yeshivos.

2.4 The money

Nobody publishes this cleanly, so treat everything here as a range with wide error bars.

US community kollel stipends run from a few hundred dollars a month at the low end to roughly $1,500–$2,500/month at established, well-funded kollelim (est.; synthesized from scattered community reporting — no authoritative published survey exists). Older published references cite figures as low as $320/month; aspirational benchmarks in the same discussions cite $400/week. The spread is real and it tracks the kollel’s donor base, not its Torah.

What the stipend is for matters more than its size. The standard framing in the community-kollel world is that the stipend plus low out-of-town cost of living lets a family live on one-and-a-fraction incomes — the wife working, often in chinuch or a profession, with the stipend covering the gap and buying her a lower-pressure job than she’d otherwise need. It is rarely designed as a sole family income.

So price the package, not the stipend:

  • Tuition remission at the local mosdos — frequently worth more than the stipend itself in a high-tuition state
  • Housing — assistance, a below-market rental, or in some communities a purchase subsidy
  • Health insurance — a large US number that gets skipped in these conversations
  • Moving costs
  • Term — two years? five? open-ended? What happens at the end, and who decides?
  • Employer — the kollel or the shul? Who can end it, and on what notice?
  • The wife’s realistic local earning power, which is a fact about the local economy, not about her résumé

Relocation incentives are now a real category:

  • Providence, RI — $50,000 plus a year of free tuition and a $1,000 move-in stipend, for a small number of families (v, reported 2023)
  • New Orleans — the “Minyan Project”: housing or rental subsidy, moving allowance, low-cost day school tuition, free communal memberships (self-rep.)
  • Greater MetroWest NJ — an $1,800 “Smart Move” grant plus Tuition Max, capping day school tuition at 18% of AGI for families earning roughly $150K–$300K (v)
  • Albany, Rochester, Atlanta, Boston, Scranton, West Hartford, Lincolnwood, Livingston, Richmond, East Brunswick, Plano, Dayton and others list incentives in the OU directory (self-rep.)

2.5 What comes after

Most community-kollel yungerleit do not stay in kollel indefinitely. The standard exits:

  • Chinuch — rebbe, morah, menahel. Frequently the reason the kollel was placed where it was in the first place.
  • Rabbanus — the local shul or one elsewhere, usually after semicha completed during the kollel years.
  • Klei kodesh adjacent — kashrus and hashgacha, mikvah, chevra kadisha, sofrus, communal administration, mosdos development.
  • Out into the workforce — the largest category by count, and the one the system prepares people for least well. Which is what Part 3 is about.

Part 3 — Parnassa

Housing is a one-time decision. Parnassa is the thing that determines whether the decision holds. This part covers what frum people actually do for a living, where those jobs are, and the one variable — tuition subsidy — that swamps salary differences between communities.

3.1 Klei kodesh

The chinuch and rabbanus track is the largest employer of yeshiva-educated men in the frum world, and the compensation picture is sobering but not uniform.

Rebbeim. Starting salaries at stable, on-time-paying yeshivos in the New York area run around $28,000 for a half-day position, with experienced rebbeim around $50,000 after roughly ten years (v, community reporting). National averages for “yeshiva” positions sit around $42,000, typically ranging $35,000–$45,000 (v, ZipRecruiter, Jan 2026) — a figure that blends roles and should be read loosely.

The out-of-town advantage is real and underappreciated. Out-of-town schools frequently structure full-day positions — secular studies in the morning, limudei kodesh in the afternoon — which can roughly double a rebbe’s income relative to a half-day in-town position. Out-of-town schools also tend to pay more for degrees and certification. This inverts the usual in-town/out-of-town intuition: for a rebbe specifically, out-of-town often pays better, not worse, and the cost of living is lower on top of it.

Moros are paid materially less than rebbeim in most schools (v, widely reported; the gap is a known and openly discussed feature of the field).

Adjacent klei kodesh roles with real demand: kashrus supervision and hashgacha administration, mikvah operation, sofrus and STa"M, chevra kadisha, mosdos development and fundraising (which pays better than teaching and is chronically short of good people), and school administration.

3.2 Programming, tech, and remote work

This is the fastest-growing parnassa track in the frum world, in both directions — frum people entering tech, and tech being the field that makes out-of-town living work.

Why it fits. Software work is the rare high-income field that is genuinely location-independent, genuinely schedule-flexible, and does not require a degree pipeline that conflicts with a yeshiva education. Remote work removes the specific frictions frum employees otherwise negotiate constantly: leaving early Friday, Yom Tov clusters, kosher lunch logistics, and workplace culture built around other people’s calendars.

Where the jobs actually are. Two separate questions, and people conflate them:

If you are working remotely, the community’s local tech economy is close to irrelevant. What matters is: reliable high-speed internet; a time zone that works with your employer (Mountain and Pacific communities can be an asset or a liability depending on where the company is); and — the one people miss — whether the community already has remote workers. A community where you are the only person davening Mincha at 1:30 on a Tuesday is a lonely one, and it usually also means no local shared-office setup, no local network, and no informal referral chain when your contract ends.

Budget for a longer runway than the bootcamp marketing suggests, and treat the first job as the hard part.

3.3 The other frum-heavy fields

Worth knowing because they’re the fields that already have infrastructure — networks, mentors, and employers who understand a frum schedule.

  • Allied health and therapy — OT, PT, speech, ABA/BCBA, social work, nursing. Very heavily represented, license-portable across states (with paperwork), strong demand in every community listed in this document, and Bensalem, Memphis, Louisville, Rochester and others explicitly cite therapy demand.
  • Healthcare generally — most out-of-town communities are anchored on a hospital system, and it is usually the single largest local employer.
  • Real estate, mortgage, title, insurance — frum-dense, relationship-driven, and one of the standard exits from kollel.
  • Accounting and finance — the CPA path is well-worn and structurally compatible.
  • E-commerce, Amazon FBA, and wholesale — high variance, low barrier, heavily frum, and much less reliable than its reputation.
  • Sales — the highest-variance option, and the one that most often replaces a degree.
  • Trades and services — undersupplied in almost every frum community and quietly lucrative in the growing ones (Lakewood, Toms River, Jackson, Monsey, Beitar, RBS).

3.4 The two-income structure

In the yeshivish and chassidish world, the normal configuration is that the wife’s income is primary or co-primary during the kollel years, and often stays significant afterward. This makes the local labor market for women a first-order community variable, and it is almost never discussed in relocation materials.

Questions worth asking about any community:

  • What do women here actually do? (In small communities the honest answer is often: teach in the local school, or work remotely.)
  • How many teaching positions exist, and are they filled by kollel wives already?
  • Is there a local employer that hires professionally — a hospital system, a school district, a university, a large corporate?
  • Is there a remote-work culture, or would she be the only one?
  • Childcare: does it exist, what does it cost, and does it run on a schedule compatible with a frum calendar?

3.5 Tuition — the variable that dominates everything

Usually the largest financial difference between American frum communities — and a function of state law rather than of the schools.

Read the sticker prices below as list prices, not as what families pay. Three mechanisms sit between the two, and an earlier draft of this document ignored all of them:

  • Sibling discounts are near-universal; one published model runs to a 40% maximum family discount (v).
  • Family caps on a percentage of AGI are spreading: 12% under Chicago’s TAP Fund, 18% under Greater MetroWest’s Tuition MAX, and 15–18% at schools using that model (v) — regardless of how many children attend. One documented case: a family at $150,000 AGI with four children in school paying $13,156 per child, not sticker (v).
  • Vouchers and ESAs pay per child and, in Indiana, without any income test (v).

So a per-child band multiplied by five children overstates the real cost badly — and overstates it most in the expensive communities, which is where the caps and scholarships do the most work.

The high-cost baseline. Average private-school tuition in Brooklyn runs roughly $23,000 (elementary) and $25,000 (high school) (v, PrivateSchoolReview), though yeshivish schools in Brooklyn run far below that — roughly $7,000/year for grades Pre-1A–8, with nursery and kindergarten around $5,000 (v, community reporting). Lakewood averages roughly $5,500–$6,500 per child, with some schools up to $8,500 and a few outliers far higher (v). The Modern Orthodox day school world in the New York metro is where the $25,000–$35,000 per child figures live.

New York State launched a pilot in 2025 offering $15,000 per child per year (capped at $30,000 per family, and at 50% of tuition) to communal professionals with children in one of 54 participating schools in NYC, Long Island, and Westchester, beginning with the 2026–27 school year (v, JTA). UJA-Federation separately caps tuition at $16,350 per K–8 child, or 15% of AGI, for families earning under $350,000 at participating schools (v).

The school-choice states are a different world. As of 2025–26, broad ESA-style or voucher programs operate in Arizona, Arkansas, Florida, Indiana, Iowa, New Hampshire, Ohio, Tennessee, Utah, West Virginia, and Wyoming, with Texas launching universal access in 2026–27 (v, FutureEd / school-choice trackers). Concretely, from community sources:

  • Ohio (EdChoice Expansion) — $6,166 per child K–8 and $8,408 in high school, available statewide, at full value below 450% of the federal poverty level and prorated above it (v, Ohio Dept. of Education, 2026–27). Community materials describing this as “regardless of income” are out of date — it is universal in availability but income-scaled in amount. Schools must accept the voucher as payment in full from households at or below 200% FPL (v). Cincinnati, Columbus, Cleveland and Dayton all sit inside this.
  • Indiana (Choice Scholarship) — income limits were removed entirely for 2026–27: 100% of families are eligible (v). The award is 90% of state per-student funding or actual tuition, whichever is lower — typically $6,100–7,500 per child, averaging $6,536 (v). This is the only genuinely income-blind program among the states here, which makes it the most valuable to a high earner and the most reliable to everyone else. South Bend and Indianapolis both cite it; South Bend’s tuition band is under $10K.
  • Arizona (ESA + STO tax credits) — Phoenix and Scottsdale cite generous day-school scholarships at all income levels (self-rep.). Phoenix’s reported tuition band is under $10K.
  • Pennsylvania (EITC / Private School Tax Credits) — funds school-run scholarships rather than paying families directly. Northeast Philadelphia, Bensalem, Scranton, and Harrisburg all cite it as the reason their tuition is low.
  • Florida — roughly 60% of ESA-participating schools are religious, with recent growth among Jewish providers (v). Jacksonville and Tampa report tuition under $10K.

What the bands actually mean for a family. They are per-child list prices. Multiply one by five children and you get a number almost no family pays. What matters instead:

  • In a voucher or ESA state, the subsidy is per child and mostly income-blind, so a large family captures it five times over. Indiana’s average award is ~$6,536 per child with no income test (v); Ohio’s EdChoice Expansion pays $6,166 (K–8) and $8,408 (9–12), at full value below 450% of the federal poverty level and prorated above it (v).
  • In New York, New Jersey and Maryland, there is no equivalent, so the family is dependent on the school’s scholarship committee or an AGI cap where one exists. That makes the cost income-tested, and therefore unpredictable in a way a voucher is not.

The consequence is that the tuition gap between communities grows with income. At a kollel or single-earner income, scholarship absorbs most of the difference and the gap is perhaps $15,000–25,000 a year. At a two-professional income it can exceed $40,000, because the voucher states keep paying and the scholarship states stop. §3.6 works both cases.

Caveat. These are self-reported bands and they mix school types. Verify against the actual schools you’d use, at your actual family size, with the actual scholarship committee, before treating any of it as a number.

4A — The New York / New Jersey core

This is where the majority of American frum Jews live, and it is the least documented in relocation literature precisely because none of these places are recruiting.

LAKEWOOD, NJ Yeshivish. The largest Torah center in the Western hemisphere.

  • Character: Built around Beth Medrash Govoha, with a very large chassidish minority and dozens of distinct kehillos. Now effectively a city rather than a community — Westgate, Cedarbridge, Somerset, Coventry, Whispering Pines, Chateau and a dozen more sub-neighborhoods, each with its own feel.
  • Size: Orthodox population estimated over 100,000 (est.). Lakewood grew roughly 45–46% between 2010 and 2020 — the highest rate of any New Jersey municipality — and is now the state’s fifth-largest city (v, US Census). Births exceed 5,000 per year (v), among the highest rates anywhere.

Add NYC’s 3.876% resident income tax on top of the 10.9% state rate — a cost the Five Towns and Monsey do not carry.
  • Parnassa: Manhattan commute, strong medical and professional presence.

STATEN ISLAND, NY Willowbrook and the surrounding neighborhoods — mixed, and cheaper than Brooklyn.

  • Character: Willowbrook is the established center, with Young Israel of Staten Island and the Jewish Foundation School; a substantial yeshivish and Sephardi population has grown alongside.
  • Housing: Borough median $755K, +3.4% year-over-year (2026) (v).
  • Parnassa: Manhattan and Brooklyn commute; large healthcare sector.

PASSAIC / CLIFTON, NJ Yeshivish, dense, and one of the strongest mid-size frum communities in America.

  • Character: Predominantly yeshivish with a significant Modern Orthodox presence in Passaic Park and a growing chassidish population. Deep mosdos and a walkable, high-density layout.
  • Kollel: Three in the Ichud directory — the Passaic Clifton Community Kollel, Kollel Choshen Mishpat of Passaic Clifton, and Kollel Birchas Shmuel at Bais Medresh L’Torah. The choshen mishpat kollel makes Passaic one of the few places outside Israel with a serious dayanus track.
  • Housing — the city figure and the frum figure are different markets. Passaic Park, the frum neighborhood, carries a median real estate price of $856,367 and a median sale of $645,000 (v, +24% year-over-year). Passaic city reads $543,000–$555,666 (v) because it blends in the large, much cheaper multi-family stock of a city whose median household income is $55,371 (v). Passaic’s citywide $390/ft² is a higher rate per foot than Teaneck’s $350 (v). Neighboring Clifton is $625,000 at $335/ft² (v). Property tax: Passaic County’s median annual bill is $7,544 at 1.97%; Clifton’s median bill is $9,809 (v).
  • Parnassa: NYC commute (bus/rail), large local professional community, substantial frum business base.

BERGEN COUNTY, NJ — Teaneck, Bergenfield, Fair Lawn, Englewood, Passaic-adjacent Predominantly Modern Orthodox, at large scale.

  • Character: Teaneck is one of the largest and most institutionally dense Modern Orthodox communities in the world — the OU directory lists 20 shuls and 6 schools in the Teaneck area (v) — including Shaarei Orah, a substantial Sephardi kehilla. Bergenfield, New Milford, Fair Lawn, and Englewood extend it.
  • Mosdos: TABC, Ma’ayanot, Frisch, Yavneh, RYNJ, Ben Porat Yosef, and more.
  • Housing. Teaneck is one of the least condo-contaminated markets in this document: 73.78% of its housing units are detached single-family homes, and 61.5% are three- or four-bedroom (v) — so its published figures mean roughly what they appear to mean. Median home cost $722,044, median transaction $679,000, single-family sale median $570,000, at $350/ft² (v). Property tax is the sting: Teaneck’s average bill is $10,940, ranging to $14,700, at a 2.05% effective rate; Bergenfield’s median bill is $10,421 at 3.41%, and Bergen County averages 2.73% (v) — among the highest municipal rates in the country. Stack that on NJ’s 10.75% top income rate and a $20–25K tuition band and Bergen County is the most expensive combined package in this document.
  • Tuition: Among the highest in the country — Fair Lawn reports $20–25K (self-rep.), and NJ has no ESA program.
  • Parnassa: Manhattan commute; very high professional density (law, medicine, finance).

HIGHLAND PARK / EDISON, NJ Genuinely mixed — one of the few large communities where yeshivish and Modern Orthodox families share schools and shuls without friction.

  • Size: Over 1,500 shomer Shabbos families (self-rep.); the OU listing shows 8 shuls, 20 minyanim, 6 schools, 1 kollel.
  • Mosdos: RPRY, Netivot Montessori, Yeshiva Shaarei Tzion, plus high school options locally and nearby.
  • Kollel: Highland Park Community Kollel (Ichud, Lakewood-affiliated).
  • Housing: The self-reported $400–599K band understates the family-home market. Single-family homes sold in Highland Park over the past year averaged $649,579, with an August 2026 median listing price of $589K (v); Zillow’s typical value of $494,826 and Redfin’s $500K median both reflect the borough’s condo and smaller-home stock (v). Rentals $1,500–2,000 (self-rep.). The recurring cost is the tax — Edison’s median annual property tax bill is $9,568, typically ranging $7,150–$13,650 (v), and Highland Park’s rate is comparable.
  • Parnassa: Rutgers, Johnson & Johnson, Bristol-Myers Squibb, RWJ and Saint Peter’s hospitals; NYC rail commute.
  • Character note: The community explicitly markets itself on “middos over materialism” and low-key living. That is a real cultural signal, and it is the main reason people choose it over Bergen County.

ELIZABETH & HILLSIDE, NJ Elmora / Elmora Hills. Compact, established, institutionally strong for its size.

  • Mosdos: Anchored on the Jewish Educational Center (JEC) — coed lower school, then separate JEC boys’ and Bruriah girls’ middle and high schools. Bruriah sits in Hillside.
  • Kollel: Two listed (unaffiliated). Hillside lists two as well.
  • Housing: Elizabeth $600–799K; Hillside notably cheaper at $200–399K (self-rep.).
  • Tuition: Elizabeth $15–20K; Hillside $11–15K (self-rep.).
  • Parnassa: Direct transit to Manhattan, Brooklyn, and NJ employment centers.
  • Position: Short drive to Lakewood, Passaic, and Teaneck — Elizabeth’s main structural advantage.

TOMS RIVER, JACKSON, HOWELL, MANCHESTER, BRICK, NJ The Lakewood overflow belt. Growing faster than almost anywhere in America.

  • Toms River (North Dover): Over 2,000 Orthodox families have moved in over the past few years (v). Predominantly yeshivish and chassidish. The township’s first mesivta — a Belzer mosad for ages 14–17 — received unanimous zoning approval (v). Median home price $543K, +8.5% year-over-year (v, Redfin).
  • Jackson: Several thousand frum families (v). Agudath Israel settled a seven-year land-use lawsuit against the township; Jackson’s first yeshiva with a dormitory was approved in 2023 (v).
  • Character: These are new communities in the literal sense — infrastructure is being built now, zoning fights are live and ongoing, and the relationship with long-time residents is contentious in a way that shows up in local politics.
  • Who it’s for: Families who want Lakewood’s ecosystem at a lower price and are willing to live somewhere still under construction.

BAYONNE, NJ Small, deliberate, and being built.

  • Character: Explicitly recruiting — “ground floor” positioning. Lakewood-style kollel with daily minyanim; diverse hashkafa; walkable town.
  • Mosdos: Local options thin; schools are in Elizabeth (JEC, Bruriah, 15 min) and Passaic (20 min).
  • Housing: Affordable by NJ metro standards, with substantial rental stock (self-rep.).
  • Parnassa: 20 minutes to NYC.

CHERRY HILL, NJ South Jersey. Mixed, growing, and structurally underrated.

  • Character: The West Side is the frum center. Genuinely mixed — the OU listing shows Ashkenaz and Sefard nusach, a Young Israel, a Torah Links branch, and a Chabad, with Chofetz Chaim and Lakewood kollel affiliations (v). That combination in one small community is unusual.
  • Mosdos: Politz Day School (elementary and middle) locally; Philadelphia yeshivos and day schools a short commute away. The OU listing counts 10 schools in reach and 4 shuls, 2 mikvaos, 1 kollel locally.
  • Housing: $450,000–$600,000 typical (self-rep., 2026); rentals $2,000–2,500.
  • Tuition: $11–15K band (self-rep.) — and Politz benefits from Pennsylvania-adjacent options plus NJ’s absence of a voucher program is a real constraint here.
  • Position: About an hour to Lakewood, 90 minutes to New York, under two hours to Baltimore. Multiple weekly food deliveries from Lakewood establishments (self-rep.).
  • Parnassa: Philadelphia’s hospitals, universities, and professional sector; Center City is a straightforward commute.

WATERBURY, CT Yeshiva-first. A community that exists because a yeshiva was put there.

  • Character: Built around Yeshiva Ateres Shmuel of Waterbury and its associated mosdos, with a strong baal teshuva and “second-chance” reputation in the mesivta world. Yeshivish.
  • Kollel: Kollel Avreichim of Waterbury, plus the Blue Ridge Kollel listing.
  • Housing: Cheap by Northeast standards (est.).
  • Note: Its identity is tied to the yeshiva’s; evaluate the yeshiva, not just the town.

MANALAPAN, EAST BRUNSWICK, WEST ORANGE, LIVINGSTON, FLEETWOOD (Westchester), NJ/NY Mid-size suburban communities, mostly Modern Orthodox, mostly commuter.

  • Manalapan — Surprisingly deep for its size: 5 shuls, 11 schools in reach, 1 kollel, listing Chofetz Chaim, Lakewood, and Ner Yisroel affiliations. Housing $400–599K, tuition $11–15K (self-rep.). 20–30 min to the Jersey Shore and Deal’s Syrian community.
  • East Brunswick — Young Israel of East Brunswick, ~200 families, one-shul community. Housing $600–799K, tuition $15–20K (self-rep.). Rutgers, Princeton, J&J, BMS nearby.
  • West Orange — AABJ&D and Ohr Torah anchor it, with a growing Sephardi kehilla. Kushner/JEC schools. Housing $400–599K (self-rep.).
  • Livingston — Suburban Torah and Etz Chaim; strong special-needs infrastructure (Friendship Circle, LifeTown). Housing $600–799K (self-rep.).
  • Fleetwood, NY — Small Westchester community, 30 minutes to Grand Central by Metro-North, at a fraction of typical Westchester cost. Mikvaos and kosher food in Riverdale/New Rochelle, 10–15 min. Housing $600–799K (self-rep.).

4B — The Mid-Atlantic

BALTIMORE, MD Yeshivish at scale, with real internal variety. One of the three or four most important Torah centers in America.

  • Character: Centered on Park Heights, Greenspring, Cheswolde, and Pikesville. Predominantly yeshivish, with substantial Modern Orthodox, chassidish, and Sephardi communities and a famously wide range of shuls. Baltimore’s self-image as a place where different types of frum Jews coexist without ideological warfare is largely earned.
  • Mosdos: Ner Yisroel (Yeshivas Ner Yisroel) anchors the town and seeds kollelim nationally. Plus Talmudical Academy, Torah Institute, Bais Yaakov of Baltimore, Bnos Yisroel, Ohr Chadash, Beth Tfiloh, and many more.
  • Kollel: Six in the Ichud directory alone — Baltimore Community Kollel, Kollel Avreichim of Baltimore, Kollel D’Baltimore, Kollel Nachlas Yosef, Kollel Zichron Yehoshua Heschel, Kollel of Greenspring — plus Ner Yisroel’s own kollel and others outside that registry. One of the deepest kollel ecosystems in the country.
  • Housing: Baltimore city median $285,000, +11.8% year-over-year; Baltimore–Columbia–Towson metro $380,000 (Oct 2025) (v). Frum neighborhoods run above both — Greenspring and upper Park Heights meaningfully so (est.).
  • Tuition: Mid-range. Maryland’s BOOST program is small relative to Ohio or Indiana; Baltimore’s affordability comes from scale and low housing cost, not from state subsidy (est.).
  • Parnassa: Federal, defense, and cyber (the Fort Meade / NSA corridor), Johns Hopkins and the medical sector, plus a very large internal frum business economy. DC commute possible.
  • Why it matters: Baltimore is the standard American answer to “I want a big yeshivish community and I cannot afford Lakewood or Brooklyn.”

SILVER SPRING / KEMP MILL, MD (Greater Washington) Mixed, professional, and expensive.

  • Character: Kemp Mill and White Oak. Silver Spring Jewish Center and Young Israel Shomrai Emunah anchor it. The community’s self-description emphasizes range of background and openness; the OU listing counts 7 shuls, 2 mikvaos, 8 schools, 1 kollel.
  • Kollel: Greater Washington Community Kollel — listed as Torah MiTzion and unaffiliated, which correctly signals a different flavor from a Lakewood-model kollel.
  • Housing: $600–799K band, rentals $2,000–2,500 (self-rep.).
  • Tuition: $20–25K (self-rep.) — among the highest bands in this document.
  • Parnassa: Federal government, contractors, NIH, law, policy. Extremely high professional density and correspondingly high salaries, which is the trade for the tuition.

PHILADELPHIA, PA Three distinct frum geographies, and one of the best tuition situations in the country.

  • Northeast Philadelphia — Yeshivish and growing hard. Over 50 families in recent years (self-rep.). The OU listing counts 6 shuls, 3 mikvaos, 15 schools in reach, 1 kollel, Lakewood-affiliated. Housing — the community’s own figures are rent a 2–3 bedroom home for $1,500–2,000/month; buy for $350K with about $3,000/year in property taxes (self-rep.). The rent holds. The purchase price does not describe a house a growing family buys. Bustleton and Somerton run $389,500–444,000 all-types median at $267/ft² (v, 2026), and that median is itself carried by the rowhouse and twin stock; detached four-bedrooms on the frum blocks list at $500,000–550,000 (v, Aug 2026). The $3,000 tax bill is real but is a lag rather than a rate — Philadelphia charges 1.3998% on assessed value less a $100,000 homestead exemption (v), so $3,000 implies an assessment near $314,000 against a market price well above it, and the citywide revaluation lands for tax year 2027 (v). Carrying costs here are still genuinely low for the Northeast corridor. They are not $350K-and-$3,000 low. 90 minutes to NYC, just over an hour to Lakewood, 2 hours to Baltimore; Trenton station gives a 50-minute train to Penn Station.
  • Overbrook Park / Lower Merion / Bala Cynwyd — Older established community, mixed Modern Orthodox and yeshivish, home to Yeshivas Philadelphia (Philadelphia Yeshiva), one of the historically most influential American yeshivos.
  • Somerton — Smaller, its own kollel.
  • Kollel: Three in the Ichud directory: Philadelphia Community Kollel, Northeast Community Kollel, Somerton Community Kollel.
  • Tuition: Pennsylvania’s EITC / Private School Tax Credit program funds large school-run scholarships. Northeast Philadelphia describes tuition as “astonishing” in the good direction (self-rep.) — this is the single biggest reason the region is growing.
  • Parnassa: Philadelphia’s universities, hospitals, and Fortune 500 base; the community runs a resume-matching service (self-rep.).

BENSALEM, PA (Bucks County) Small, warm, and cheap. A satellite done deliberately.

  • Character: Under Rabbi and Mrs. Moshe Travitsky. One shul, one kollel (Lakewood-affiliated), mikvah, eruv, preschool, and an active outreach organization. Schools, kosher shopping, and dining are a 15–20 minute drive (Northeast Philadelphia).
  • Housing: Homes from $350K, apartments within the eruv (self-rep.) — that is the entry rung; the township’s all-types median is $409,777–450,000 (v, 2026) against a Bucks County median of $379,000 (v). The community reports Bucks County taxes lower than surrounding areas (self-rep.); the median Bensalem bill is $4,977 (v), which beats Lakewood and Passaic comfortably but is roughly what a Northeast Philadelphia detached house will cost once its assessment catches up. What Bensalem actually buys you is the missing 3.74% wage tax, not a cheaper house.
  • Tuition: Under $10K band, via PA EITC (self-rep.).
  • Position: 25 minutes to Northeast Philadelphia, under an hour to Lakewood, under 90 minutes to Brooklyn.
  • Parnassa: Broad professional market; the community runs a local Jewish job network of business owners and reports heavy demand for therapists (self-rep.).

SCRANTON, PA Yeshivish, institutionally dense for its size, and inexpensive.

  • Mosdos: 3 shuls, 2 elementary schools, 3 girls’ high schools, a yeshiva, and a kollel (v, self-rep.) — an unusually complete stack for a town this size, driven by the yeshiva’s dormitory population.
  • Kollel: Scranton Community Kollel, Lakewood-affiliated.
  • Housing: Spacious homes in the Hill Section around $400K; the OU band is $200–399K, rentals $800–1,500 (self-rep.).
  • Tuition: $11–15K band, with PA EITC (self-rep.).
  • Position: Two hours to NY/NJ and Philadelphia; near the Poconos.

HARRISBURG, PA Small, Modern Orthodox, very affordable.

  • Mosdos: Kesher Israel (daily minyanim, mikveh, eruv); The Silver Academy, PreK–8, sharing a new campus with the JCC.
  • Housing: $200–399K, rentals $800–1,500 (self-rep.).
  • Tuition: Under $10K (self-rep.).
  • Limits: No local high school. Plan for that.

PITTSBURGH, PA Squirrel Hill and Greenfield. Urban, walkable, genuinely mixed, and a real tech city.

  • Character: Over 80 years of continuous Jewish life in Squirrel Hill. Full spectrum — yeshivish, Modern Orthodox, Chabad, Sephardi. The OU listing counts 6 shuls, 2 schools, 1 kollel.
  • Kollel: Kollel Jewish Learning Center (Beacon Street), Lakewood-affiliated.
  • Housing: $400–599K band, rentals $1,500–2,000 (self-rep.).
  • Tuition: $11–15K, with PA EITC (self-rep.).
  • Housing — and note which Squirrel Hill. The frum community lives mainly in Squirrel Hill South and Greenfield, not Squirrel Hill North. Squirrel Hill South’s median single-family sale is $525,000 at $333/ft²; Squirrel Hill North’s is $835,000 at $341/ft² (v, 2026) — the North figure is an affluent sub-neighborhood and should not be read as the community’s price. Allegheny County assesses at a common level ratio of about 50%, so the 2.22%-of-assessed rate works out near 1.1% of market value (v).
  • Parnassa: Carnegie Mellon, University of Pittsburgh, world-class hospital systems, and over 1,800 tech companies employing 86,000+ people as of 2024 (v) — Google, BNY Mellon, PNC. One of the best programming markets on this list relative to cost of living.

WHITE OAK, PA 20 minutes from Pittsburgh. Very small, very cheap.

  • Housing: Homes from about $180,000 (self-rep.); rentals under $800 — the lowest band in the entire OU directory.
  • Mosdos: One shul, one kollel, two Orthodox day schools in Pittsburgh with free door-to-door bus service (self-rep.).
  • Tuition: Under $10K (self-rep.).
  • Who it’s for: Someone who wants Pittsburgh’s job market at a fraction of Pittsburgh’s housing cost, and is comfortable in a very small kehilla.

NORFOLK / VIRGINIA BEACH, VA Small, walkable, and near a Navy-and-research economy.

  • Character: The frum community lives mostly in Norfolk’s Ghent neighborhood — walkable, urban, near downtown and major hospitals including a well-regarded children’s hospital. 3 shuls, 1 mikvah, 4 schools, 1 kollel (v).
  • Kollel: The Norfolk Kollel (unaffiliated).
  • Housing: Homes within the eruv in the $400–600Ks (self-rep.).
  • Tuition: $11–15K (self-rep.).
  • Parnassa: The world’s largest naval base, NASA Langley, universities, and hospitals.

RICHMOND, VA Small and growing, with a kollel-style program.

  • Mosdos: Multiple minyanim, an active Va’ad Hakashrus, a new mikvah, a large eruv. 3 shuls, 2 mikvaos, 5 schools, 1 kollel (v).
  • Housing: $400–599K (self-rep.); tuition $11–15K.
  • Position: Close to DC, Baltimore, and NY by East Coast standards.

4C — The Midwest

The Midwest is where the out-of-town model works best: real cities with real economies, deep frum infrastructure built over generations, cheap housing, and — in Ohio and Indiana especially — the best tuition situation in the country.

CHICAGO, IL West Rogers Park, Peterson Park, Lincolnwood, Skokie, Northbrook, Buffalo Grove. A major Torah center with genuine internal variety.

  • Character: The West Rogers Park / Lincolnwood / Skokie corridor is one continuous eruv-linked Jewish hub. The OU listings count 30–34 shuls and 18–23 schools across the corridor (v). Every tzibbur is represented: yeshivish, chassidish (the Veitzener Kollel and Kollel Zichron Yitzchok Zev / Chicago Chassidishe Kollel), Sephardi (Sephardic Community Kollel of Chicago), Modern Orthodox, and Chabad.
  • Kollel: Seven in the Ichud directory — Chicago Community Kollel, Kollel Ateres Ami, Kollel Tiferes Yisroel, Kollel Zichron Eliyahu, Kollel Zichron Yitzchok Zev, Sephardic Community Kollel, Veitzener Kollel — plus Kollel Torah MiTzion. Lincolnwood alone lists Lakewood, Ner Yisroel, Torah MiTzion, YU, and unaffiliated kollel affiliations simultaneously, which is about as wide a tent as exists anywhere.
  • Mosdos: Telshe Chicago, Hebrew Theological College, Arie Crown, Joan Dachs Bais Yaakov, Ida Crown, Hanna Sacks, Yeshivas Tiferes Tzvi, and many more.
  • Housing. The published medians mislead badly, and in both directions. West Ridge’s all-types median sale is $236,500–$262,000 (v, 2026) — that figure is dominated by the neighborhood’s large condo, co-op and two-to-four-flat stock. Detached single-family homes run about $500,000–$535,000, with larger four- and five-bedroom houses at or just under $500K (v). The cross-check that proves the point: neighboring Skokie prices at $272–276/ft² and Lincolnwood at $261/ft² (v), both above West Ridge’s all-types $189–208/ft² — which means the low figure is measuring apartments, not houses. On a single-family basis a 2,800 ft² house on the frum blocks is roughly $730,000–$770,000 (est.). Nearby: Peterson Park’s median sale is $520,000, Sauganash averages $541,752 (v). Property tax: Cook County’s effective rate is 1.91% with a median bill of $6,191; the City of Chicago runs 1.8–2.0% with a median bill of $4,814 (v) — call it $12,000–14,000/year on a family house.
  • Tuition: Lincolnwood $11–15K; Northbrook and Buffalo Grove $15–25K (self-rep.). Illinois has no broad ESA program.
  • Suburbs: Northbrook (YU-affiliated kollel, $400–599K homes), Buffalo Grove (Torah Academy PreK–8, Northern Lights Kollel, listing Chofetz Chaim/Lakewood/YU affiliations, $600–799K), Skokie (large, established, mixed).
  • Parnassa: Deep, diversified metro economy — finance, healthcare, logistics, tech, professional services.

CLEVELAND, OH Cleveland Heights, University Heights, Beachwood, South Euclid. Deep, old, and cheap.

  • Character: One of the strongest yeshivish communities in the Midwest, alongside a substantial Modern Orthodox community. The OU listing shows 13 shuls in Cleveland Heights alone (v); the frum population is spread across Cleveland Heights, University Heights, Beachwood, Shaker Heights, and South Euclid.
  • Mosdos: Telshe Yeshiva, Hebrew Academy of Cleveland, Yavne, Mosdos Ohr HaTorah, Fuchs Mizrachi.
  • Kollel: Six in the Ichud directory — Cedar Green Community Kollel / Kollel Yad Chaim Mordechai, Hebrew Academy Kollel / Kollel Ateres Nochum Zev, Kollel Bais Shalom, Kollel Nachlas Yaacov, Kollel of Young Israel, and Torah LIFE Institute / Ateres Chaim Boruch. Very few American cities have this density.
  • Housing: Among the cheapest large frum communities in America to buy — and not to hold. Cleveland Heights runs $120 per square foot (v, 2026), so a 2,800 ft² house is about $336,000 (comp.); the median was $223K in January 2026, +21.6% year-over-year (v). But Cleveland Heights’ effective property tax rate is about 3.00% (v) — among the highest in the United States — with bills running $6,059 to $10,802 depending on ZIP (v). That is roughly $10,000/year on a $336,000 house. Beachwood is the local answer: listings average around $415K at an effective 1.80%, median bill $6,944 (v). Cuyahoga County overall averages 2.55% (v).
  • Tuition: Ohio EdChoice Expansion — $6,166/child K–8, $8,408 high school, full award below 450% FPL, prorated above (v, 2026–27). Transformative for a single-earner or kollel family; tapering for a high-earning one.
  • Parnassa: Cleveland Clinic, University Hospitals, Case Western, and a broad regional economy.

DETROIT, MI — Oak Park, Southfield, Huntington Woods Deep kollel infrastructure, extremely cheap housing.

  • Character: Oak Park is the denser, more yeshivish center; Southfield has a large Modern Orthodox community around Young Israel of Southfield plus everything else. The OU listings count 16 shuls in Oak Park and 12 in Southfield (v). Southfield reports heavy in-migration from New York, New Jersey, Baltimore, Silver Spring, Philadelphia, Florida, Memphis, Montreal, and Israel (self-rep.).
  • Mosdos: Yeshiva Beth Yehudah (one of the oldest in America), Bais Yaakov of Detroit, Farber Hebrew Day School / Yeshivat Akiva, Yeshiva Gedolah of Greater Detroit.
  • Kollel: Six in the Ichud directory — Kollel Institute of Greater Detroit, Kollel Yeshiva Bais Yehuda, Kollel Hora’ah Bais Yehuda, Choshen Mishpat Kollel, Kollel Bais Tefila, and Dovid Ben Nachum Kollel (chassidish). The presence of both a hora’ah kollel and a choshen mishpat kollel makes Detroit one of the strongest semicha-and-beyond destinations outside New York and Israel.
  • Housing: Oak Park median $266K (+26.1% year-over-year) (v); Oak Park’s OU band is $200–399K with rentals $800–1,500. Southfield’s band is $400–599K with rentals $800–1,500 (self-rep.).
  • Tuition: Oak Park $11–15K, Southfield $15–20K (self-rep.). Michigan has no broad voucher program — Detroit’s affordability is housing-driven.
  • Parnassa: Automotive and engineering, healthcare (several top-ranked hospitals), and IT — “Automation Alley.” Wayne State and U-Michigan nearby.

COLUMBUS, OH Fast-growing job market, Ner Yisroel kollel, and Ohio’s voucher.

  • Mosdos: 3 Orthodox shuls, a community kollel, a Modern Orthodox K–12 day school, a girls’ high school, mikvah, eruv, JCC, preschools (v).
  • Kollel: Columbus Community Kollel, Ner Yisroel-affiliated.
  • Housing: $200–399K band, rentals $800–1,500 (self-rep.) — cheap for a city this size.
  • Tuition: OU band says $15–20K, but Ohio EdChoice applies here too, which changes the net substantially (v).
  • Parnassa: One of the fastest-growing employment markets in the Midwest. Nationwide, Ohio State, Cardinal Health, Honda, Chase, Wendy’s, Abbott, Battelle, L Brands, Abercrombie, DSW — plus Intel’s semiconductor fabs under construction, which is the largest single tech investment in the region’s history (v).

CINCINNATI, OH Amberley Village. Small, tight, and remarkably cheap to educate children in.

  • Character: 3 Orthodox shuls — Sha’arei Torah, Zichron Eliezer, and Golf Manor Synagogue — all within walking distance of each other (v), with daily minyanim and shiurim. 3 shuls, 2 mikvaos, 4 schools, 1 kollel in the OU listing.
  • Kollel: Cincinnati Community Kollel, listing both Lakewood and Ner Yisroel affiliation.
  • Mosdos: Cincinnati Hebrew Day School (boys and girls, EC–8), Ohr Torah Cincinnati (EC–8), Atara girls’ high school, plus a boys’ mesivta.
  • Housing — three neighborhoods, three prices, and that resolves an apparent contradiction. The community advertises a 2,500 ft², 4-bedroom home on a half-acre for under $350K (self-rep.) — about $140/ft². Amberley Village’s verified rate is $237/ft² with a $435,000 median (v), at which 2,500 ft² would be ~$592,000. Both are true, because they describe different neighborhoods: Roselawn’s median is $217,000 and Golf Manor averages $280,236 (v), while Amberley Village is the affluent one at $435,000–450,000 (v). The community’s figure describes Golf Manor and Roselawn. Ask which streets, and whether the shuls and schools are walkable from them. Hamilton County’s effective rate is 1.44–1.53%, county median bill $3,898 (v).
  • Tuition: Under $10K band (self-rep.), with Ohio EdChoice paying $6,166/child K–8 and $8,408 high school — full award below 450% FPL, prorated above (v, 2026–27). For a kollel or single-earner family this is arguably the best chinuch-cost situation of any established community in America; a high earner sees the award taper.
  • Parnassa: Broad regional economy; less tech-heavy than Columbus.

GREATER DAYTON, OH Very small, very cheap, and one hour from both Cincinnati and Columbus.

  • Mosdos: Beth Jacob and Chabad; Hillel Academy. 2 shuls, 1 mikvah, 1 school (v). No local high school.
  • Financial: SEEDS subsidies up to $500 for K–12 Jewish programming, EdChoice scholarships, and tuition assistance at Hillel Academy (self-rep.). First year of shul membership free, half price in year two (self-rep.).
  • Housing: $220–300K, rentals $1,200–3,600; cost of living 2.7% below the national average (self-rep.).
  • Parnassa: Wright-Patterson Air Force Base, LexisNexis, Kettering Health, Premier Health, Dayton Children’s Hospital, local universities (v, self-rep.).

MILWAUKEE, WI Larger and deeper than its reputation, with an unusual tuition arrangement.

  • Mosdos: Two Orthodox day schools, two girls’ high schools, two boys’ high schools, and a yeshiva — with FREE day school tuition — plus a community kollel, 11 Orthodox minyanim, four separate eruvin, four mikvah locations, a JCC and an active Federation (v, self-rep.). The free-tuition claim is the community’s own wording and appears to rest on Wisconsin’s long-running parental choice program (est. — the connection is my inference, and the claim’s scope is ambiguous in the source; verify with the schools directly, because if it holds it is the single most important fact about Milwaukee for a frum family).
  • Character: Explicitly spans Modern Orthodox, Centrist, Chassidic/Yeshivish, and Chabad, and markets that coexistence as its defining feature (self-rep.).
  • Kollel: Milwaukee Kollel.
  • Housing: Affordable (self-rep.); Milwaukee metro is inexpensive relative to Chicago.

MINNEAPOLIS / ST. LOUIS PARK, MN Two kollelim, small community, cold winters, strong economy.

  • Kollel: Minneapolis Community Kollel and Kollel Lomdei Torah — two in the Ichud directory, which is notable for a community this size.
  • Parnassa: Target, UnitedHealth, 3M, Mayo-adjacent medical sector; one of the strongest corporate bases in the upper Midwest (est.).
  • Note: Not in the OU relocation directory, so less structured data is available here than for comparable communities.

ST. LOUIS, MO — University City, Olivette, Creve Coeur — and CHESTERFIELD, MO Two linked communities, 25 minutes apart, with different characters.

  • St. Louis: 2 Orthodox day schools (one coed, one separate), a coed yeshiva high school, a Chofetz Chaim boys’ yeshiva, and a Bais Yaakov for girls. Two active kollelim. (v) The OU listing counts 4 shuls, 5 schools; other sources cite 7 shuls in the wider community. Kosher restaurants (Italian/Japanese, Indian), a deli, cafes, and kosher sections at Schnucks and Dierbergs.
  • Chesterfield: West of St. Louis, newer and growing. Eruv, mikvah, and the Chesterfield Kollel (Lakewood-affiliated). Missouri Torah Institute is 10 minutes away; St. Louis’s fuller school network is 25 minutes. Housing $400–599K, rentals $800–1,500, tuition $15–20K (self-rep.).
  • Parnassa: Fortune 500 headquarters, tech startups, a large medical sector.

INDIANAPOLIS, IN Small, cheap, Ner Yisroel kollel, and Indiana’s voucher.

  • Mosdos: 2 shuls, 1 mikvah, a community day school, daycare, and the Indy Kollel (v). No local high school — the standard limit for a community this size.
  • Kollel: Indy Kollel, Ner Yisroel-affiliated.
  • Housing: $115K for a 2-bedroom condo; $300–400K for a 3–4 bedroom house with a backyard within walking distance of the shuls (self-rep.). 1% state property tax cap; 3% state income tax.
  • Tuition: $11–15K band, but Indiana removed all income limits for 2026–27 — every family is eligible, averaging ~$6,536 per child (v), which the community cites directly.
  • Parnassa: Manufacturing, healthcare (two large hospital systems), sports, finance, construction, education, tech. Most offices within a 30-minute drive.

SOUTH BEND, IN A complete small frum community with an unusually strong school stack for its size.

  • Character: Centered on the Twyckenham Hills area, with an eruv enclosing over 2.5 square miles (v, community). Two shuls — Hebrew Orthodox Congregation and Midwest Torah Center — plus multiple minyanim (v).
  • Mosdos: South Bend Hebrew Day School (founded 1973, over 200 students, PreK–8, boys and girls) (v), plus a boys’ mesivta and a Bais Yaakov — meaning a family can educate children K–12 locally, which most communities this size cannot offer.
  • Kollel: South Bend Community Kollel, Lakewood-affiliated, housed in the shul building. Daily shiurim plus daily and weekly boys’ learning programs (v). Also a retiree kollel.
  • Kosher: South Bend Kosher (SBK) under Rabbi Yosef Chaim Danziger. Midwest Premium Kosher Market (full market, chalav Yisroel), Kramer’s Kosher Cuisine, Brand “Ess” Pizza, a kosher frozen yogurt shop, plus kosher sections at Martin’s, Kroger, Meijer, Trader Joe’s, and Costco (v).
  • Mikvah: Newly completed, men’s and women’s, at Hebrew Orthodox Congregation (v).
  • Housing: 3–4 bedroom houses with spacious yards, $175K–$350K (v, community). OU band: $200–399K, rentals $800–1,500.
  • Tuition: Under $10K, via Indiana’s state school voucher program, which the community cites as a defining advantage (v).
  • Parnassa: Notre Dame and the surrounding education sector, healthcare, manufacturing. A jobs assistance network is listed (self-rep.). Not a tech hub — this is a remote-work or local-professional community.
  • Position: 90 miles to Chicago, 3 hours to Detroit, 4 to Cleveland — genuinely within reach of three major frum centers (v).
  • Honest assessment: South Bend is one of the best value propositions in this document for a kollel or single-income family: full K–12 chinuch, a real kollel, sub-$10K tuition, sub-$350K housing, and Chicago close enough for anything the town lacks. The constraint is size — the shidduch pool, the professional job market, and the number of people your children will grow up with are all small, and that is not fixable by anything except more families moving there.

OVERLAND PARK, KS (Kansas City) One shul, growing fast.

  • Character: Centered on Congregation BIAV, the only OU shul in the area, with Hyman Brand Hebrew Academy revitalized by an influx of young families over the past five years (self-rep.). Award-winning NCSY chapter; an active Vaad Hakashrus expanding kosher availability.
  • Housing: Townhouse rental around $1,600/month; starter home $250–300K, all within walking distance of the shul (self-rep.).
  • Tuition: $11–15K (self-rep.).

OMAHA, NE Very small, integrated campus, low cost.

  • Mosdos: Beth Israel under Rabbi Ari Dembitzer; a K–6 day school, JCC, mikvah, and nursing/rehab center on a single integrated campus (v).
  • Housing: Median home price $155,400; average 2-bedroom rent $1,021 (self-rep.) — among the lowest figures anywhere in this document.
  • Parnassa: Fortune 500 and 1000 headquarters, a “silicon prairie” tech presence, large medical sector. Average commute 25–35 minutes (self-rep.).
  • Limits: K–6 only. This is a community for young families or for people who will send out for high school.

LOUISVILLE, KY One shul, one school, very low cost.

  • Mosdos: Anshei Sfard; a growing Montessori-model day school. Mikvah within walking distance of most community homes; eruv; Vaad Harabonim (v).
  • Housing: A 4-bedroom home with basement, 3 bathrooms, and yards for $350–400K (self-rep.); OU band $200–399K, rentals $800–1,500.
  • Tuition: $11–15K (self-rep.).
  • Parnassa: Medicine, senior healthcare, retail; several large corporate headquarters. No commute over 20 minutes (self-rep.).

4D — The South and Southeast

Fast-growing, low-tax, and — in Florida, Texas, Tennessee, and Nevada — no state income tax. Several of these communities are in genuine boom conditions.

MEMPHIS, TN Small, unusually cohesive, and a long-standing net importer of families.

  • Character: 3 Orthodox shuls that collaborate rather than compete (self-rep.), with Ashkenaz, Sefard, and Ari nusach all present. More than 100 new Jewish families moved to Memphis in the past five years (v, self-rep.).
  • Mosdos: Margolin Hebrew Academy (PreK–12) and Bornblum Jewish Community School (K–8), plus several preschools. A boys’ mesivta and girls’ high school are listed.
  • Kollel: Memphis Kollel (unaffiliated).
  • Housing. The 38120 ZIP, where much of the community lives, has a median sale of $442,000 at $162 per square foot (v, 2026); East Memphis’s median is $335,000 (v). The citywide $117/ft² figure is not the frum neighborhood. Tennessee’s assessment ratio does the heavy lifting on tax: Shelby County levies $2.702382 per $100 on 25% of value (v), an effective rate of about 0.68% — roughly $3,000/year on a $442,000 house (comp.), with no state income tax. Rentals $1,500–2,000 (self-rep.).
  • Tuition: $11–15K (self-rep.).
  • Tax: No state income tax, low property tax (v).
  • Parnassa: Healthcare (multiple hospital systems and private practice), IT, education, engineering, real estate, accounting, business management (self-rep.). Jobs assistance network listed.
  • Note: A yoetzet halacha is listed — one of relatively few communities in this size class with one.

ATLANTA, GA — Toco Hills and Dunwoody Two distinct communities in one metro, both substantial.

  • Atlanta (Toco Hills): 11 shuls, 4 mikvaos, 5 schools, 1 kollel (v). Five OU-affiliated shuls sit close to Emory, the CDC, Grady, Children’s Healthcare of Atlanta, and Georgia Tech. Nine kosher restaurants; kosher delis and bakeries in the supermarkets and Costco (self-rep.). Housing $600–799K, tuition $15–20K.
  • Dunwoody: Northern suburb, 16.5 miles from downtown. Median home $650K (typically 4–5 bedrooms with a backyard); average 4-bedroom rent $3,750 (self-rep.). 2 shuls, 2 mikvaos, 6 schools, 1 kollel. A 52-acre JCC, kosher Kroger. Retiree kollel and shidduch committee listed.
  • Kollel: Atlanta Scholars Kollel (kiruv-oriented); Dunwoody’s is Ner Yisroel-affiliated.
  • Parnassa: One of the strongest corporate bases in the South — Home Depot, Coca-Cola, UPS, Delta, Turner, plus Cox, Cisco, AT&T, IBM (v). Atlanta has been the #1 US moving destination for six consecutive years (self-rep.).

DALLAS, TX and PLANO, TX A single metro community across multiple neighborhoods, with a rapidly improving tuition picture.

  • Dallas: 8 shuls, 4 mikvaos, 6 schools (v). Mosdos: Torah Day School of Dallas, Akiba Academy, Yavneh Academy, Mesorah High School for Girls, plus a boys’ mesivta and a coed Modern Orthodox high school. Congregation Shaare Tefilla (Modern Orthodox, 175+ families) and a range of others.
  • Plano: The affordable end of the metro. Congregation Lev Yisrael; the Plano branch of the Dallas kollel was recently established with four kollel couples (self-rep.). Housing: $400–500K for a four/five bedroom, 2,500 sq ft home with a large yard or pool and a private garage (self-rep.). Five kosher restaurants, three sushi takeouts, an ice cream store, three kosher groceries in the metro.
  • Kollel: Dallas Community Kollel / Dallas Area Torah Association (DATA), unaffiliated, with the Plano branch.
  • Tuition: Currently $15–20K (self-rep.) — but Texas launches universal school choice in 2026–27 (v), which should materially change this. Worth watching closely.
  • Parnassa: Toyota, State Farm, AT&T, JCPenney, Dr Pepper headquarters; UT Southwestern Medical Center, UT Dallas, Children’s Medical Center. No state income tax.

HOUSTON, TX One of the top American destinations for relocating frum families, and the infrastructure backs it up.

  • Mosdos: 3 day schools, 1 girls’ high school, 1 boys’ yeshiva high school, 1 coed high school (v). 3 mikvaos, 4 eruvin.
  • Shuls: 9 diverse shuls — Bellaire Jewish Center, Beth Rambam, a heimish minyan, Chabad, the Meyerland Minyan, Torah VaChessed, Young Israel, United Orthodox Synagogues (v).
  • Kollel: The Kollel of Houston Torah Center, Lakewood-affiliated. Plus outreach organizations (TORCH, J-Hype, Lubavitch) and NCSY/Bnei Akiva.
  • Kosher: 8 restaurants, 6 supermarkets with kosher sections, 2 bakeries, 5+ caterers (v).
  • Housing: $400–599K band, rentals $1,500–2,000 (self-rep.).
  • Tuition: $11–15K (self-rep.), with Texas ESA arriving 2026–27.
  • Parnassa: Energy, the Texas Medical Center (the largest medical complex in the world), aerospace, engineering. No state income tax, low property tax rate, jobs assistance network (self-rep.).
  • Caveat: Flood risk is a real and specific consideration in Meyerland. Check the flood maps before you check anything else.

SAN ANTONIO, TX Small, deliberately low-pressure, and honest about being small.

  • Character: The community’s own pitch is worth quoting because it is unusually candid: “No traffic. No fashion arms race. No pressure to keep up with anyone’s car, kitchen renovation, or kids’ résumé.” 2 shuls, 1 mikvah, 1 school, 1 kollel (v).
  • Kollel: San Antonio Community Kollel (unaffiliated). Retiree kollel also listed.
  • Housing: $200–399K, rentals $800–1,500 (self-rep.).
  • Tuition: Under $10K, with tuition vouchers/rebates listed (self-rep.).
  • Limits: One school, no high school. Plan accordingly.

SOUTH FLORIDA The largest frum region outside the Northeast, and internally very varied.

  • Miami Beach: 9 shuls, 1 mikvah in the OU listing (v); the Miami Beach Community Kollel and the Sephardic Congregation of Florida (Temple Moses) among others. Established, dense, expensive, and heavily Sephardi and Latin American.
  • North Miami Beach (NMB): One of the strongest yeshivish communities in the South — Kollel Zichron Michel of North Miami Beach and the BMMB Kollel are both in the Ichud directory. Deep mosdos.
  • Hollywood, FL: Hollywood Community Kollel (Ichud). Growing quickly, and cheaper than Miami Beach.
  • Boca Raton / Boynton Beach / Delray Beach: Boca is the institutional anchor — Katz Hillel Day School and Katz Yeshiva High School serve the whole area. Boca median home $675–685K (2026) (v); Boynton and Delray are the affordable alternatives within the Boca school orbit, with Delray heavy on 55+ and gated communities and a newly-built 25-square-mile eruv (self-rep.). Boca Raton Kollel in the Ichud directory.
  • Pinecrest/Miami: Suburban, large lots, tree canopy. Homes from $250K but the OU band reads $800K–1M with rentals over $2,500; tuition $20–25K (self-rep.) — the most expensive band in Florida. Alexander S. Gross Hebrew Academy; Young Israel of Kendall.
  • Tax: No state income tax, and Florida runs a large ESA program — about 60% of participating schools are religious, with recent growth among Jewish providers (v).

ORLANDO, FL Small, new, and expanding fast — including a purpose-built development.

  • Character: Orlando Torah Center under Rabbi Menachem Zev Kramer (a musmach of Yeshivas Chofetz Chaim). Orlando Torah Academy, a Chofetz Chaim network affiliate. 2 shuls, 1 mikvah, 4 schools, 1 kollel (v).
  • Kollel: Orlando Community Kollel, Lakewood-affiliated, newly established, offering nightly chavrusa learning and evening boys’ programming (self-rep.).
  • Eden Gardens: A purpose-built Orthodox development — new homes with private pools, 4 to 11 bedrooms, a condo-hotel planned, and tuition vouchers/rebates listed. Housing band $400–599K, rentals over $2,500, tuition $11–15K (self-rep.). Read the developer marketing carefully; a community built as a real-estate product is a different proposition from one that grew.
  • Housing: $400–599K, rentals over $2,500 (self-rep.).

TAMPA, FL Six shuls, five of them Chabad, and a Chofetz Chaim kollel.

  • Character: 6 community Orthodox shuls, 5 of which are growing Chabads; one kollel affiliated with the Rabbinical Seminary of America (Chofetz Chaim); three eruv communities (Temple Terrace, Carrollwood, Tampa Torah); 2 mikvaos within walking distance (v). This is a Chabad-forward community and that shapes everything about it.
  • Housing: $400–599K, rentals $2,000–2,500 (self-rep.).
  • Tuition: Under $10K, with Florida ESA (self-rep.).
  • Parnassa: Named #2 US city to find a job in 2024 by WalletHub (v). No state income tax.

JACKSONVILLE, FL Mandarin neighborhood. Small, growing, and the OU’s 2024 fair winner.

  • Character: Over 35 new families since 2024 (self-rep.). Etz Chaim and Chabad, plus a newly formed shtiebel minyan. Explicitly positions itself as a “label-free zone.” 3 shuls, 2 mikvaos, 4 schools, 1 kollel (v).
  • Kosher: A glatt kosher meat restaurant and two chalav Yisroel eateries (v).
  • Housing: $200–399K, rentals $1,500–2,000 (self-rep.) — among the cheapest in Florida.
  • Tuition: Under $10K with vouchers/rebates (self-rep.).

NASHVILLE, TN Very small, very expensive housing, booming city.

  • Character: One Orthodox shul, a school network from preschool through high school, kosher restaurants, and kosher meat and groceries at several area stores (self-rep.). 1 shul, 1 mikvah, 3 schools (v). Nashville adds over 100 new residents a day (self-rep.).
  • Housing: $800K–1M band, rentals $2,000–2,500 (self-rep.) — by far the most expensive housing of any small community in this document, and the thing that most limits it.
  • Tuition: $11–15K with vouchers/rebates. No state income tax (v).

NEW ORLEANS, LA Metairie. Tiny, and paying people to come.

  • Character: Congregation Beth Israel in Metairie, with Rabbi Uri and Dahlia Topolosky. Kosher deli, upscale Moroccan restaurant, kosher market, mikveh, JCC, day school, eruv. Café du Monde is now certified kosher (v).
  • The Minyan Project: A commitment to relocate 10 young families with housing rental or loan subsidy, moving allowance, low-cost day school tuition, and free memberships to community organizations (v, self-rep.).
  • Honest read: This is a community that needs people more than it can currently support them. That is either the opportunity or the risk, depending on temperament.

CHARLOTTE, NC 2 shuls, 1 mikvah in the OU listing (v). Minimal published detail; growing metro. Verify locally before relying on anything.

MOUNTAIN VIEW, NC A newer listed community with limited published information. Treat as early-stage.

SAVANNAH, GA Savannah Kollel is in the Ichud directory — a small, historic community with a kollel presence.

4E — The West

LOS ANGELES, CA The second-largest frum concentration in America, spread across several distinct neighborhoods.

  • Hancock Park / La Brea / Beverly-Fairfax — The yeshivish and chassidish center. Home to Yeshiva Gedolah of Los Angeles, Yeshiva Ohr Elchonon Chabad, Bais Yaakov, and many kehillos. Median home value in Hancock Park proper around $1,859,147 (v, Niche 2026) — though the frum streets vary widely and this figure is skewed by the historic mansion district.
  • Pico-Robertson / Beverlywood — Mixed yeshivish, Modern Orthodox, Sephardi, and Persian. Very dense shul and restaurant infrastructure. The published $841,000 median is condo-weighted and is not a house price: the neighborhood’s median home price is $1,275,000 with an average sale of $1,564,663, and a 12-month median of $1,214,500 (v). Average rent $3,607 (v).
  • Valley Village / North Hollywood / Sherman Oaks — The Valley communities, meaningfully cheaper than the city side. Average rent $2,547 (v). Emek, Yeshiva Aharon Yaakov Ohr Eliyahu, Valley Torah.
  • Kollel: Seven in the Ichud directory — Kollel Los Angeles, Kollel Ohr Haemet, Los Angeles Intercommunity Kollel, Merkaz Hatorah Community Kollel, Mishkan Torah Valley Community Kollel, Valley Village Community Kollel, and YACHAD: Los Angeles Iranian Kollel. That last one reflects LA’s very large Persian community, which is a defining feature of the city and has no real parallel elsewhere in America.
  • Mosdos: YULA, Shalhevet, Harkham Hillel, Toras Emes, Bais Chaya Mushka, and many more.
  • Parnassa: Entertainment, healthcare, real estate, apparel, tech. Very high cost of living and California’s top-bracket state income tax.

SAN DIEGO and LA JOLLA, CA Two full-service communities, expensive, and unusually pleasant.

  • San Diego: Two Orthodox schools (PreK–8 coed) plus separate boys’ and girls’ yeshiva high schools. Kosher dining and catering, mikvah, and an eruv for each shul neighborhood (v). Beth Jacob is the founding Orthodox shul; a new campus in San Carlos is slated to open 2027 (self-rep.). 2 shuls, 2 mikvaos, 4 schools, 1 kollel (unaffiliated).
  • La Jolla: Congregation Adat Yeshurun, with a newly renovated mikvah and eruv. A mix from Modern Orthodox to yeshivish, K–12 with separate boys’ and girls’ high schools. 3-bedroom condos from $700K, minutes’ walk from shul (self-rep.). Across the street from UC San Diego; 10 minutes from UCSD and Scripps hospitals.
  • Housing: San Diego’s OU band is over $1 million with rentals over $2,500; tuition $20–25K (self-rep.).
  • Parnassa: Biotech, technology, university and hospital systems. Typical commute 10–20 minutes (self-rep.).

DENVER, CO Full-spectrum, outdoors-oriented, and one of the better-balanced out-of-town options.

  • Character: 7 shuls across the spectrum, 2 mikvaos, 5 schools, 1 kollel (v). Two Orthodox elementary schools, a Modern Orthodox high school, a yeshiva, and a Bais Yaakov — a full K–12 stack in both directions.
  • Kollel: Denver Community Kollel, Lakewood-affiliated.
  • Housing: Condos from $350,000 up to $3 million homes; large rental communities from $1,500/month (self-rep.). OU band: $600–799K.
  • Tuition: $11–15K (self-rep.).
  • Parnassa: Broad metro economy; commute to downtown and major business districts under 30 minutes in most cases (self-rep.). Community offers job-finding support.
  • Draw: 300 days of sun, mountains, skiing. This is a genuine and non-trivial factor in why people move and why they stay.

PHOENIX and SCOTTSDALE, AZ A combined community with the best tuition-subsidy structure in the West.

  • Character: The combined Phoenix–Scottsdale community has 9 Jewish day schools serving over 1,000 children K–12; large eruvin; over a dozen kosher restaurants plus groceries, bakeries and caterers; kollel learning; and four mikvaos (v, self-rep.). Ashkenazic, Sephardic, Chabad, Yeshivish, and Modern Orthodox Jews all live here (self-rep.) — the community explicitly names all five.
  • Kollel: Kollel Avreichim of Arizona (Ichud). Scottsdale lists an unaffiliated kollel.
  • Housing: Phoenix OU band $600–799K with rentals $800–1,500; Scottsdale $800K–1M with rentals $2,000–2,500 (self-rep.).
  • Tuition: Phoenix reports under $10K; Scottsdale reports $20–25K (self-rep.) — the gap reflects which schools each community’s families use. Arizona’s STO tax credits and Empowerment Scholarship Accounts (ESA) enable generous day school scholarships at all income levels (v, self-rep.), which is the reason Phoenix’s number is what it is.
  • Parnassa: Semiconductor cluster, healthcare, a growing corporate base. Low corporate tax, low property tax.

LAS VEGAS / PECCOLE RANCH and HENDERSON, NV No state income tax, full infrastructure, and two distinct sub-communities.

  • Las Vegas / Peccole Ranch: Describes itself as “the most developed out-of-town community” (self-rep.) — Young Israel within walking distance of kosher groceries, shopping, two kosher restaurants, a multiplex, condos and apartments, and the mesivta and beis midrash. 2 day schools, boys’ and girls’ high schools, 3 mikvaos, an eruv, 11 kosher restaurants (v). Median home $450K (self-rep.); OU band $400–599K, tuition $11–15K.
  • Henderson: The quieter alternative. Standalone mikvah, a 2+ square-mile eruv, local markets with kosher staples; day schools a 20-minute commute. 4 shuls, 4 mikvaos, 7 schools (v). Explicitly positions itself against “the pressure to keep up” (self-rep.). Housing $600–799K, tuition $15–20K.
  • Tax: No state income tax, low property tax, low corporate tax (v).

SEATTLE, WA Seward Park. A 120-year-old community in a top-tier tech economy.

  • Character: Seven Orthodox shuls across several neighborhoods (v), with a historically significant Sephardic community — one of the oldest and largest in North America.
  • Mosdos: Three PreK–8 day schools, a coed yeshiva high school, and a girls’ high school (v).
  • Kollel: The Seattle Kollel (Ichud).
  • Parnassa: Eight Fortune 500 companies headquartered locally — Microsoft, Boeing, Costco, Amazon, Starbucks, T-Mobile among them — plus Google and Meta offices, a large biotech sector, and the University of Washington (v). For a programmer, this is the single strongest local market on this list.
  • Tax: No state income tax. Housing is expensive; the trade is explicit.

PORTLAND, OR Small, unified, with a thriving kollel and a high-tuition state.

  • Character: Two child-centered day schools for infants through 8th grade, a high school in development, a thriving community kollel, daily minyanim and night seder, an award-winning NCSY chapter, kosher mikvaos and eruv, kosher caterers, bakeries, a restaurant, and grocery offerings under Oregon Kosher (v). 1 shul, 2 mikvaos, 3 schools, 1 kollel.
  • Kollel: Portland Kollel (unaffiliated).
  • Housing: $400–599K, rentals $1,500–2,000 (self-rep.).
  • Tuition: $11–15K (self-rep.); Oregon’s top income bracket is 6%+.
  • Limits: High school still in development. One shul.

TUCSON, AZ Tucson Torah Center in the Ichud directory. Small, with Arizona’s ESA and STO benefits available.

PALO ALTO, CA The Jewish Study Network appears in the Ichud directory — a kiruv-oriented presence in Silicon Valley rather than a conventional community kollel. Relevant primarily to someone already working in Bay Area tech.

4F — New England and Upstate New York

GREATER BOSTON, MA Brighton, Brookline, Newton, Malden, Cambridge, Sharon. Multiple neighborhoods, one shared school system.

  • Character: 13 shuls, 7 mikvaos, 8 schools across the area (v). All the neighborhoods support the same Orthodox day schools and yeshivos: five elementary/middle schools and four high schools, including coed, girls’, and boys’ options (v). Ashkenaz, Sefard, and Ari nusach all present; a yoetzet halacha is listed.
  • Kollel: Kollel of Greater Boston, listing both Lakewood and unaffiliated.
  • Kosher: Six kosher restaurants in Brookline and Boston, several caterers, and the KVH vaad (v).
  • Housing. The self-reported $600–799K band does not describe Brookline at all. Brookline’s median single-family sale was $2,675,000 in 2025, up 15%, at $797 per square foot (v) — the highest $/ft² of any community in this document outside Boro Park. Newton’s median single-family is $1,825,000 (v). Sharon is the affordable option at $345/ft² (v), with an average single-family tax bill of $14,353 for FY2026 (v). Brookline’s residential rate is $10.24 per $1,000, which is roughly $27,400/year on the median house (comp.).
  • Parnassa: One of the top high-tech centers in the US, plus the largest concentration of universities and teaching hospitals in the country (v).

PROVIDENCE, RI Small, chesed-oriented, and offering the largest relocation incentive in America.

  • Character: East Side of Providence and nearby Pawtucket. Tree-lined streets, homes with yards, children walking or biking to school. Four Orthodox shuls, all within walking distance — Sha’arei Tefilla (housed in the day school), Mishkon Tfiloh, Beth Shalom, Ohawe Shalom. 5 shuls, 1 mikvah, 2 schools, 1 kollel (v).
  • Mosdos: Providence Hebrew Day School (PreK–8) plus a Bais Yaakov-type girls’ high school, and a mechanchim kollel where rebbeim from the school learn in the afternoon — an unusual and rather elegant arrangement.
  • The incentive: $50,000, a year of free tuition at the local Orthodox school, and a $1,000 move-in stipend, targeting five to ten families (v, reported 2023). Applications reportedly ran into double digits within two months.
  • Housing — and this reframes the incentive. The self-reported $600–799K band understates it: the median home price on the East Side of Providence is now over $900,000, at $389–437 per square foot (v, 2026). Providence’s residential rate is $26.75 per $1,000 (v), roughly $24,000/year on a $900K house (comp.); Rhode Island’s statewide average effective rate is 1.53% (v). Read against those numbers, the $50,000 grant is not a bonus on a cheap house — it is a down-payment subsidy on an expensive one, and the tuition line (under $10K) is doing more of the work than the cash. Rentals $1,500–2,000 (self-rep.).
  • Retiree kollel listed.

WEST HARTFORD, CT Mid-size, professional, high tuition.

  • Tuition: Under $10K (self-rep.).
  • Parnassa: Known regionally as a strong STEM area (self-rep.).

ALBANY, NY State capital, half the real-estate cost of downstate, semiconductor economy.

  • Character: 2 shuls, 1 mikvah, 5 schools (v). Two day schools with small class sizes, quality preschools, NCSY, a Federation, mikveh, eruv, JCC, day camps, and community beis midrash programming. A Vaad Hakashrut covering senior meal plans, a caterer, a nursing/independent living center, and a butcher and bakery inside a large supermarket (v).

  • Tuition: $11–15K (self-rep.) — NY has no broad voucher program, though see §3.5 on the new state pilot.

ROCHESTER, NY Diverse for its size, with a Chofetz Chaim and Lakewood kollel presence and a full K–12 stack.

  • Character: Explicitly Modern Orthodox, Sephardic, Yeshivish, and Chabad shuls in one community (v) — genuinely unusual at this scale. 3 shuls, 2 mikvaos, 6 schools (v).
  • Mosdos: PreK through high school, including a boys’ mesifta, a girls’ high school, and a post-high-school beis medrash (v).

BUFFALO / AMHERST / WILLIAMSVILLE, NY Small, safe, cheap.

  • Character: Young Israel of Greater Buffalo with consistent daily minyanim under Rabbi Avi Okin, and a growing Chabad presence within the same kehilla. An eruv encompassing four shuls. 6 shuls, 2 mikvaos (v).
  • Mosdos: Ohr Temimim (boys and girls), three kosher dining options.

  • Tuition: $11–15K with vouchers/rebates listed (self-rep.).

Part 5 — Eretz Yisroel

Israel is not “America with better food.” The housing market, the school system, the kollel economy, and the legal status of a ben Torah all work on entirely different principles. This part covers the structure, the numbers, and — because it currently dominates everything else — the political situation.

5.1 How the map is organized

Israeli frum geography sorts into five categories, and mixing them up produces bad expectations.

  1. Yerushalayim — the largest and most internally varied charedi population in the world, spread across a dozen neighborhoods with distinct characters and price levels.
  2. The charedi cities — Bnei Brak, Modiin Illit (Kiryat Sefer), Beitar Illit, Elad, and Beit Shemesh/RBS. Municipally charedi, purpose-built or effectively so, with the full infrastructure and the full social norms.
  3. Charedi neighborhoods inside mixed cities — Ashdod, Netanya, Petach Tikva, Rechovot, Haifa, Tzfas, Tveria, Kiryat Gat, Netivot, Rechasim. Cheaper, smaller, more variable.
  4. Religious Zionist and Anglo towns and yishuvim — Efrat, Gush Etzion, Modiin, Chashmonaim, Ma’ale Adumim, Ra’anana, Karnei Shomron, and dozens of smaller yishuvim.
  5. The periphery — the Negev, the Galil, and deep Yehuda and Shomron. Cheapest housing in the country, thinnest infrastructure, longest commutes.

  • Sanhedria / Sanhedria Murchevet — Charedi, central, older stock, walkable to Geula.

  • Neve Yaakov / Pisgat Ze’ev — Northern, most affordable, mixed populations, growing Anglo charedi presence.

  • Bayit Vegan — Established, charedi-yeshivish, home to major yeshivos.
  • Ramat Shlomo — Charedi, newer, north Jerusalem.
  • Rechavia / Talbiya / Old Katamon / Baka — National-religious and Anglo, expensive to very expensive.
  • Givat Ze’ev — Just outside the city, charedi neighborhoods, cheaper.
  • Telzstone (Kiryat Ye’arim) — Small charedi town 10 minutes west of Yerushalayim, heavily Anglo, purpose-built, with its own mosdos.

5.3 The charedi cities

BNEI BRAK

  • The densest and most institutionally important charedi city in the world. Ponevezh, Slabodka, Chevron-adjacent, Vizhnitz, Lelov, and dozens of other yeshivos and chassidus centers.
  • Extremely dense; very little green space; old housing stock in much of the city.
  • Average rent ₪4,397/month (early 2026) (v).
  • Ampersand, the KamaTech charedi co-working space, is here — the center of the charedi tech sector (v).
  • Anglo presence exists but is smaller proportionally than in Yerushalayim or RBS.

MODIIN ILLIT (KIRYAT SEFER)

  • Population 89,627 (2024) (v) — the largest Jewish community in Yehuda and Shomron.
  • Purpose-built charedi, primarily Lithuanian-yeshivish with a large chassidish population. Sub-neighborhoods: Kiryat Sefer, Achuzat Brachfeld, Green Park, Kiryat HaRabanim.
  • Among the more affordable serious charedi options; comparable to or below RBS on price (v).
  • 30–40 minutes to both Yerushalayim and the Tel Aviv area, which is its structural advantage.

BEITAR ILLIT

  • Population 72,964 (February 2026), with 55.4% under the age of 18 (v) — one of the youngest populations of any city on earth.
  • Heavily chassidish (Ger, Belz, Vizhnitz, Sanz, Toldos Aharon and others) alongside a Lithuanian population.
  • 5,000 new housing units approved August 2025 — nearly doubling the city — plus 230 more approved December 2025 (v). Expect substantial construction and substantial disruption.
  • Average rent ₪4,592/month (v).
  • 20 minutes to Yerushalayim.

ELAD

  • Purpose-built charedi city near Rosh Ha’ayin, notable for a large Sephardi charedi population alongside Ashkenazi.
  • Closer to the Tel Aviv job market than Beitar or Modiin Illit.

BEIT SHEMESH / RAMAT BEIT SHEMESH

  • Population surpassed 170,000 as of 2026 (v) — now one of Israel’s larger cities, and the single most important destination for Anglo frum aliyah.
  • The neighborhoods are genuinely different places:
    • RBS Aleph — Established, strong Anglo presence, mixed charedi and national-religious, the widest range of hashkafa.
    • RBS Bet — Predominantly Israeli charedi, including significant kanoi elements. Not the default Anglo landing spot.
    • RBS Gimmel — Newer, heavily Anglo yeshivish. Described by residents as “Lakewood transplanted to Israel,” with streets named after gedolim and a de facto communal dress standard; families from Passaic, Monsey, or the Five Towns frequently report that it feels immediately familiar (v, community reporting).
    • RBS Daled / Hey / Vav and further phases — under construction and expanding.
    • Old Beit Shemesh — Cheaper, more Israeli, more mixed.
  • Prices: RBS Gimmel runs roughly ₪19,000–₪24,000 per square meter; a recent 3-bedroom listing at ₪2,390,000 for ~105 sqm works out to about ₪22,762/sqm (v, 2026). City-wide, a 4-room apartment recently sold at ₪2,415,000 (~$775,000) and a 3-room at ₪2,140,000 (~$693,000) (v).
  • Trajectory: Beit Shemesh prices rose 66.5% over seven years, driven by charedi demand against previously low prices; Q2 2025 showed a slight decline of 0.42% (v). Newer neighborhoods including Gimmel were projected to rise 8–10% in 2025 (v).
  • Anglo infrastructure: Dozens of Anglo WhatsApp groups, women’s groups, English shiurim, and chesed networks (v). This is the practical reason RBS works for people who don’t yet have Hebrew.

5.4 Mixed cities and the periphery

  • Ashdod — Large charedi population, notably Ger and other chassidus, plus a large Sephardi charedi community and a substantial French population. Average apartment ₪2.09 million (Q1 2026), 14th nationally; average rent ₪4,402/month (v) — considerably cheaper than the Jerusalem corridor, with beach access.
  • Netanya — Large Anglo and French communities, mixed religious and charedi. Average apartment ₪2.87 million (Q1 2026), 6th nationally; average rent ₪4,729/month (v).
  • Petach Tikva, Rechovot, Rehovot-adjacent — Established mixed cities with real charedi neighborhoods and better access to the Tel Aviv job market.
  • Haifa and Rechasim — Rechasim is a small charedi town near Haifa with a strong yeshiva presence; Haifa itself is inexpensive and has a small but real frum community.
  • Tzfas — Small, distinctive, heavily breslov and chassidish alongside a national-religious population and a well-known Anglo baal teshuva community. Cheap by Israeli standards. Limited parnassa.
  • Tveria — Cheap, growing charedi population, limited economy.
  • Kiryat Gat / Carmei Gat — Carmei Gat is a new Anglo-oriented neighborhood positioned on transit: 38 minutes by train to Tel Aviv, 35 minutes’ drive to Modiin, next to Highway 6 (v, developer). New construction with 334 units in one recent project. OU band: ₪600–799K equivalent with rentals ₪1,500–2,000 equivalent; tuition under $10K (self-rep.). Worth watching, and worth remembering that developer marketing is marketing.
  • Netivot, Ofakim, Yerucham, Arad, Dimona — Negev towns with growing charedi communities, often kollel-anchored, very cheap housing, very thin job markets.
  • Emanuel — Charedi town in the Shomron, inexpensive, isolated.

5.5 Religious Zionist and Anglo communities

  • Gush Etzion — Suburban communities 15–45 minutes from Yerushalayim, Beit Shemesh, and Beitar (v). Five religious elementary schools, special-education resources described as among the country’s best, very active Bnei Akiva, and high schools experienced with olim (v). Sub-communities differ sharply: Neve Daniel, Elazar, Alon Shvut are religious-Zionist suburban; Tekoa and Nokdim are mixed religious and secular with a desert view 15 minutes from Yerushalayim; Ma’ale Amos and Meitzad are growing charedi yishuvim with large English-speaking populations, Ma’ale Amos guided by an English-speaking rav (v). OU band: ₪400–599K equivalent, rentals ₪800–1,500 equivalent, tuition under $10K (self-rep.). Dedicated local aliyah staff.
  • Efrat — The largest Anglo religious-Zionist town, full infrastructure, expensive by regional standards.
  • Modiin and Chashmonaim — Modiin is a large modern city with a substantial Anglo religious population; Chashmonaim is a religious yishuv nearby, heavily Anglo. Both are commutable to Tel Aviv and Yerushalayim, and both are expensive.
  • Ma’ale Adumim, Ra’anana, Karnei Shomron, Ginot Shomron, Beit El, Kochav Yaakov, Tel Zion — Each with its own character; Tel Zion and parts of Kochav Yaakov are charedi.
  • Deep Yehuda and Shomron — Shilo, Eli, Ateret, Ma’aleh Levona, Itamar, Elon Moreh, Har Bracha, Otniel, Susya. The lowest cost per square meter in the country for detached homes with land, and the trade is distance, infrastructure, and a security and legal environment that is materially different from inside the Green Line.

5.6 The housing and mortgage reality

Several structural facts that surprise Americans.

The state owns the land. The Israel Land Authority controls roughly 93% of the country’s land (v), releasing it through tenders. Supply is administratively throttled rather than market-driven, which is the single biggest structural difference from the US.

Construction is concrete and stone, partly for security — every apartment requires a mamad, a reinforced room. Build times run to years, not months.

Apartments are the normal family home. A 4-room apartment is roughly 90–110 m² and houses a family. Detached houses exist mainly in yishuvim and the periphery. The national average apartment price was ₪2.33 million in Q1 2026 (v); Jerusalem averages ₪36,000/m², median ₪33,000/m² (v).

Mortgages work differently, and the difference is not cosmetic. There is no 30-year fixed-rate mortgage of the American kind. An Israeli mashkanta is assembled from several tracks at once, and as of 2026 (v):

Track Rate
Fixed, non-indexed (kalatz) 5.3–6.1%
Fixed, CPI-indexed (kevua tzmuda) 4.5–5.2%
Floating (mishtana) 4.8–5.5%
Prime 5.25%

The Bank of Israel rate was 3.75% in June 2026 with inflation at 2.6%, and further cuts toward 3.5% were expected by year-end (v). Terms run to 30 years, and payments are capped at 50% of net monthly income (v).

The loan-to-value rules are the part olim should notice:

Buyer Maximum LTV Minimum down payment
New oleh 90% 10%
Israeli citizen 75% 25%
Non-resident / investment 50% 50%

(v) — that is a materially better borrowing position than a citizen gets, and it is time-limited, so it belongs in the aliyah timing conversation.

The CPI-indexed tracks are the trap. On an indexed track the principal rises with the cost-of-living index, so the balance can grow even while you pay. Model the downside before committing, not the headline payment.

Mechir LaMishtaken, the subsidized-housing lottery for eligible buyers, is a major channel into new construction — and access to it is one of the benefits currently entangled in the political situation in §5.8.

Arnona is not American property tax. Israel’s municipal tax is charged per square meter of the apartment, not as a share of its value, so it does not scale with the market. Jerusalem runs ₪40.40–113.22 per m² per year; Beit Shemesh ₪41.26–47.48, roughly ₪3,500–5,000/year on a 100 m² apartment (v). Rates rose 1.626% nationally in 2026 (v). Against the $10,000–27,000 annual bills in §6.1, arnona is close to a rounding error — and §6.2 covers the discounts, including the rule change that specifically hurts avreichim.

Prices moved with the war. Israeli housing dipped, then rose for two consecutive months after the ceasefire, then dipped again (v, Times of Israel housing snapshots, Feb–Mar 2026). Anyone buying should look at the latest CBS index rather than any figure in this document.

5.7 Kollel in Eretz Yisroel — the economics

This is a completely different institution from an American community kollel.

Scale. At the start of 2024 there were roughly 169,366 yeshiva and kollel students in Israel (v, IDI). Between 2013 and 2023 that number grew 83% (v). Learning full-time into adulthood is the statistical norm in Israeli charedi society, not an elite track.

The income stack. A typical avreich’s household income is assembled from several small sources rather than one salary:

  • Government stipend — roughly ₪800–900/month from the Education Ministry, with an additional ₪1,000–2,000/month for those with at least three children, no car, and a non-working wife, subject to conditions (v).
  • Kollel stipend from the institution itself, varying enormously by kollel.
  • Keren Lomdei Torah — a fund providing ₪14,000/year in twelve monthly payments to avreichim whose wives earn no more than ₪1,200/month (v).
  • The wife’s salary — usually the largest single component. Charedi women’s employment stands at 81% (v, 2025), versus 53% for charedi men.
  • Family support, from parents and in-laws, which in the Israeli system is often formalized at the time of the shidduch and can include the apartment itself.
  • Bituach Leumi child allowances.

The outcomes. 33% of charedi families live below the poverty line, yet 75% own an apartment — slightly higher than the rest of Jewish society (v, IDI). Both halves of that sentence are true and they explain a great deal: the system produces low cash income alongside high asset ownership, because apartments come through family and subsidy channels rather than through earnings. 47% of charedi children were below the poverty line after transfers (2022) (v). Charedi men who leave learning for work earn about 49% of non-charedi wages, largely for lack of training (v).

For an Anglo. Anglo kollelim exist throughout Yerushalayim (over 20 in Har Nof alone with English representation), RBS, Telzstone, and elsewhere. Practical differences from an American community kollel: there is usually no communal job attached, the stipend is smaller, the cost of living is lower, and the standing of a full-time learner is normative rather than exceptional. What you give up is the American safety net — health insurance is via the kupot cholim rather than an employer, and the American communal-kollel package (tuition remission, housing help) generally does not exist in the same form.

5.8 The political situation — and why it is not background noise

This is currently the single most important variable for anyone planning a charedi life in Israel, and it is unresolved.

The legal chain of events:

  • June 2024 — the High Court ruled that with the relevant law and government decision expired, there was no longer any legal framework for charedi military-service exemptions, and that state financial support for exempt students was also illegal (v).
  • April 2024 onward — yeshivos and kollelim have been denied funding in respect of draft-eligible students: roughly ₪5,000/year per unmarried yeshiva bochur and ₪10,000/year per married kollel student, totaling about ₪650 million/year (v, IDI).
  • August 2024 — the Attorney General ordered daycare subsidies cut for children of yeshiva students who disobey draft orders (v).
  • March 2025 onward — kollel students subject to the draft have been denied daycare subsidies, worth roughly ₪26,400/year per family on average, about ₪250 million/year in total (v, IDI).
  • Additional sanctions — loss of access to discounted housing deals (Mechir LaMishtaken), cancellation of after-school program subsidies, and cancellation of discounts on arnona (municipal tax) and public transportation (v).
  • November 19, 2025 — the High Court required the government to formulate an effective enforcement plan; a contempt motion followed (v).
  • December 2025 — the Bank of Israel published its assessment of the economic cost of non-enlistment (v).
  • April 26, 2026 — the High Court ordered the government to enforce the draft, including withholding state benefits and opening criminal proceedings against draft evaders. Critically, it extended the daycare-subsidy denial to all draft-subject yeshiva students, including those who work — not only those learning full time (v).
  • July 17, 2026 — the Knesset dissolved without passing a draft law. In its final week it instead passed a Basic Law declaring Torah study a foundational state value, and a temporary law freezing enforcement against qualifying yeshiva students (v).
  • Also in 2026 — the Knesset Finance Committee advanced a UTJ-sponsored bill to restore daycare subsidies by barring the government from considering yeshiva-student status in eligibility decisions (v).

What this actually means, practically:

  1. The subsidy stack in §5.7 is not stable. Several of its components have already been cut for draft-eligible men, restored, re-cut, or frozen. A family budget built on the historical numbers is building on sand. Anyone modeling a move should model the post-sanction case, not the pre-sanction one, and treat any restoration as upside rather than as baseline.
  2. The legal exposure is real and now includes criminal proceedings, not only financial pressure. For a young man of draft age, or a family with sons approaching it, this is a first-order consideration and it requires actual current advice from someone qualified — not a document like this one.
  3. The Basic Law and the enforcement freeze are recent and provisional. A Basic Law is a strong statement of principle; a temporary enforcement freeze is a temporary enforcement freeze. Their durability depends entirely on the composition of the next government.
  4. The Knesset is dissolved and elections are ahead. Every number in §5.7 is, in effect, a political variable pending that outcome. This is the least predictable moment in decades to model charedi household economics in Israel.
  5. Second-order effects. Sanctions on subsidies push more charedi men toward the workforce, which is a large part of why the charedi tech track (§3.2) has grown. Whatever one’s view of the underlying machlokes, the labor-market direction of travel is clear, and the training infrastructure — KamaTech, Bizmax, charedi vocational programs — is where the growth is.

How to use this section. Not as a prediction. As a list of things to check the current status of before making a decision, because every one of them has changed at least once in the last twenty-four months.

5.9 Chinuch in Israel

The differences from America that catch olim off guard:

  • Registration is not automatic. Charedi schools screen, and acceptance depends on the family’s standing, the rav’s recommendation, and the school’s assessment of fit. A family that would be unremarkable in an American community can find itself negotiating for placement.
  • Anglo children need a landing plan. Some schools and neighborhoods handle new olim well — RBS, Har Nof, parts of Gush Etzion which explicitly cites high schools experienced with olim — and some do not.
  • Tuition is dramatically lower than the US. Most Israeli communities in the OU listing report under $10K equivalent, and much of the system is state-funded.
  • The tzibbur question is sharper. In America, the range within a single school is often wide. In Israel, schools are more finely sorted by kehilla, chassidus, and hashkafa, and switching is harder.
  • Boys’ and girls’ tracks diverge earlier, and the secular-studies content in charedi boys’ schools is substantially thinner than in American yeshivos — which is directly connected to the wage gap in §5.7.

5.10 Aliyah practicalities

  • Nefesh B’Nefesh handles most Anglo aliyah logistics and runs community-specific guidance, employment support, and post-aliyah programming.
  • Sal klita — the absorption basket, paid over the first months, plus tax benefits, customs exemptions, and reduced arnona for a period.
  • Health insurance via the kupot cholim (Clalit, Maccabi, Meuchedet, Leumit) — coverage from arrival, and structurally very different from and generally far cheaper than the American system.
  • Professional licensing — medicine, law, therapy, and accounting all require conversion processes of varying difficulty. Software does not, which is a large part of why it is the standard Anglo profession in Israel.
  • Time zone — Israel is 7 hours ahead of US Eastern. A US-employer remote job from Israel means afternoon-to-evening working hours, which collides with seder, with family time, and with the school day. This is the most common failure mode of “I’ll just keep my American job,” and it is worth planning around honestly before rather than after.

Addendum — Beyond the US and Eretz Yisroel

Roughly 85% of the world’s frum Jews live in the United States or Israel. The rest — call it the third world of frum life — is not a rounding error: it includes the largest yeshiva in Europe, one of the densest chassidish communities anywhere, and several communities with better cost structures than anything in the American Northeast. It also includes communities under real demographic and political pressure.

This addendum is shallower than Parts 4 and 5, because the data is thinner and less standardized. Treat it as orientation.

One cross-cutting fact. The Ichud HaKollelim directory reaches all of this: kollelim in Toronto (4), Thornhill, Montreal (4), London, Borehamwood, Leeds, Antwerp, Amsterdam, Basel, Berlin, Milan, Rome, Saint-Louis (France), Gibraltar, Cape Town, Johannesburg, Melbourne, and Mexico City. There is also a separate European Community-Kollel Network (ECKN) operating in the same space. Wherever there is a mid-sized frum community outside the US and Israel, there is usually a kollel in it, and it is usually the community’s Torah engine.

Canada

TORONTO and THORNHILL, ON The largest frum community in Canada, and the birthplace of the community-kollel model.

  • Geography: Everything runs along Bathurst Street, and Torontonians locate themselves as “down south” or “up north” along it. The older communities sit in North York (Bathurst/Lawrence, Bathurst/Sheppard); the growth has moved steadily north into Thornhill and, most recently, Thornhill Woods — which grew 614% between 2001 and 2011 (v). That northward migration is driven by exactly what drives it in America: newer houses, larger lots, lower prices.
  • Mosdos: Yeshivas Ner Yisroel of Toronto (est. 1959, Bathurst Glen, Thornhill), Netivot HaTorah Day School (PreK–8, 600+ students, Thornhill) (v), Eitz Chaim, Bnos Bais Yaakov, TanenbaumCHAT, Yeshivat Or Chaim / Ulpanat Orot, and a substantial chassidish school network.
  • Kollel: Kollel Ohr Yosef (601 Clark Avenue West, Thornhill) serves Thornhill and northern Toronto (v); Ichud also lists Forest Hill Community Kollel, Kollel Avreichim of Toronto, Kollel Oholei Yom Tov, and Toronto Sephardic Kollel. Toronto’s historical significance is worth stating plainly: the first community kollel in the world was established here in 1970 (v), and the entire model in Part 2 descends from it.
  • Character: Full spectrum — a large yeshivish community, a substantial chassidish population, a large Modern Orthodox community, a Sephardi community with its own kollel, and Chabad.
  • Housing: Expensive by North American standards; the Greater Toronto market is among the costliest on the continent (est.).
  • Parnassa: Canada’s financial and corporate capital. Note for Americans: cross-border work, tax, and immigration questions are non-trivial and require actual advice.

MONTREAL, QC Two communities that barely overlap, and the most dramatic demographic split in North America.

  • Geography: Côte Saint-Luc holds the largest Jewish population in the metro at 18,180; Hampstead has the highest density at 63.8% Jewish, Côte Saint-Luc at 53.9% (v). These are the Modern Orthodox and traditional communities. Separately, Outremont and Parc Extension hold the chassidish concentration — Satmar, Belz, Skver, Vizhnitz, Klausenberg — plus Tosh in Boisbriand, a self-contained chassidish village of over 2,400 people (v).
  • The demographic split is stark. Between 2011 and 2021, Outremont gained 1,560 Jews while Côte Saint-Luc lost 930 and Hampstead lost 890 (v). The median age of Montreal’s Jewish community overall is 39.6; the median age in the Outremont Jewish community is 17.6 (v). Charedim are now roughly a fifth of Greater Montreal’s Jewish population (v). One half of Montreal Jewry is aging and shrinking; the other is very young and growing fast.
  • Mosdos: Hebrew Academy (Côte Saint-Luc, 521 students), Yeshiva Gedola Merkaz Hatorah, Beth Jacob, Skver girls’ school in Outremont, Bais Yaakov, and a dense chassidish school network (v).
  • Kollel: Four in the Ichud directory — Kollel Kesser Torah (Montreal Community Kollel), Kollel Petach Tikvah, Kollel Pe’er HaTorah, and the Sephardic Kollel Avrechim Foundation – Kehillas Chouva Yisrael, reflecting Montreal’s large Moroccan and Sephardi community.
  • Cost: Housing and tuition are materially cheaper than Toronto or the American Northeast (est.) — the standing trade-off is Quebec’s language politics and tax load.

Smaller Canadian communities: Ottawa, Vancouver, Winnipeg, Hamilton, Calgary, Edmonton, and Halifax each have Orthodox shuls and, in several cases, day schools. All are small; verify the K–12 stack before considering any of them.

The United Kingdom

The demographic story first, because it dominates everything. Britain’s strictly Orthodox population is growing at roughly 3.5% per year against 0.2% for the rest of British Jewry (v, JPR). Estimates of current share vary — JPR has framed it as one in seven of all British Jews, rising to one in five by 2040, while other reporting cites around 80,000 charedim, about one in four, and projects 40% by 2040 (v — the sources disagree, and the discrepancy is about definitions of “strictly Orthodox,” not about the direction). Strictly Orthodox births rose 35% between 2007 and 2015 (1,431 to 1,932 per year) (v), on an estimated TFR around 7 children per woman (v). On current trends, around half of Jewish children born in the UK by 2031 will be charedi (v). Charedi growth has already reversed the overall decline of British Jewry.

STAMFORD HILL, LONDON The largest strictly Orthodox community in Europe.

  • Size: Approximately 25,000 charedim, about 10% of the Borough of Hackney, growing at roughly 5% per annum (v).
  • Character: Overwhelmingly chassidish — Satmar, Belz, Bobov, Vizhnitz, Ger, Skver, and many more — plus a Litvishe minority. Yiddish is widely spoken. Extremely dense, with old and expensive housing stock.
  • Mosdos: Yesodey Hatorah, Beis Chinuch, Lubavitch, and a very large network of chadarim and yeshivos. Upwards of 1,500 boys aged 13+ learn in yeshivos that are not registered as schools and therefore fall outside Ofsted inspection (v).
  • The live issue: UK schools legislation targeting unregistered settings is an active and serious conflict. The head of Gateshead Yeshiva has publicly told the strictly Orthodox community to leave the UK if the Schools Bill passes (v). Anyone considering a move to a British charedi community should treat the regulatory status of its yeshivos as a first-order question, not a background detail.

GOLDERS GREEN, HENDON, EDGWARE, BOREHAMWOOD, LONDON The Litvishe and Modern Orthodox side of London.

  • Character: Golders Green and Hendon are the established centres — a mix of yeshivish, Modern Orthodox, and a growing chassidish presence. Edgware and Borehamwood are the suburban expansion.
  • Mosdos: Hasmonean, Menorah, Beis Yaakov, Immanuel College, plus the yeshivos and seminaries.
  • Kollel: Ichud lists The London Chaburah and the Borehamwood Kollel (Kollel Toras Chanoch).
  • Housing: Flats in Golders Green Central run roughly £450,000–£700,000 depending on size and condition; borough-level annual house price growth hit 4.6% to early 2026 (v). Houses cost considerably more. London is expensive and the frum streets are the expensive part of it.

MANCHESTER — Broughton Park, Prestwich, Salford, Whitefield The second-largest UK frum community, and cheaper than London.

  • Character: Broughton Park is predominantly Litvishe charedi (v), with a large chassidish presence alongside; Prestwich and Whitefield are more mixed. A very substantial and self-sustaining community.
  • Mosdos: Manchester Yeshiva, Manchester Mesivta, Beis Yaakov Manchester, Broughton Jewish Cassel Fox, King David, and many more.
  • Cost: Materially cheaper than London on housing, which is the main reason for its growth (est.).

GATESHEAD A yeshiva town, in the fullest sense of the term.

  • Gateshead Talmudical College is the largest yeshiva in Europe and among the most prestigious advanced yeshivos in the Orthodox world (v). The Gateshead Seminary (the “Sem”) is equally significant on the girls’ side.
  • The community itself is small — roughly 350–400 families (v). Almost everything about the town is downstream of the mosdos.
  • Cost: Housing in the North East of England is among the cheapest in Britain (est.). Parnassa is the constraint.

Also: Leeds (Ichud lists the Leeds Community Kollel), Liverpool, Birmingham, and Gibraltar — a small, ancient, and complete community with its own kollel in the Ichud directory.

Continental Europe

ANTWERP, BELGIUM “The Jerusalem of the North.” The densest chassidish community in continental Europe.

  • Size: A Jewish community of roughly 20,000–25,000, of whom about 15,000 are charedi, many of them chassidish (v).
  • Infrastructure: 30 of Belgium’s 45 active synagogues are Orthodox and in Antwerp (v); directories list around 20 shuls, 13 kosher restaurants, 4 kosher stores, 4 caterers, and 3 mikvaos (v).
  • Character: Historically built around the diamond trade, which has contracted significantly — a real economic question for the community’s future. Belz, Satmar, Ger, Vizhnitz and Machzikei Hadass all present. Belgium’s Jewish schools are concentrated here and in Brussels.
  • Kollel: Kollel Antwerp HaChodosh (Ichud).

AMSTERDAM, NETHERLANDS

  • Roughly 15,000 Jews (v). The NIHS, an Orthodox Ashkenazi kehilla dating to 1635 with about 2,000 members, supports the full infrastructure — kashrus, shuls, mikveh, rabbanus, social services, cemeteries, and school subsidies (v). Buitenveldert holds an Orthodox shul, a Jewish elderly home, and three Jewish schools (v).
  • Kollel: Kollel Rabonim Rabbeinu Tzvi Ashkenazi (Ichud).

SWITZERLAND — Zurich, Basel, Geneva

  • Zurich ~6,800 Jews, Geneva ~4,400, Basel ~2,600 (v). Zurich holds three distinct communities each around its own shul: the Orthodox Israelitische Religionsgesellschaft (IRG), the Reform Or Chadasch, and a sizable chassidish community around Agudas Achim (v). A school, library, mikveh, and two kosher stores (v).
  • Kollel: Kollel Basel (Ichud).
  • Note: Very high cost of living, very high salaries, and a small but complete frum infrastructure.

VIENNA, AUSTRIA

  • The IKG has nearly 8,000 members; roughly 12,000 Jews live in Vienna (v). Four Jewish schools, kosher restaurants, and full availability of what a frum life requires (v). The charedi community operates independently with a separate school system and institutions (v) — largely Bucharian, Georgian, and chassidish.

BERLIN, MILAN, ROME

  • Small but real Orthodox communities, each with an Ichud kollel — The Berlin Kollel, Kollel Milano – Mesilat Yesharim, and Kollel Roma. Milan and Rome both have long-established Italian and Sephardi kehillos with their own minhagim.

FRANCE

  • Europe’s largest Jewish community at roughly 450,000 (estimates range 453,000–600,000) (v). Modern Orthodoxy is the predominant organized religious stream; roughly 7% are charedi (v). Heavily Sephardi, largely North African in origin.
  • Paris has more than 20 Jewish day schools plus kindergartens and seminaries; there are also Jewish schools in Strasbourg (a community of 16,000), Nice, Toulouse, Marseille, Bordeaux, Metz, and Aix-les-Bains (v) — the last of which is a yeshiva town in the Gateshead mould.
  • Kollel: Kollel Saint-Louis (Alsace) in the Ichud directory.
  • The trajectory matters. 3,300 French Jews made aliyah in 2025, a 45% increase over 2024, and surveys suggest 38% of French Jews — roughly 200,000 people — are actively considering leaving (v). Israel’s Aliyah Ministry closed 2025 with 21,900 olim overall, with growth specifically from France and the UK (v). Any assessment of a French community has to account for the possibility that a meaningful share of it will not be there in a decade.

Australia

MELBOURNE The largest and most institutionally complete frum community in the southern hemisphere.

  • Character: Centred on East St Kilda, Caulfield, and Ripponlea. Chabad is the dominant organized presence — the Yeshivah Centre operates Yeshivah College (boys, est. 1955, ~420 students), Beth Rivkah Ladies College (est. 1956), Kollel Menachem Lubavitch, and Ohel Chana seminary (v). Alongside it sits Adass Israel, a substantial and separate chassidish/charedi kehilla with its own mosdos (est.), plus Mizrachi, Yeshivah Gedolah, and a Sephardi community.
  • Kollel: Kollel Beth HaTalmud – Yehuda Fishman Institute (Ichud), a serious Litvishe beis medrash.
  • Considerations: Extremely far from everywhere else — flights to Israel, New York, or London are long and expensive, which matters for shidduchim, for family, and for sending children out to yeshiva or seminary. Housing in Melbourne’s Jewish suburbs is expensive.

SYDNEY

  • Centred on Bondi and the eastern suburbs. The Yeshiva Centre (Chabad, est. 1956, Flood Street, Bondi) and Yeshiva College Bondi (v), plus Moriah College, Kesser Torah, and the Sydney Community Kollel (v). Smaller and less charedi than Melbourne.

South Africa

  • Total Jewish population roughly 52,300 — Johannesburg ~30,000 (57.5%) and Cape Town ~12,500 (23.9%) (v).
  • Johannesburg is one of the most religiously observant Jewish communities in the diaspora by percentage. Yeshiva College, founded in the mid-1950s, was South Africa’s first and is now its largest Modern Orthodox day school; several more religious schools followed in the 1970s and 1980s, and by the 2020s Johannesburg had multiple yeshivos and kollelim across the charedi spectrum (v). Kollel Yad Shaul, Ohr Somayach, Aish, and Chabad have all been major forces in the community’s shift toward observance since the 1970s (v).
  • Kollel: Kollel Toras Chaim (Johannesburg) and the Cape Town Kollel (both Ichud).
  • Cape Town’s Orthodox community is contracting, and not primarily through emigration. Orthodox marriages fell from an average of 64 per year (2002–06) to about 40 (2013–17), and annual births from 111 (2005–10) to 75 in 2017 (v). That is a birth-rate story, and it is the opposite of the pattern everywhere else in this document.
  • Considerations: Emigration, currency, security, and infrastructure are standing concerns, and they show up in every honest conversation about South African Jewish life.

Latin America

  • Mexico City — About 40,000 Jews nationally, the world’s 14th-largest community (2020) (v). Unusually, Mexican Jewry is organized by country of origin rather than by denomination: Monte Sinai (Damascus and Lebanon), Maguen David (Aleppo), Comunidad Ashkenazi (Central and Eastern Europe), and Comunidad Sefaradi (Turkey, Greece, the Balkans) (v). Observance levels are high by diaspora standards, and the community is unusually wealthy and unusually insulated — security is a defining daily consideration. A charedi “City of Torah” has been planned for Mexico, which would be the first purpose-built charedi city in Latin America (v). Kollel Ohr HaTalmud is in the Ichud directory.
  • Panama City — Small but remarkably complete. Numerous Orthodox shuls, and kosher food, catering, and ritual services described as rivaled only by Israel and the US (v). Heavily Sephardi, heavily observant, and wealthy.
  • Buenos Aires — The largest Jewish community in Latin America (v), with a substantial Orthodox sector including Chabad, Sephardi kehillos (Syrian and Moroccan), and yeshivos. Argentina’s economic volatility is the standing constraint.
  • São Paulo — The second-largest in Latin America (v) (population figures vary widely between sources and should be verified). Growing Orthodox institutions, including recently opened schools running at capacity with waiting lists (v).

How to read this addendum

Three patterns run through all of it:

  1. The charedi share is rising everywhere, and fast. Britain at 3.5% annual growth against 0.2%, Montreal’s Outremont at a median age of 17.6, Stamford Hill at 5% a year. Communities that look stable in aggregate are usually two communities — one shrinking and one growing — and the aggregate hides it.
  2. Kollel is the transplant mechanism. Almost every one of these communities has a kollel, and in most of them the kollel is the reason there is still Torah infrastructure. The Ichud/BMG model that began in Toronto in 1970 has been exported to five continents.
  3. The specific risks are different from America’s, and they are not financial. In Britain it is school regulation. In France it is security and a population that is actively considering leaving. In South Africa it is emigration and infrastructure. In Latin America it is security and currency. In Australia it is distance. None of these show up in a housing comparison, and all of them matter more than one.

Part 6 — Comparative tables

6.1 US communities — housing by segment, plus the taxes

Why each community lists a different number of prices. A community’s housing stock is not one market. Detached single-family homes are only 34.46% of Lakewood’s units (v) — the rest is townhouses, duplexes and 3,500-plus units of 55-plus senior housing, so any “Lakewood median” is mostly measuring that two-thirds. Meanwhile Passaic Park, the frum neighborhood, runs a median real estate price of $856,367 against Passaic city at $543–555K (v). One number per community cannot describe either place, and forcing every community into the same number of tiers is the same mistake in a different shape. So each row below lists only the segments that genuinely exist there. Lakewood needs five. Brookline and South Bend need one. The count is itself information.

Labels: Entry = cheapest realistic way in · Attached = townhouse/duplex/co-op · All-types = a blended figure, flagged because it understates houses · Family = detached 4–5BR on the frum blocks, the number that matters · New = new construction · Upper = top of the local market.

Confidence: (v) verified against a named market source, reading date given · (comp.) computed from a verified rate in the same row · (est.) inferred from local $/ft² · (s-r) self-reported — the community’s own recruitment copy, undated and not independently checked.

The (s-r) tier is wrong in a known direction, which is the useful part. Asked what homes cost, a community answers with the cheapest realistic way in, because that is the answer that attracts families; the figure then lands in a Family column that asked a different question. It also carries no date, where every (v) reading here does, so it ages downward in a market that has risen. Twelve (s-r) prices have now been checked against the market: five were materially low, five held, two were bands too wide to be wrong — and not one came back high. The failures were the point estimates and the narrow bands — Northeast Philadelphia’s $350,000 against $389–444K, Omaha’s $155,400 against $289–307K, Nashville’s $800K–1M against a $1.1M ZIP median, Plano’s $400–500K against the verified $/ft² printed beside it. The ones that held were bands wide enough to span most of their own level: South Bend’s $175–350K has a top double its bottom, and a band that wide contains the answer without asserting anything — while the reader anchors on the bottom, which is the number the community chose to lead with. San Antonio’s $200–399K illustrates both failures at once, since its top turns out to be the market median exactly. So read an (s-r) point estimate as a floor, and an (s-r) band as its top. Earlier drafts wrote these cells as (v, self-rep.), which asserted verification of a source Appendix B disqualifies; see weakness 15. If you know a market directly, your number beats mine.

Read the income tax column carefully. It shows the top marginal rate, which applies to the last dollar of a very high income — New Jersey’s 10.75% starts above $1 million. At $150,000 the ranking is different and sometimes reversed: Indiana costs such a family ~$7,050 and New Jersey ~$5,500, because Indiana adds a county tax and New Jersey’s graduated schedule never reaches its top band. §3.6 has the effective rates at real frum-family incomes; use that table for anything below roughly $300,000. Property tax, by contrast, is directly comparable — it is charged on the asset regardless of income.

Arnona in one paragraph. Israel’s municipal tax is charged per square meter of the apartment, not as a percentage of value — so it does not scale with the market the way American property tax does, and it is far cheaper. Jerusalem runs ₪40.40 to ₪113.22 per m² per year; Beit Shemesh ₪41.26 (older neighborhoods) to ₪47.48 (newer), which is roughly ₪3,500–5,000/year on a 100 m² apartment (v). Rates rose 1.626% nationally in 2026 (v). Compare that to $10,000–27,000 a year in the American communities in §6.1: arnona is close to a rounding error by US standards.

Two discount points that matter for a kollel family. New olim receive a 90% arnona discount for 12 months (v), and Jerusalem, Beit Shemesh and Modiin Illit run local top-up programs reaching 90–95% for large families (v). Kollel students can apply on income grounds — but the rules changed: eligibility is now assessed on average income over the past year rather than three months of payslips, which specifically disadvantages avreichim whose income is irregular (v). That is a small, concrete instance of the broader squeeze described in §5.8.

Part 7 — How to actually evaluate a community

Marketing materials answer the questions that are easy to answer well. Here are the ones that decide whether a move holds.

Before the visit

  1. The K–12 question. Not “is there a school” — is there a school for each of my children, in each of the next fifteen years, that I would actually choose? Get the enrollment numbers per grade, not the total. A school with 200 students across PreK–8 has about 22 per grade, which means one class, which means no fit alternative if it goes badly.
  2. The high school question, specifically. If there is no local mesivta or Bais Yaakov, where do people send? Is dorming normal here, and at what age? What does that cost?
  3. The tuition question, in dollars. Ask the school, not the community: what does a family with my income and my number of children actually pay, after scholarship, after voucher or ESA? Get a number.
  4. The parnassa question, for both of you. Named employers, not sectors. If remote: who else here works remotely, and can I speak to them?
  5. The exit question. If this doesn’t work out in three years, what does leaving cost? A house in a small community is far less liquid than a house in Lakewood.

On the pilot Shabbos

  1. Go to a weekday Maariv, not just the Shabbos davening. Shabbos always looks good. Tuesday night tells you what the community actually is.
  2. Count the young families in shul. Then ask how many arrived in the last three years and how many left. Communities always report the first number and rarely the second.
  3. Ask who is carrying the load. In a small community, a handful of people run everything. Find out who, how old they are, and what happens when they stop.
  4. Ask about the machlokes. Every community has one. A community that claims otherwise is either hiding it or hasn’t noticed it. What you want to know is whether it’s the kind you can live around.
  5. Ask the women. Separately, and about their own lives — childcare, work, isolation, whether they have friends here or just neighbors. This is the variable that most often determines whether a family stays, and it is the least represented in any relocation material.
  6. Check the shidduch reality. For a community under a few hundred families: where do the children go to date, who pays for it, and does the community have a functioning network or just good intentions?

For a kollel position specifically

  1. Get the whole package in writing. Stipend, tuition remission, housing, health insurance, moving costs, term length, and who your employer actually is.
  2. Talk to the yungerman who left. Not the one who stayed. The kollel will introduce you to the latter; find the former yourself.
  3. Ask what the community expects of you beyond learning — how many chavrusas, how many shiurim, whether you’re expected to teach in the school, and what happens if you say no.
  4. Ask what the exit looks like. Do yungerleit here transition into local jobs, or do they leave town? If they leave, the community is a waystation, and that’s fine — as long as you know it going in.

For Israel specifically

  1. Verify the current status of every subsidy in §5.7 and every sanction in §5.8 before you model anything. All of it has moved in the past two years, and the Knesset is dissolved.
  2. Get real advice on draft status for yourself and for sons approaching draft age, from someone qualified and current. Not from a document, and not from a forum.
  3. Confirm school placement before you confirm the apartment. In that order, and in writing where possible.
  4. Model the mortgage under inflation and rate stress, not at today’s payment.
  5. If you plan to keep an American job, sit down and actually write out a weekday: what time you start, what time you finish, where seder goes, when you see your children. Then decide.

Known weaknesses in this document

  1. OU profiles are self-authored recruitment copy. Every community in that dataset is trying to attract families. Amenity lists are accurate (they’re checkboxes); the narratives are optimistic; the housing and tuition bands are what the community believes is achievable, not the average outcome.
  2. The Ichud directory undercounts. It lists 112 against a reported 125 members, and it covers only one network.
  3. Neither dataset covers Lakewood, Brooklyn, Kiryas Joel, or the chassidishe world properly, because those communities have no reason to appear in relocation directories. Those sections here are correspondingly thinner and lean more on general reporting.
  4. The housing figures were rebuilt three times, and the final structure is the admission. Version one quoted the OU directory’s “Affordable Homes/Co-ops” bands — the entry rung — in a column headed “Homes.” Version two replaced them with neighborhood-wide price-per-square-foot, still too low, because such figures blend condos, co-ops and two-to-four-family stock in with houses. Version three used single-family figures where they existed but mixed units of analysis — comparing a township that contains retirement villages (Lakewood) against a frum neighborhood (Passaic Park) against an affluent sub-neighborhood that is not where the local frum community lives (Squirrel Hill North) — and produced the absurd result that Lakewood was cheaper than both. The underlying problem is that no single number can describe a community’s housing, because the stock is not one market: detached single-family homes are only 34.46% of Lakewood’s units. §6.1 therefore now lists however many segments genuinely exist in each community — entry, attached, family house, new, upper — each labelled with what it measures. Lakewood needs five; Brookline and South Bend need one. The family-house column is the one that matters and the one public aggregators are worst at isolating; several of its cells are marked (est.) and are the weakest figures in this document. Anyone with direct knowledge of a local market should trust their own number over mine.
  5. An earlier draft inferred house sizes by dividing a median price by a median $/ft². That is invalid — they are medians of two different, differently-contaminated distributions, and the quotient means nothing. It produced the claim that Monsey’s typical house is about 1,720 ft²; actual Monsey listings run 4,700–5,000 ft², and the published $580/ft² is the attached/condo rate, not the house rate (houses are nearer $215/ft²). All derived square footages have been removed; where a size appears now, a source stated it.
  6. An earlier draft multiplied a per-child tuition band by five children. That is not how schools price. Sibling discounts are standard, and many schools cap total family tuition at 12–18% of AGI regardless of how many children attend (v). The error inflated the expensive communities specifically — because that is where caps and scholarship do the most work — and it drove a headline claim of an “$85,000 a year” gap that was roughly three times too large at a $150,000 income. §3.5 and §3.6 are rebuilt; the corrected finding is that the gap is modest at kollel-family incomes and large at professional ones, because voucher states do not means-test and scholarship states do.
  7. An earlier draft used top marginal income tax rates to describe families earning $150,000. New Jersey’s 10.75% applies above $1 million. At $150,000 the effective ranking is different and partly reversed: Indiana ~$7,050 (state plus county), New Jersey ~$5,500, Ohio ~$3,400. The top-rate column survives in §6.1 because it is correct for high earners, but §3.6 now carries effective rates at real frum-family incomes and §6.1 points to it.
  8. US housing medians are metro- or neighborhood-level, not frum-street-level. Frum blocks generally price above their surrounding median. Some year-over-year swings quoted (West Rogers Park at +50%, Amberley Village swinging between $435K and $802K within 2026, Pico-Robertson at −35%) reflect thin transaction volume rather than real market moves, and are flagged in place.
  9. Property tax figures mix verified bills with computed estimates. Where a rate is marked (v) it is sourced; where a dollar figure is marked (comp.) it is that rate applied to the home price in the same row; where marked (est.) the rate itself is my estimate. Several commercial aggregators compute “effective rate” against assessed rather than market value and produce impossible numbers — one lists Edison, NJ at 5.92% — so verified dollar bills are preferred over rates throughout.
  10. The 2,800 ft² reference house is a convention, not a claim. Where it appears, it means “a house of that size, at this community’s verified rate, would cost X” — nothing more. It is not an assertion that local houses are that size, and in several communities they plainly are not.
  11. Kollel stipend figures are the weakest numbers in this document. No authoritative survey exists. Treat the ranges in §2.4 as orientation, not as data.
  12. Israeli policy figures have a short shelf life. Everything in §5.7 and §5.8 has changed at least once in the past two years, the Knesset dissolved in July 2026, and elections are ahead. Re-verify before relying on any of it.
  13. The Addendum is thinner than Parts 4 and 5, and knowingly so. Diaspora community statistics outside the US and Israel are collected irregularly, by different bodies, on different definitions. Where UK sources disagree on the charedi share of British Jewry (one in seven versus one in four), that disagreement is reported rather than resolved, because it turns on definitions this document is not equipped to adjudicate.
  14. Population figures for frum communities specifically are almost never measured. Municipal census data captures the whole town; Jewish federation studies capture the whole Jewish community. Where a frum-specific number appears here, it is an estimate unless marked otherwise.
  15. The self-reported housing figures were never held to the standard the rest of §6.1 was, and they are either low or unfalsifiable. Weakness 4 describes the fix: stop printing the entry rung in a column headed Family. That fix was applied wherever a market source was pulled to replace the community’s own number — and only there. Where no market source was pulled, the version-one figure stayed in place and inherited the version-three label, carrying the composite marker (v, self-rep.), which asserts verification of exactly the source weakness 1 disqualifies. The problem was never the method. It was that the method was applied to a convenience sample. The bias is one-directional, and four things push the same way. A community asked what homes cost answers with the cheapest realistic way in, because that is what attracts families. The answer is undated, in a market that has risen. It carries no $/ft², and $/ft² is this document’s contamination detector everywhere else — it is how Monsey’s condo rate and Southfield’s small houses were caught, and it is absent from precisely the rows that needed it. And where the price is wrong the property tax cell, computed from it, is wrong by the same factor while being marked as though independently sourced. All twelve have now been checked, and the result corrects a claim an earlier version of this appendix made. Materially low (five): Northeast Philadelphia $350,000 → $389–444K all-types, $500–550K detached; Bensalem “from $350,000” → $409,777–450,000; Omaha $155,400 → $289,000–306,750, a figure that was roughly half the market and that nothing in the row’s structure — no segments, no date, no $/ft², just the words “single market” — was capable of catching; Plano $400–500K → ~$550,000, refuted not by new research but by the verified $220/ft² printed in the same cell; and Nashville $800K–1M → $1,100,000, the ZIP median for 37205 across 464 sales. Held (five): Louisville, Norfolk (Larchmont-Edgewater $450–534K), Indianapolis (ZIP 46260 at $296–326K), South Bend (city $185–190K), Springfield city ($309–319K). Too wide to be wrong (two): San Antonio’s $200–399K, whose top turns out to be the ZIP median exactly, and the combined Jacksonville/Orlando/Tampa $200–599K, which spanned three metros and every segment at once. An earlier version of this entry said every checked figure moved up. Four had. Of twelve, five did, five held, and two were unfalsifiable. The direction never reversed — nothing came back high — and the pattern in the failures is sharper than the original claim was. The cells that broke were point estimates and narrow bands. The cells that held were bands wide enough to span most of their own level; South Bend’s top is double its bottom, which contains the answer without asserting anything. That is not accuracy, and the reader anchors on the bottom of a band, which is the number the community led with. Read an (s-r) point as a floor and an (s-r) band as its top. The only prices still unchecked are the flanks of the East Denver row, whose centre is verified. The consequence reaches past those rows. Measurement effort in §6.1 correlates with price. Part of the in-town versus out-of-town cost gap this document describes is therefore an artifact of which markets got looked up, and every correction so far has run upward, concentrated in the rows nobody looked up. Expect the next one to do the same.

What this document deliberately does not do

It does not rank communities, recommend one, or argue about how the housing market ought to work. A previous version of this material included proposals for restructuring the Israeli land and construction system; those have been removed as out of scope. This is a description of what exists.

Appendix C — Corrections to the original Orthodox Real Estate Matrix

This primer replaces an earlier document, The Orthodox Real Estate Matrix: Comparing the US and Israeli Markets. Its factual claims were checked; most of the descriptive material held up. The following did not, and is corrected or dropped here. (Corrections to this document’s own earlier drafts are in Appendix B, not here.)

1. The Israeli price conversion is arithmetically wrong. The earlier draft stated a national baseline of “roughly $770 per sq. ft. (₪24,000 per sq. meter).” Those two figures are not the same number. ₪24,000/m² ÷ 10.764 ft²/m² = ₪2,230 per sq. ft., which at recent exchange rates of roughly 3.6–3.7 ₪/$ is about $600–620 per sq. ft. The stated \(770 implies an exchange rate near 2.9 ₪/\), which has not been the rate. The dollar figure overstates by roughly 25%, and every US-vs-Israel comparison built on it inherits the error. The ₪24,000/m² side of it is a reasonable national ballpark; the dollar side is not.

2. “Willowbrook is the most affordable established Modern Orthodox enclave within the five boroughs” — no. Staten Island’s 2026 median is $755K (v); Kew Gardens Hills’ average home value is $467,848 (v, Zillow 2026). Kew Gardens Hills is substantially cheaper and is an established community by any measure.

3. The out-of-town price-per-square-foot figures are stale. The draft gave Cleveland at $115–160/ft² and Detroit at $115–155/ft². Cleveland Heights’ median is now $223K, up 21.6% year-over-year, and Oak Park, MI is $266K, up 26.1% (v, 2026). On typical house sizes those medians land above the quoted bands. The Midwest is still cheap; it is not as cheap as those figures suggest, and it moved sharply in the last year.

4. The ILA figure is correct. The Israel Land Authority does control roughly 93% of Israel’s land (v). That part stands, and it is retained in §5.6.

5. Linden, NJ was over-weighted. It appears in the draft as one of three named “high-density budget commuter hubs.” It is a real but small community; Passaic, Elizabeth, Hillside, Bayonne, and Highland Park/Edison are all larger and more institutionally complete, and are covered here instead.

6. The framing treated “Orthodox” as meaning “Modern Orthodox.” The draft’s US section was organized around Modern Orthodox strongholds — Five Towns, Surfside, Pico-Robertson — with yeshivish and chassidish communities appearing only as parenthetical notes (“while sections are strictly Yeshivish or Chassidic…”). Lakewood, Brooklyn, Monsey, Kiryas Joel, Baltimore, and Passaic — collectively the large majority of American frum Jews — were absent or marginal. That framing is not used here.

7. Parts 3 and 4 of the draft are dropped as out of scope. Those sections proposed mechanisms to bring Israeli housing prices down toward American levels: breaking the ILA land monopoly, subsidizing modular construction, high-speed rail decentralization, corporate build-to-rent, and applying sovereignty in Yehuda and Shomron. Whatever their merits, they are policy argument rather than community reference. The underlying factual observations that are useful to a family — the ILA’s control of land supply, the concrete-and-stone construction requirement and its effect on build times, and the mortgage structure — are retained in §5.6 without the accompanying proposals.

8. The draft’s deep-periphery price range is retained qualitatively but not numerically. Its figure of $175–240/ft² (₪6,800–9,000/m²) for Itamar, Elon Moreh, Har Bracha, Shilo, Eli, Otniel and Susya could not be independently verified for 2026. The structural point — that the deep periphery is the cheapest detached housing in the country, and that the discount reflects distance, infrastructure, and legal and security conditions — is retained in §5.5 without the specific numbers.
