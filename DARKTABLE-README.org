* Start here

*What darktable is.* A free, open-source raw photo editor. It takes the raw
file off your camera and turns it into a finished photograph. It does the job
that the Develop module of Adobe Lightroom Classic does — and several things
that program cannot do — for zero money, with your files staying on your own
disk.

*The single most important property.* It is *non-destructive*. Your raw file is
never modified. An edit in darktable is a list of instructions — "exposure
+0.7, shadows lifted, these spots healed" — stored beside the photo, not baked
into it. You can reopen a photograph five years later, disagree with every
decision you made, and change any of them. Internalise this, because half the
program's design follows from it.

*The workflow in one breath.* Import photos into the library (lighttable) →
cull them down to the keepers → develop each keeper in the darkroom → export
finished JPEGs/TIFFs. Four stages. Everything in this guide hangs off them.

*The one honest warning before you start.* darktable does everything a raw
editor should do and several things most cannot. But almost nothing is called
what you would guess. Contrast lives inside ~color balance rgb~. Presets are
called styles. Clarity is called ~local contrast~. This is the whole learning
curve, and it is vocabulary rather than difficulty. Section 8 is the
dictionary; read it once and the fog lifts.

*Time budget.* Install plus settings: twenty minutes. Fluent with eight
sliders: two weeks if you shoot regularly. Reading this document end to end:
one evening, and it replaces most of that two weeks.

*How to read this.* First time through: sections 1–12 in order, doing each step
at your machine as you reach it. After that it is a reference — the contents
links jump straight to any task.

*Who this assumes you are.* A working photographer — exposure, colour, raw
files and histograms need no explanation here. What is explained from zero is
the program itself: every concept, term, menu path and click sequence, however
basic. If you have never opened darktable before, nothing below assumes that
you have.

* 1. What a raw file actually is

You do not strictly need this section to operate the software. You need it to
understand why the software is shaped the way it is, which is what stops the
vocabulary problem from feeling arbitrary. Ten minutes.

** The sensor records three incomplete pictures

A digital sensor is a grid of photosites. Each photosite measures one thing
only: how many photons hit it during the exposure. It has no idea what colour
anything is. Colour is faked with a mosaic: a filter sits over the sensor so
that each photosite sees only red, green, or blue light — roughly one blue,
one red, and two green sites per cluster, because human vision leans on green.
This arrangement is called a *Bayer pattern*, and your D7200 uses one.

So the raw file off the card is a single-channel mosaic of brightness numbers
plus metadata (camera model, lens, ISO, shutter, aperture, whitebalance guess).
It is not yet an image. It is the ingredients of one.

** Demosaicing

*Demosaicing* is the computation that invents full colour information at every
pixel by blending each site's reading with its neighbours' readings. Different
algorithms trade fine detail against artifacts:

- *RCB* (*ratio-corrected bilinear*-family; darktable's default is RCD) — fast,
  excellent detail, occasionally fabricates coloured fringes on repeating
  patterns.
- *VNG4* — older, softer, far more resistant to those fringes. This becomes
  relevant for moiré on the D7200; see section 18.
- Others exist (AMaZE, markesteijn for X-Trans); irrelevant for your Bayer
  camera beyond these two.

You change this in the ~demosaic~ module. Ninety-five percent of frames: never
touch it.

** Bit depth, and why your raw looks flat when you open it

Each photosite number is stored with 12-bit precision on the D7200: 4,096
levels of brightness per channel, concentrated on the shadow side (sensors
are linear; half of those levels describe the darkest stop alone). A JPEG has
8 bits: 256 levels, distributed the way a monitor expects them.

When you open a raw file, darktable shows you a *neutral interpretation*: flat
contrast, muted colour. That is correct behaviour — nobody has yet decided how
to spend those 4,096 levels. Your job in the darkroom is largely to spend
them: place the tones, choose the contrast curve, decide saturation. The
flatness is headroom, not a defect. (Why raw files look flat before you touch
them is covered from the capture side in ~D7200-GUIDE.org~.)

** Non-destructive editing, precisely

When you drag the exposure slider, darktable does not alter one byte of the
raw file. It writes a parameter — exposure = +0.70 — into an *edit history*
stored in two places: darktable's central database, and (if enabled, and it
should be) a small text file next to the photo called an *XMP sidecar*, named
like your raw but with an ~.xmp~ extension.

Consequences worth having in your bones:

- The original negative survives every edit forever. There is no such thing as
  ruining a photo beyond recovery — reset the history and it is untouched.
- Sidecar files make edits portable and backup-able. Back up the raw files
  together with their sidecars and your entire body of work — images and all
  decisions — survives any disaster, reinstall, or database loss.
- Two programs can disagree about sidecars. If you ever run Lightroom over the
  same folder, see section 22 before letting them near each other.

** Scene-referred versus display-referred

Two philosophies for how an editor should process your photo:

- *Display-referred* (old way, most phone apps): assume the image starts out
  looking right on a screen, then tweak from there. Simple, but it caps how
  far highlights and shadows can be pushed before falling apart.
- *Scene-referred* (darktable's native philosophy): treat the image as
  measurements of light in the scene, do all corrections in that space, and
  only at the very end compress the scene's enormous brightness range into
  what a screen can show. That final compression is done by a *display
  transform* module — for you, ~sigmoid~.

You chose scene-referred in settings (next-but-one section) and will mostly
never think about it again. What it buys you: highlight recovery and shadow
lifting that behave like film, without the grey murk old HDR tools produced.

** Colour management in three sentences

Your camera tags files with a colour space; sRGB is the safe universal one.
Your monitor has its own profile, which Windows stores centrally. darktable
uses both to translate faithfully between them. The only thing you must do is
make sure darktable reads the system monitor profile (section 4) so the same
file shows the same colours in every program.

* 2. Why darktable and not something else

Four free raw editors are worth taking seriously. Here is why the other three
lost, and when you should reconsider.

** RawTherapee 5.13

The best demosaicing and detail extraction of anything free, arguably of
anything at any price. But its local editing never matured into a real masking
system — "selective editing" exists, but it is limited compared to what
darktable or ART offer. If your editing is mostly global adjustments and you
chase maximum micro-detail, RawTherapee is defensible. For mask-driven work,
disqualified.

** Ansel

A fork of darktable by Aurélien Pierre, who wrote several of darktable's best
modules. His critique — darktable is over-engineered, drowning in options — is
fair, and he stripped tens of thousands of lines to prove it. But there has
been *no stable release since December 2023*; it forks the darktable 4.0
codebase; and edit histories diverge from mainline darktable at 4.2+. Frozen
at 4.0 means none of the recent years' work: no neural denoise, no AI object
mask, no sigmoid refinements. A faster, leaner darktable from four years ago is
still four years ago.

** ART

RawTherapee's pragmatic cousin: cleaner interface, genuinely good masks. Its
AI denoising and masking are bolted on as shell scripts, each needing its own
Python environment — on Windows that means MSys2 or WSL2. On paper strong, in
practice a science project rather than a workflow.

** The case for darktable, compressed

The only free raw editor with a complete masking system *and* local AI, on a
real release schedule, with a real development team, whose edits survive
version upgrades.

** When the honest answer is "pay Adobe"

Three things darktable will not do, stated plainly so you never waste an
evening hunting for them:

1. *Depth-aware background blur.* Its lens blur is flat — see section 12.
2. *Generative anything.* No sky replacement, no object insertion. This is
   deliberate project policy ("AI may correct technical defects, but may not
   change what was in front of the camera"), not a missing feature.
3. *An ecosystem* — phone sync, cloud, social plumbing. Files stay local and
   plain.

If any of those become load-bearing for you, that is the moment to look at
Adobe; ~LIGHTROOM-GUIDE.org~ in this folder covers that path thoroughly.

* 3. Install

*Time: 5 minutes.*

1. Download darktable 5.6 for Windows from https://www.darktable.org/install/
2. Run the installer. Nothing to decide inside it.
3. Launch it once, click around, then quit. First launch creates your
   configuration folder and database (where they live, and how to back them
   up: section 20).

*Updating between releases.* darktable edits are forward-compatible in
practice; still, before a major upgrade (5.x → 6.x someday), let the program
write all sidecars, copy the database file, then upgrade. Two minutes of
paranoia, years of calm.

** Optional but useful: Nikon NX Studio

Free from Nikon's site. Do not edit in it — its editing tools are crude. Use
it as a *reference renderer*: it is the only software that reproduces Nikon's
Picture Controls exactly as the camera intended them. When an edit has drifted
somewhere strange and you cannot tell which way is up, open the same raw in NX
Studio, see what Nikon thinks the frame should look like, and re-anchor
yourself.

** OpenCL, or: why darktable feels slow until you flip one switch

*OpenCL* is darktable using your GPU for image processing instead of the CPU.
On a 24-megapixel body this is the difference between snappy and wading
through mud. It is off by default on Windows.

Enable it in preferences (section 4), then verify which GPU it actually grabbed
— machines with two GPUs habitually get handed the wrong one, and nobody tells
you. This laptop carries an Intel Iris Xe (the CPU's built-in graphics) and an
NVIDIA T550 (4 GB). You want the T550 doing the work; the release notes'
documented advice for low-memory GPUs like this one shapes several settings
below.

If darktable ever crashes on opening an image after enabling OpenCL, the fix is
to turn it back off — a few drivers misbehave. The program is fully usable
CPU-only; merely slower.

* 4. Settings that matter

*Time: 10 minutes.* Open preferences — gear icon, top right of the lighttable,
or ~Preferences~ in the application menu. Every pane exists; these matter.

** Processing pane

Three decisions, two of which are confirmations:

- *auto-apply pixel workflow defaults:* = scene-referred (sigmoid). This is
  already the default in 5.x; confirm it. Sigmoid compresses the scene into
  display range with minimal fuss. The alternatives — filmic rgb and AgX —
  offer more controls for people who want to engineer their own highlight roll;
  sigmoid gives you 90% of the result with none of the fighting. You can move
  up later, or never.
- *auto-apply chromatic adaptation defaults:* = modern. Also the default;
  confirm. ("Chromatic adaptation" is the white-balance machinery; modern uses
  a more accurate method than legacy.)
- *pixel interpolator:* = bicubic. Safe choice for zooming/rotating previews.

** Performance pane

- Tick *OpenCL support*. Then check the log line or the OpenCL identification
  sub-pane: confirm the device listed is the *T550*, not Intel graphics. If
  both appear, force the NVIDIA one via the scheduling profile drop-down.
- Under *darktable resources*, raise memory limits to generous. With 48 GB of
  RAM there is no reason to be timid; the defaults assume machines from a
  poorer age.

** AI pane

darktable 5.6 added local AI: interactive masking models and neural denoise/
upscale. Fresh installs ship with AI switched off. Set-up, four steps:

1. Tick *enable AI features*. Takes effect immediately, no restart. When
   disabled, no ONNX runtime loads and nothing AI-related happens at all.
2. Click *download/update default*. One click fetches the recommended model
   set (~600 MB total; the individual files are listed with sizes and provenance
   in the panel).
3. *No GPU setup needed on Windows.* DirectML support comes bundled and runs
   on any DirectX 12 GPU, including the T550. Ignore the CUDA install script
   unless DirectML proves too slow — CUDA needs a separate ONNX Runtime
   download and buys very little here. If a model ever exceeds what the GPU
   can hold, darktable automatically falls back to CPU rather than failing.
4. Set the object-mask model to *SAM 2.1 tiny*, not base-plus. Tiny is the
   documented recommendation for low-memory systems and this one has 4 GB.
   If tiny ever fails to cleanly separate a subject, try *small* next, and
   only then base-plus. A third family, SegNext, exists as an alternative when
   SAM behaves badly on some subject; ignore until needed.

All inference runs locally. In the project's own words: *"Nothing leaves your
machine. No cloud inference, no telemetry."*

** Storage / XMP sidecars

Search "xmp" in the preferences search box. Set sidecar writing so every edit
is written to disk beside the photo, not just held in the database. Sidecars
are your insurance policy (section 20) and your passport if you ever move to
another library tool. While you are there, note the metadata options for
embedding copyright/contact info into exports — worth filling in once.

** Interface odds worth five minutes

- *Font size* in darkroom/global: bump up if module labels feel cramped.
- Everything under *shortcuts* is remappable; section 19 covers the ones worth
  knowing first.

* 5. A tour of the views

darktable's top-right (or top-centre) view switcher shows five faces of the
same program. Two you will live in; three you should know exist.

** Lighttable

The library. Photo grid, folders/collections on the left, tools along the top
and right. Importing, culling, rating, comparing, batch operations, export —
all here. Sections 6, 7 and 16 cover it.

** Darkroom

One photo, full attention, the editing modules. Sections 9–12.

** Map

Photos with GPS coordinates land on a map (the D7200 has no internal GPS, but
phone-shot JPEGs and GPS logs do). Drag photos onto the map to tag locations by
hand. Niche; pleasant when you want it.

** Print

A print-layout module feeding a system printer directly: images placed on
paper definitions with margins, borders and per-image text, colour-managed via
the same profiles as export. Workflow: select image(s) → print view → choose
printer/paper in the left panel's printer settings → set media/profile to match
→ layout panel sets size/margins/alignment. For controlled home printing
without leaving the program; for lab submissions, export TIFFs instead
(section 16). When you print seriously at home, revisit; the module is
straightforward once the ICC profile of your paper exists.

** Slideshow and tethering

Slideshow needs no explanation.

*Tethering* is studio capture: camera connected over USB, shots appearing on
the monitor within seconds of firing — client-visible, focus-checkable at 100%,
card-free. The D7200 tethers fine (Nikon's own hotends aside, darktable speaks
PTP natively).

A session, concretely:

1. Plug USB into camera and machine; power the camera from mains if the shoot
   is long (tethering drains batteries faster than shooting does).
2. Camera dial to P/A/S/M as usual; *set the camera to its normal card-writing*
   (tethering mirrors files, it does not replace storage).
3. In darktable switch to the *tethering* view; it detects the body and shows
   a live session workspace.
4. Create a *session*: a named working folder (date+job pattern) where captured
   frames land and auto-import into a filmstrip as they arrive.
5. Shoot. Each frame appears within a second or two; click any to inspect at
   100% for focus, or apply a quick style to preview the look live.
6. Afterwards the session folder imports like any other job (section 6).

Where this matters: product shots, headshots, anything with a client at your
shoulder saying "let me see". Where it doesn't: everything else — the card
reader workflow loses nothing except the live monitor.

* 6. Importing and the lighttable

** Getting files off the card

Two routes:

- *copy & import* — darktable copies from the card into your photo folder
  structure and registers them in the library. Use this with the card reader.
- *add to library* — registers files already sitting somewhere on disk. Use
  this when you have copied files yourself (or are adopting an old archive).

Either way the photos are not moved *into* darktable — there is no such place.
The library is a database pointing at plain files in plain folders. Delete
darktable tomorrow and your folder tree survives intact.

** A folder structure that scales

Keep it boring and physical: ~Pictures\2026\2026-08-24 <event>~ style — year
folder, date-and-name subfolders. Boring survives contact with decades. Within
darktable, use *collections* (next subsection) for the views you actually want;
never reorganise folders by hand once imported, or the database loses track.

** Collections: the views you actually want

The left panel's *collect* module chooses what the grid shows: by folder, by
date, by tag, by rating, by colour label... Stack multiple rules to narrow.
This is how a flat folder of 900 frames becomes "four stars, tagged wedding".
Save frequent combinations with the ≡ menu as *collections presets*.

** Tags

The *tagging* module (left panel): hierarchical tags like ~people|family|dana~
create themselves into trees. Apply to selections of many photos at once. Tags
embed into exported files' metadata and round-trip through other programs.

** What happens at import

Thumbnails build fast; larger previews build in background jobs (progress
bottom-left). Culling on half-built previews misleads — give a fresh import a
minute. Each photo gets an XMP sidecar as soon as edits exist (per section 4).

* 7. Culling: choosing the keepers

Raw shooting generates chaff; culling is deciding what deserves development
time. darktable's culling is keyboard-first and fast once learned.

** The rating pass

In the lighttable grid:

- Arrow keys move between photos; number keys ~0–5~ assign stars; ~R~ flags
  reject.
- ~F1–F5~ assign colour labels (red/yellow/green/blue/purple). Stars alone are
  usually enough; labels earn their keep marking *states* — green = edited,
  red = needs retouching, and so on. Pick meanings once, keep them forever.
- The top tray filters the grid by rating/colour/text. Cull with "show all",
  browse later with "≥ 3 stars".

A workable scheme, honed by millions of photographers: first pass, reject the
broken frames (blinked, blurred, duplicated). Second pass, three stars to the
worth-editing. Third pass, four or five stars to the best of those. Never
five-star generously; the stars are only useful if they stay scarce.

** Zoomable view and culling mode

- *Zoomable view* (~z~ or the second layout button): a canvas of thumbnails at
  any size — good for judging similar frames side by side at magnification.
- *Culling mode* (~x~ fixed layout, ~Ctrl+X~ dynamic): shows two or more
  selected photos large, synchronised pan and zoom. Scroll-wheel zooms both in
  lockstep — the honest test of "which of these two is sharper". Dynamic mode
  advances the pair through the filmstrip as you arrow along; this is the
  fastest serious comparison tool in the program.

** The exit rule

When culling feels finished, walk away and re-cull tomorrow before deleting
anything permanent. Rejection is cheap to undo today; the trash bin is less
sentimental. (darktable's reject flag hides photos; actual deletion happens
only if you run the "delete originals" operation deliberately.)

* 8. What everything is called

darktable splits the adjustments most editors bundle into one "basic" panel
into separate, single-purpose *modules*. More powerful — each module has room
for real controls and masks — and, initially, bewildering: the thing you want
exists, under a name you would not have guessed.

Here is the dictionary. Left side: the intent, phrased the way you'd say it.
Right side: where it actually lives. If you know Lightroom's Basic panel
names, the mapping below doubles as a translation table.

- Brighten or darken overall → ~exposure~
- Lift shadows, pull highlights → ~tone equalizer~
- Add overall contrast → ~color balance rgb~, contrast slider
- Boost colour (vibrance/saturation) → ~color balance rgb~, vibrance/chroma
- Perceived midtone brightness without touching exposure → *brilliance*,
  also inside ~color balance rgb~
- Punch up midtone detail (what others call clarity) → ~local contrast~
- Bring out fine texture → ~diffuse or sharpen~
- Sharpen (edge acutance) → ~sharpen~, normally left off — see section 18
- Cut through atmospheric haze → ~haze removal~
- Set the white point and black point → mostly ~sigmoid~ plus ~exposure~
  working together
- White balance / remove colour casts → ~white balance~ (simple) or
  ~color calibration~ (powerful, modern)
- Remove dust spots, blemishes, wires, small litter → ~retouch~
- Reduce noise → ~denoise (profiled)~; hard cases → ~neural restore~
- Select the subject automatically → *AI object mask*, available wherever a
  mask can be drawn (section 12)
- Blur a background → ~blurs~, lens-blur mode
- Fix lens distortion, vignetting, colour fringes → ~lens correction~
- Crop, straighten → ~crop and rotate~; fix leaning buildings →
  ~rotate and perspective~
- Save and reuse a whole look → *styles* (what other programs call presets);
  per-module starting points are called *presets*
- Simulate classic film stocks → ~film emulation~ (optional extra package)
- Recover detail from scanned negatives → ~negadoctor~ (only if you scan film)

One module deserves early acquaintance because it has no equivalent anywhere
else: *brilliance*, inside ~color balance rgb~. It adjusts perceived
brightness of tones without the exposure side-effects — brightening shadows
with brilliance keeps their colour and contrast in a way the exposure slider
cannot. You will reach for it constantly.

There is deliberately no single "everything" panel by default. The next
section builds you one.

* 9. The Quick Access Panel

Most raw editors hand you one column containing exposure, contrast, shadows,
highlights, saturation, vibrance, texture, clarity. darktable scatters those
across six modules, because each gets deeper controls that way. Fine — except
for the first month, when you just want the damn sliders.

The *Quick Access Panel* (QAP) is the answer: a strip at the top of the
right-hand darkroom column that pulls chosen sliders out of many modules into
one place. A fresh install populates it sensibly for your workflow setting —
exposure, contrast via color balance, brilliance, vibrance, chroma, local
contrast among them. It is the closest thing to muscle memory from any other
editor's Basic panel.

** Customising it

Any of these work:

- Right-click the QAP's icon in the module strip.
- Right-click any module's name elsewhere and choose to add its sliders.
- Open *manage module layouts* (search it in the module search box), tick
  *show quick access panel*, then add with the *+* beneath a group name and
  remove with the *x* beside any slider.

** The rule that makes this work

#+begin_quote
*For your first two weeks, use only the Quick Access Panel and the crop tool.*

Do not go browsing the module list. That list is where enthusiasm goes to die:
people open every module on day one, conclude the software is hostile, and
quit. It is not hostile; it simply refuses to hide anything from you. Get
fluent in eight sliders. Then add modules one at a time, each only when a real
photograph demands it.
#+end_quote

* 10. How the darkroom thinks

Open a photo in the darkroom and the right column lists *modules*. Understand
five behaviours and the darkroom stops being mysterious.

** The pipeline has a fixed order

Modules are not applied top-to-bottom as displayed; they execute along a fixed
internal chain (the *pixelpipe*): geometry first, then exposure/tonality, then
colour, then detail work like denoise and sharpening, then the display
transform. The display order groups related modules, not execution order. You
cannot break this order, which is liberating — there is no wrong place to put
exposure, because exposure happens where exposure happens. Multiple instances
of one module run where you slot them (next point).

** Enable, disable, reset

Every module header carries three controls: the power icon (on/off), the
*reset* arrow (back to module defaults), and the hamburger ≡ menu (presets,
multiple instances). A disabled module contributes nothing — toggling modules
on and off to see what each contributes is half of learning to edit.

** Presets and multiple instances

The ≡ menu holds *presets*: named starting points for that one module. Store
your own any time (section 13 turns them automatic).

The same menu offers *new instance* — a second copy of the module in the same
edit. This sounds exotic and is actually the beating heart of local editing:
one exposure module globally, a second exposure module masked to brighten only
the subject. Modules × masks = every local adjustment you have ever wanted.

** History, snapshots, duplication

- Left panel, *history*: every action as a stack. Click any earlier line to
  time-travel; nothing is lost by exploring.
- *compress history* button squashes the stack after long sessions.
- ≡ on the history panel: *create style from history* — instant style from the
  current edit (section 14).
- *snapshots* module: bookmark the current look, keep editing, click to compare.

** Copying edits between photos

Select a developed photo, then Ctrl-click others (or shift-click a range).
Top toolbar → *copy history* (or right panel's copy/paste icons): paste all,
or expand to choose individual modules ("paste parts"). This is how one
correctly-white-balanced frame becomes fifty. For folder-wide consistency,
styles plus auto-apply usually beat pasting (sections 13–14).

* 11. The modules that matter, one by one

Twelve modules cover essentially all serious raw editing. Each entry: what it
does, the controls that matter, starting points, mistakes to avoid. Order here
is roughly the order your eye should meet them.

** exposure

What it does: sets overall brightness, and defines the black point via its
black-level slider. The single most-used control in photography.

- Start here on every frame. Push until highlights threaten, pull back.
- Hold ~O~ over the image (or enable clipping preview) to see blown
  highlights blink red and crushed blacks blue. Some blinking is fine if it is
  a specular catchlight or the sun itself; large areas are not.
- The white/black sliders of other editors live partly here and partly in the
  display transform (~sigmoid~); between the two, all tonal placement is yours.

Mistake to avoid: using exposure for local brightness problems — that is what
masked instances or the tone equalizer are for.

** white balance / color calibration

Two modules, one job: making colours neutral-correct.

- ~white balance~: the simple route — temperature and tint sliders, presets
  (daylight, tungsten...), or click the eyedropper on something that ought to
  be grey/neutral and let darktable compute.
- ~color calibration~: the modern CAT-based route. Its *auto* button estimates
  illuminance; its sliders go beyond temperature into full primaries. Learn the
  simple module now; graduate when you want creative colour control.

Eyedropper advice: aim at something genuinely neutral — concrete, white paint,
a grey card. Skin is never neutral; do not ask it to be.

** sigmoid

The display transform: compresses the scene's huge range into screen range.
You chose scene-referred defaults, so sigmoid sits near the pipe's end doing
the heavy lifting quietly.

- *contrast*: gentle overall punch. Small moves; ±0.2 is a lot.
- *skew*: shifts midtones brighter/darker without moving extremes — often the
  best "levels" control you own.
- *latitude*: how aggressively highlights and shadows compress. Wider latitude
  = more retained highlight/shadow detail but flatter look.

At the start: leave it alone entirely except perhaps contrast. It is already
doing correct work invisibly.

** tone equalizer

The shadows-and-highlights machine, and darktable's quiet superpower. It is a
tone curve whose bands correspond to exposure zones — five sliders, each
adjusting one brightness band independently: darkest, shadows, midtones,
highlights, brightest.

- Lift ~shadows~ +0.5 to +1.5 stops on almost any D7200 file; the sensor holds
  the detail and this module pulls it out cleanly, far more controllably than
  any single shadows slider.
- Pull ~highlights~ down on skies and bright skin without dulling the whole
  frame.
- The underlying trick (it builds an internal mask per zone) occasionally
  produces halos around extreme edges; drop its *masking iteration* quality
  setting up, or ease off the slider, if you see light seams.

This module is where the D7200's base-ISO dynamic range visibly earns its
keep. Spend learning time here.

** color balance rgb

The colour and contrast Swiss Army knife. Three areas:

- *Contrast* slider: the main global contrast control in a scene-referred
  workflow. Prefer it over anything labelled "curves" while learning.
- *Vibrance / chroma / saturation* group: vibrance boosts muted colours more
  than loud ones (safe, natural); chroma is uniform boost; saturation is the
  blunt instrument. Vibrance first, always.
- *Brilliance*: perceptual midtone brightness — the sleeper hit described in
  section 8.
- Four-way colour wheels (global/shadows/midtones/highlights) for grading:
  push shadows slightly teal and highlights slightly warm and photographs
  start looking "graded". Subtlety pays; small values only.

** local contrast

Clarity, honestly named. Adds punch to medium-sized detail — faces, foliage,
brickwork — without touching fine grain or global contrast.

- Default strength is reasonable; overshoot produces crunchy halos.
- Its *saturation protection* slider keeps colours from going electric.
- Use after tone equalizer, before sharpening-type work.

** diffuse or sharpen

One engine with two personalities:

- As *sharpener* (first tab): diffuses-inward enhancement of edge acutance.
  Gentle settings only — the D7200 needs little sharpening (section 18).
- As *blurrer* (diffuse mode): soft-focus diffusion that mimics large-format
  lens rendering. Niche, lovely, ignore for now.

When you crave more micro-texture: first tab, low iterations, small radius,
and judge at 100% zoom.

** denoise (profiled)

The everyday noise reducer, and the anchor of section 13's automation.

- *Auto* mode reads the camera model and ISO embedded in the file and loads
  the matching measured noise profile — the D7200 is among 300+ cameras with
  real lab-measured profiles, interpolated between neighbouring ISOs. You are
  not on generic fallback math.
- One *strength* slider. Auto-plus-a-nudge handles ISO 1600–6400 invisibly;
  extreme ISO gets the neural treatment (section 15) layered on top.
- Because it runs late-ish in the pipe, it cleans noise without smearing the
  detail work you did upstream.

** retouch

Spot removal, healing, cloning — the surgery kit. Full sub-guide:

1. Choose a shape: circle, ellipse, path, brush.
2. *Ctrl-click the shape icon to lock the tool.* Without this, darktable
   returns the mouse after every spot — with it, you click away ten dust spots
   in ten seconds. Single most useful trick in the module.
3. Click a blemish. darktable picks a nearby source region automatically and
   shows it connected by a line; drag the source if it chose badly.
4. Mode matters: use *heal*, not clone, by default. Heal blends the sampled
   patch's texture with the destination's colour and lighting — invisible.
   Clone stamps verbatim, seam included; reserve it for copying real structure
   (removing a logo, doubling a flower).
5. ~fill~ mode paints flat colour (rarely useful photographically);
   ~blur~ mode blurs the patched area flat (useful for licence plates).
6. The module also contains a wavelet decomposition — frequency separation.
   That is the honest way to smooth skin while keeping pores: decompose the
   image into layers of detail scale, blur only the coarsest layer of the
   skin, leave the fine layers untouched. Overkill for dust; worth knowing it
   lives here rather than needing GIMP.

** lens correction

Corrects your lens' optical sins automatically:

- Open it, tick *auto-set* — darktable identifies body and lens from EXIF and
  pulls the correction profile (distortion, vignetting, transversal chromatic
  aberration) from its database. Your Nikon glass is covered.
- Manual tabs exist for unlisted lenses; the geometric tab also fixes horizon
  tilt independent of cropping.
- Leave corrections subtle-by-default; the auto profile applies designed
  amounts, which is exactly right.

** crop and rotate

Straightforward, with three notes:

- Aspect-ratio presets live in a drop-down; freehand is default.
- Composition guides (rule-of-thirds, golden spirals) overlay while dragging.
- Rotation here is lossless-within-the-pipeline: pixels are re-rendered, the
  negative untouched. Straighten confidently.
- Keystoned buildings belong to ~rotate and perspective~ (vertical-lines
  correction), not this module.

** haze removal

Dehazes atmospheric murk on landscapes. Two sliders, strong effects; easy to
overdo into poster-looking results. Small doses on genuinely hazy frames only.

** The ones deliberately missing from this tour

~filmic rgb~ and AgX: alternative display transforms — sigmoid's ambitious
siblings. Not needed. ~negadoctor~: film scans. ~framing~, ~watermark~: better
handled at export (section 16). ~sharpen~: left off by design on this camera
(section 18). The remaining two dozen modules: genuine uses, none required.
Meet each when a photo demands it, not before.

* 12. Masking: the complete guide

Global adjustments get you 80% of a photograph. The last 20% — the part that
separates competent from striking — is local: this sky darker, that face
brighter, everything else untouched. Masking is how darktable does local, and
its masking system is the strongest argument for the program. Every module
accepts masks; four kinds combine freely.

** The concept in one paragraph

Any module can be limited to act on part of the frame. With the module open,
click the *mask shape* icon (brush/circle/ellipse symbols beside the module's
sliders), draw a shape, and the module's effect now applies only inside that
shape — inverted, feathered, combined however you like. Because modules can be
instantiated (section 10), "brighten the face" means: new exposure instance,
mask over the face, raise exposure inside the mask. Nothing else in the frame
is touched, and the mask remains editable forever.

** Drawn masks

Five tools, all vector-based and revisable:

- *Brush* — paint the region. Flow vs opacity: build density with repeated
  strokes (flow) rather than one heavy pass.
- *Circle / Ellipse* — click-placeable radial zones; drag centre and rim.
- *Path* — click vertices for precise polygons; nodes stay draggable forever.
- *Gradient* — click start, drag end: linear falloff, perfect for sky
  treatments. Rotate the gradient line to follow the horizon.

Per-shape controls appear once drawn: *feather* (edge softness — generous on
anything organic), *opacity* (partial-strength masks), *invert*, and the
display toggle showing the mask as a red overlay while you edit it (~B~
toggles display of drawn shapes).

** Parametric masks

The kind other programs barely attempt. Instead of drawing where, you declare
*what*: mask by luminance range, by colour channel value, by hue. Sliders set
the centre and tolerance of the targeted band, with smooth falloff.

Examples that teach the idea:

- Shadows-only lift: exposure instance, parametric mask targeting low L
  values → the lift lands only on true shadow tones.
- Blue-sky saturation: color balance instance, parametric mask on the blue
  hue band → vibrance hits sky and nothing else.
- Highlight protection: any module, parametric mask excluding high L → effect
  fades out before the brightest tones.

Parametric masks combine with drawn ones (intersection by default): "inside
this painted area AND within these tones". That intersection is the entire
skill, and it is learnable in an afternoon.

** Combining masks

Multiple shapes/forms on one module combine with set logic, chosen per form:

- *union* (+): anywhere either covers.
- *intersection* (∩): only where both agree.
- *difference* / *exclusive or*: subtract or XOR regions.

Plus a *raster mask* type: flatten-and-reuse a mask built elsewhere — handy
when several different modules should share one complicated region.

** AI object mask (new in 5.6)

Click-to-select subjects, powered locally by the SAM 2.1 models configured in
section 4. Wherever a drawn mask could go, choose the AI object shape, then:

1. Click your subject. *The first click takes 1–10 seconds* — the image
   encoder runs once per image (GPU-accelerated via DirectML when available)
   and caches. Every subsequent click on that photo is interactive.
2. The result converts to an ordinary vector path — nodes you can drag,
   feathering you can adjust — not a locked black box.
3. *Shift-click* adds negative prompts, carving away anything wrongly grabbed.
4. *Right-click* commits. Optional refinement passes tighten edges
   (DenseCRF-based boundary refinement exists for edge-critical work).
5. Two post-controls matter: *smoothing* reduces node clutter on soft-edged
   subjects; *refine boundary* runs the extra edge pass. For hair and fur, add
   5–10 px feathering regardless — a vector path fundamentally cannot
   represent individual strands, and feathering is the honest fix.
6. The mask can be exported as PNG raster for external use if you ever need
   pixel-perfect edges beyond the vectorisation.

Limits, stated plainly: it segments *connected* regions — two separate people
need two clicks/masks — and it selects objects, not materials ("all the grass"
is parametric territory).

** Background blur, and its honest limit

Recipe: AI-mask the subject → invert the mask → apply ~blurs~ in *lens blur*
mode, which simulates a real diaphragm (blade count and curvature configurable)
for genuinely photographic bokeh.

Now read darktable's own warning and believe it: *"the blurring process does
not take scene depth and depth-of-field into account, but blurs the whole
image as a flat object."*

Translation: it is a beautifully-rendered flat cut-out blur. Convincing against
walls, foliage masses, even soft street scenes at small apertures of blur.
Unconvincing down receding streets or floors, where real depth-of-field
progressively increases blur with distance and this cannot. If a specific
photograph demands true depth-graduated bokeh, the only free tool that does it
is RapidRAW (~20 MB, separate program, depth-map based). Keep it in the drawer
for that one job; it is not part of this workflow.

* 13. Automatic everything: presets that apply themselves

*The highest-value trick in this guide.* darktable can auto-apply any module
preset based on conditions embedded in the file itself: camera model, lens,
ISO, shutter speed, aperture, focal length. Configure once; stop clicking
forever. Most editors simply cannot do this.

** The flagship recipe: automatic high-ISO denoise

1. Open a high-ISO D7200 raw in the darkroom.
2. Enable ~denoise (profiled)~. It reads camera and ISO from metadata and
   loads the D7200's measured profile for that ISO automatically.
3. Leave mode on *auto*; nudge strength to taste on this representative frame.
4. Open the module's ≡ menu → *store new preset*. Name it clearly, e.g.
   "D7200 high ISO denoise".
5. In the preset dialog, tick *auto-apply this preset to matching images*, then
   set the conditions:
   - model: =NIKON D7200=
   - ISO: =1600= to =102400=
6. Save.

From now on, every imported frame at ISO 1600+ opens in the darkroom with
denoising already switched on and tuned. You never think about it again — and
low-ISO frames remain untouched, because the condition excludes them.

** Two more worth building immediately after

- *Lens corrections per lens*: same procedure on ~lens correction~ after
  confirming its auto-profile looks right — scope by lens name. One preset per
  lens you own.
- *Your baseline look*: once section 14's style exists, styles can also be
  applied automatically at import via their own matching rules.

** How auto-apply behaves

Matching happens when an image enters the darkroom (and at import for
styles). Edits land in the history like manual ones — fully removable. If a
preset misfires on some frame, just toggle the module off for that photo;
the rule stays for everything else.

* 14. Styles: presets for whole edits

A *preset* (previous section) stores one module's settings. A *style* stores
an entire edit — every module, every mask, the whole look — as a reusable unit.
Styles are what other programs call presets; darktable's naming is backwards
relative to the world, and that is now your problem only until it isn't.

** Building your first style

Develop one photograph properly — white balance, tone equalizer, color
balance, local contrast, the works, all global (no spot-specific masks).
Then: history panel ≡ → *create style from history*, deselect any modules you
want excluded, name it ("D7200 base v1").

Applying: lighttable, select photos, right panel *styles* module, double-click
the style. Or mid-edit from the darkroom's styles module.

** What belongs in a baseline style

Camera-and-taste-dependent settings only: white balance strategy (or leave WB
out entirely — usually wise), gentle tone-equalizer shadow lift, mild
vibrance, slight local contrast. Keep creative grading (colour wheels, heavy
contrast) in *separate* styles so "base + optional look" composes cleanly:
apply base to everything, layer a look onto winners.

** Sharing and importing

Styles export/import as small XML files through the same module's ≡ menus.
The community shares them freely (discuss.pixls.us is full of them); import
and they appear in the list. Version-name your own ("_v2") — you will iterate.

* 15. Neural restore: AI denoise and upscaling

Section 6 of the AI story: the models are configured (section 4); here is what
the machine actually does for you. The ~neural restore~ module lives in the
lighttable's right sidebar by default (also available darkroom-left).

** The thing to understand before anything else

Unlike every module so far, neural restore is *not part of the pipeline*. It
runs a neural network over the image and *writes a new file back into the
library* — grouped beside the original, inheriting its tags and rating,
original untouched. So the order of operations inverts:

#+begin_quote
Ordinary denoising: /edit, with denoise as one slider among many./
Neural denoising: /produce a clean file first, then edit that./
#+end_quote

Plan sessions around it: extreme-ISO keepers get neural treatment first, then
normal development of the result.

** The three tabs

- *raw denoise* — works on the sensor mosaic *before demosaicing*: denoise and
  demosaic fused into one inference pass. For extreme ISO this is the deepest
  cut available anywhere, free or paid. Output: a DNG (Bayer CFA preserved),
  ready for normal development.
- *denoise* — RGB-domain cleanup for noise that survived editing; strong at
  ISO 6400+. Output: 16-bit TIFF carrying the colour profile. Strength slider
  blends texture recovery against smoothness (DWT-based texture restoration).
- *upscale* — 2× or 4× super-resolution as a *last step before delivery*.
  Default model (RealPLKSR) is faithful — preserves detail without inventing
  plastic texture. Alternative BSRGAN model suits sources that are already
  noisy or compressed (it denoises while scaling). 2× is convincing; treat 4×
  as situational.

** Workflow details

- Click inside the small preview window to pick the region the preview
  computes; use the built-in before/after split slider to judge honestly.
- Batch: select multiple images in the lighttable and run once — tiled
  inference keeps memory sane.
- Model swapping (preferences → AI): NAFNet as alternate denoiser (tuned for
  small-sensor noise patterns), plus the BSRGAN/RealPLKSR choice above. Task
  slots and models are decoupled — switching active model is one click and no
  module changes.
- GPU OOM or unsupported operations fall back to CPU automatically rather than
  erroring; slow-but-done beats failed.

** Which denoiser when

- ISO ≤ 3200: ~denoise (profiled)~, automated per section 13. Done.
- ISO 6400–12800 ordinary shots: profiled denoise at higher strength.
- Extreme ISO, or noise visible after everything else: neural raw-denoise
  first, redevelop, then judge whether profiled denoise is still wanted on top.
- Deliverable too small/cropped too tight: neural upscale last.

* 16. Export: making the actual picture

Everything so far produced instructions. *Export* renders them into an actual
image file — JPEG for sharing, TIFF for GIMP hand-off, PNG where needed.
Module: lighttable, right sidebar (~export selected~); applies to all selected
images.

** Format decisions

- *JPEG*, quality 90–95: the universal answer for web/mail. sRGB profile
  (below).
- *TIFF*, 16-bit: the interchange format — maximum quality, huge files. This
  is the format for handing a developed frame to GIMP (see
  ~GIMP-README.org~'s round-trip recipe).
- *PNG*: screenshots-with-photography, transparency needs, otherwise skip.
- *WebP/AVIF*: smaller web files; use only where the destination is known to
  handle them.

** The settings that actually matter

- *quality* (JPEG): 90 invisible, 95 archival-ish, below 85 visibly chewy.
- *max size*: set long edge in pixels (e.g. 2048 for screens) or leave empty
  for full resolution. Upscaling belongs to section 15, not here.
- *colour profile*: *sRGB* for anything screen-bound — non-negotiable, or
  colours break on other devices. AdobeRGB only for print-house workflows that
  explicitly ask. *display profile* means "whatever my monitor uses" — wrong
  choice for delivery, right choice for test exports.
- *intent*: relative colorimetric is the safe default.
- *bit depth*: 16-bit for TIFF, 8-bit for web JPEG/PNG.
- *path/filename*: variables build tidy pipelines, e.g.
  =~/Pictures/export/$(YEAR)/$(FILE_NAME)-exported.jpg=. A few minutes with
  the variables list pays off permanently.
- *metadata*: flags control which EXIF/tags/XMP ride along. Keep copyright.
- *watermark*: text or SVG overlay with placement/opacity/scale controls —
  covers simple attribution without opening GIMP.

** Style-on-export

The export module can apply any *style* to exported copies *without altering
your stored edit* — e.g. keep masters neutral, but export a punchy-look JPEG
batch alongside. Selecting multiple styles stacks them. This separation of
"master edit" from "delivery rendering" quietly enables versioned outputs
(colour, monochrome, social-crop) from one developed file.

** Round-trip note

Exports are new, separate files. They never feed back into darktable's edit
history. To bring a finished composite home (e.g. from GIMP), import the
returned file as a new image alongside the original.

* 17. One photograph, end to end

Everything above, compressed into one worked pass with concrete numbers.
Frame: indoor reception, D7200, 35 mm f/1.8, ISO 3200, tungsten-ish mixed
light, one person against a window.

1. *Import* (section 6): card reader, copy & import into
   ~Pictures\2026\2026-08-24 simcha~. Previews build; wait a minute.
2. *Cull* (section 7): first pass rejects the blinked frames (~R~); second
   pass three-stars this one among twelve near-duplicates.
3. *Open in darkroom.* Auto-applied already: lens correction (per-lens
   preset), denoise profiled at strength 1.2 (high-ISO preset, section 13).
   Half the work happened before you touched anything.
4. *White balance:* eyedropper on the white tablecloth → close. Tint nudged
   +5 toward magenta because the window light was greenish.
5. *Exposure:* +0.3, watching the ~O~ clipping overlay — window stays blown,
   acceptable, it is the sun's fault.
6. *Tone equalizer:* shadows +0.8, highlights −0.7. The face emerges; the sky
   outside stops screaming.
7. *color balance rgb:* contrast 0.15, vibrance 0.10, brilliance 0.05 on the
   face via a second instance masked parametrically to midtones (section 12).
8. *retouch* (Ctrl-locked circle, heal mode): three sensor spots, one waiter's
   elbow at frame edge. Ninety seconds total.
9. *AI object mask* on the subject inside a fresh exposure instance: first
   click 4 seconds (encoder runs), shift-click to release her shoulder from
   the mask, right-click commits, feather +6 px. Exposure +0.2 inside the
   mask. She now separates from the background the way the eye remembered her.
10. *Crop and rotate:* horizon levelled against the doorframe, 3:2 kept.
11. *Export twice* (section 16): JPEG q92, sRGB, long edge 2048, filename
    pattern ~(FILE_NAME)-web~ → family album; TIFF 16-bit sRGB full-size →
    ~GIMP-README.org~'s workflow for the one thing darktable cannot fix: the
    exit sign glowing over her head gets removed there, and the finished TIFF
    imports back beside the raw.

Total hands-on time, once fluent: under five minutes. Every step remains
revisable forever, including the ones darktable did automatically.

* 18. Notes for the Nikon D7200

Body-specific facts that change how darktable should be used.

** No optical low-pass filter

Nikon shipped this sensor without an anti-aliasing filter: maximum sharpness
and genuine moiré risk in the same transaction. Consequences:

- *Do not stack sharpening.* Scene-referred defaults ship with ~sharpen~
  switched off. Leave it off. For extra bite use ~diffuse or sharpen~, gently,
  and judge at 100%.
- *If moiré appears* — fabric weaves, brick courses, window screens producing
  rainbow shimmer — switch ~demosaic~ to *VNG4*. It trades a little fine
  detail for far fewer artifacts than the default RCD. Change it per-problem-
  frame, not globally; VNG4's softness is real.
- In-camera there is nothing to do about this; it is a develop-side property.

** Exceptional base-ISO dynamic range

This sensor holds enormous shadow detail at ISO 100–800. The ~tone equalizer~
pulls it out with more control than any shadows slider ever gives you. This is
where darktable visibly earns its keep on your files — underexposed-by-a-stop
frames are not emergencies, they are starting points. (Shooting side:
~D7200-GUIDE.org~ explains why raw files arrive looking flat and why that is
the camera behaving correctly.)

** Above ISO 3200

Where section 13's automatic denoise preset earns *its* keep, and beyond
roughly ISO 6400 where neural raw-denoise becomes worth its detour (section
15). The D7200's noise is well-behaved chroma grain; both tools handle it.

** Picture Controls are not coming with you

In-camera Picture Control settings (Standard, Vivid...) shape the JPEG preview
embedded in the raw but carry no authority in darktable — you develop from
sensor data. If a frame's embedded preview looks better than darktable's
neutral start, NX Studio (section 3) shows you Nikon's intent; steal it with
sliders rather than chasing it with confusion.

** 1.3× crop mode files

Frames shot in the D7200's crop mode import as smaller raws and behave
normally; nothing special needed, just expect fewer megapixels.

* 20. Housekeeping: backups, databases, upgrades

** What exists where (Windows)

- *Photos*: wherever your folder structure puts them (~Pictures\...~). Plain
  files, no lock-in.
- *XMP sidecars*: beside each photo once edits exist. Plain text. The portable
  half of your work.
- *Database*: under ~%LOCALAPPDATA%\darktable~ — library.db plus data.db
  (ratings/collections cache etc.) and the thumbnail cache alongside.
- *Styles*: stored in the database; export any style to XML to keep file
  copies (do this after creating each one worth keeping).

** The backup rule

Back up two things and you can survive anything: *the photo folders including
sidecars*, and an XML export of your styles. The database itself is rebuildable
— point darktable at your photo folders after a loss and it re-imports, reading
every sidecar back into place. Ratings, tags, edits: all inside those little
files. This is why section 4 told you to enable sidecar writing.

** Upgrades

Minor releases (5.6 → 5.7): just install. Major ones: export styles, let
darktable write all sidecars, copy the database files aside, then upgrade.
Ten minutes of ceremony against years of history.

* 21. What will annoy you

Known, enumerated, survivable:

1. *Nothing is called what you would guess.* Sections 8–9 exist precisely for
   this. Fades within two weeks of regular use.
2. *Background blur is flat.* Depth-blind by design (section 12). RapidRAW is
   the free escape hatch for the rare frame that truly needs depth-graduated
   bokeh.
3. *Neural restore is a detour, not a slider.* It writes new files (section
   15). Order operations accordingly or wonder where disk space went.
4. *First AI click per image is slow.* Encoder analysis runs once and caches;
   afterwards interactive. On a cold session over many images, budget the wait.
5. *No dedicated moiré tool.* The fix is swapping demosaic algorithms
   (section 18), which is either charming or barbaric depending on your day.
6. *No generative features at all.* Deliberate policy: correction yes,
   synthesis no (section 2). When sky replacement becomes non-negotiable,
   that is Adobe-shaped work.
7. *The module list is a cliff for newcomers.* Mitigated entirely by the QAP
   discipline (section 9); unmitigated enthusiasm has ended more darktable
   trials than any bug.

* 22. If you are arriving from Lightroom

/Skip this section entirely if you are not. Nothing later depends on it./
(The reverse journey — Lightroom habits explained from zero, for someone who
ends up running both — lives in ~LIGHTROOM-GUIDE.org~, section "Coming from
darktable".)

** What transfers

Keywords, star ratings, colour labels and captions *can* move via XMP
sidecars. In Lightroom: enable *Automatically write changes into XMP*
(Catalog Settings → Metadata), then select everything and ~Ctrl+S~ to force
writes. darktable reads those sidecars at import — but only at import time,
and only for raws without an existing darktable sidecar, so migrate before
editing anything in darktable, not after.

** What does not transfer, ever

Develop settings. The two programs process images through fundamentally
different pipelines; no translation exists, and attempted translations would
render differently anyway. Your edits stay in Lightroom.

#+begin_quote
*Therefore: export all finished work as TIFF/JPEG before cancelling any Adobe
subscription.* Once the subscription lapses, existing edits are locked in a
program you cannot open. Exported files are yours forever regardless.
#+end_quote

** Habits worth keeping

Culling with stars/flags maps one-to-one (section 7). Collections ≈ darktable
collections presets. Presets ≈ styles, with the names swapped (section 14).
The Basic panel ≈ Quick Access Panel. Everything else is sections 8 through 12
with better vocabulary than you have been given credit for.

* Appendix A. Beyond sigmoid: filmic rgb and AgX

Section 4 told you to take scene-referred (sigmoid) and stop reading. This
appendix is for the day you wonder what the other buttons do — or the day a
specific photograph outgrows the defaults.

** What the display transform is actually doing

Every scene-referred edit ends with compressing ~14 stops of scene brightness
into your monitor's ~7. The transform decides *how*: where highlights roll
off, whether midtones shift, how saturated colours survive compression.
Sigmoid's answer: mathematically smooth, conservative, few controls. It fails
visibly only on extreme scenes — direct sun, stage lights against black,
long-exposure water at sunset.

** filmic rgb: the engineer's transform

The older, more controllable sibling. Its philosophy: you declare what white
is and what black is (via its *white/black relative exposure* sliders), and it
designs a tone curve honouring those boundaries while preserving midtone
contrast and hue. Extra controls: *latitude* (how much of the range compresses
vs clips), *balance* (highlight-vs-shadow bias), per-channel saturation
preservation settings.

What it buys: highlight roll-offs you can art-direct — sunsets that keep
colour bands instead of going pastel, stage shots where the spotlight stays a
spotlight.

What it costs: setup time per extreme frame; enough rope to produce grey
murk if fed nonsense values. Advice: switch to filmic rgb *per-photograph*
(via the module's preset menu, replacing sigmoid in that edit's pipeline)
when highlights carry the photograph. Never stack both transforms — pick one
per edit; they are mutually exclusive views of the same job.

** AgX: the film-stock emulator

Newest of the three, modelled on how film negative actually handles
saturation-under-brightness (bright things desaturate like film, not like
monitors). Neon signs and fire keep their hue identities instead of clipping
to yellow/white. Advice: try AgX on colour-critical night work after sigmoid
disappoints; leave it otherwise — its midtone rendering reads slightly muted
for everyday work unless that's the look you're chasing.

** The decision rule

Default sigmoid · highlights-are-the-story → filmic rgb · neon/fire/night
colour identity matters → try AgX · never two at once.

* Appendix B. color calibration, properly

The ~white balance~ module (§11) is the training-wheels version. Its sibling
~color calibration~ is the honest instrument, and worth twenty minutes once.

** What it does differently

Instead of temperature/tint approximations, it models the *illuminant* — the
actual light that lit the scene — and computes the transform from that light
to standard daylight using the modern CAT (chromatic adaptation transform)
you confirmed in §4. More accurate under mixed and unusual light, and its
sliders speak physics rather than convention.

** The workflow

1. Disable ~white balance~ if enabled (they stack; one master at a time).
2. In ~color calibration~, tab *illuminant*: choose the type — *camera* trusts
   the shot's embedded WB (usually close), *dropdown of standard lights*
   (tungsten, fluorescent, D65...), or *custom*: click a neutral patch in
   frame and darktable solves for the illuminant directly.
3. The second tab adjusts the observer's adaptation; leave alone until you
   know you want it.

** Gamut tools in the same module

Beyond neutrality: the *primary* tabs let you push/squeeze R/G/B primaries —
gentle primary compression rescues out-of-gamut LED-lit colours before they
clip to neon mush. Niche, occasionally frame-saving.

Advice: use ~white balance~ day-to-day (it's faster); reach for
~color calibration~ when mixed lighting fights back or when colour accuracy
matters more than speed — product shots, art reproduction.

* Appendix C. Detail modules worth knowing

Three modules §11 waved through; here is the full treatment.

** chromatic aberration

Fixes colour fringing on high-contrast edges (purple/green halos) that lens
profiles didn't fully correct. Two modes: *linear* for lateral CA (zoom-
dependent, slider-driven) and the newer structure-aware pass for stubborn
cases. Advice: zoom to 200–400% on backlit branches and architectural edges
once per new lens; if fringing shows, fix once, save a per-lens preset
(§13), never think about it again.

** rotate and perspective

Beyond ~crop and rotate~'s simple levelling: this module straightens
*keystones*. Workflow: tick *rotation*, dial verticals plumb against its grid
overlay → switch to perspective correction, drag guides onto two known-
vertical lines (building edges, door frames) → darktable re-projects.
*Aspect* correction compensates the resulting squash. Advice: correct
verticals almost always (leaning buildings read as error); correct
horizontals selectively — full two-point correction on interiors can feel
clinical. Auto-crop after, then fine-tune in crop.

** tone curve

The classic curve, available when ~tone equalizer~ + QAP sliders don't express
a specific idea: L-channel curves for pure tonal remapping, per-channel RGB
curves for cross-tone colour grading (the blue-down-warm move lives here too).
Advice: in scene-referred workflow, prefer tone equalizer for zone work and
color balance rgb for contrast — reach for raw curves only for looks those
can't produce, e.g. a matte fade via raised black point.

* Appendix D. Export variables reference

The export module's path/filename fields accept variables — the difference
between hand-naming four hundred exports and typing one pattern once.
The working set:

| Variable          | Expands to                                   |
|-------------------+----------------------------------------------|
| ~$(YEAR)~         | capture year, from EXIF                      |
| ~$(MONTH)~        | two-digit month                              |
| ~$(EXIF_YEAR)~ etc. | explicit EXIF-date forms                   |
| ~$(FILE_NAME)~    | original filename without extension          |
| ~$(SEQUENCE)~     | per-export counter                           |
| ~$(JOBOUVERT)~ / job codes | tethering session name, if used      |
| ~$(TAGS)~         | tags, joined                                 |
| ~$(STARS)~/~(RATING)~ | rating-derived naming                     |
| ~$(IMAGE_ID)~     | darktable's internal database ID             |

Pattern examples that earn their keep:

- =~/Pictures/export/$(YEAR)/$(FILE_NAME)-web.jpg= — mirrors your archive
  tree; exports always land beside their era.
- =~/Pictures/export/$(YEAR)/$(FILE_NAME)-$(SEQUENCE).jpg= — variant-safe
  batch exports without collisions.

Advice: build *one* canonical export path preset (§16) containing the year
structure and never type a destination again. Full variable list lives in the
export module's help tooltip — the table above covers what photographers
actually use.

* Appendix E. When things go wrong

Crashes, corruption, and the recovery paths that work:

- *darktable crashes opening a specific image.* Suspect OpenCL first: disable
  it (§3), retry — if the image opens, your GPU driver choked on that
  pipeline; update drivers or stay CPU for stability. If CPU-only also
  crashes, delete that image's sidecar and let darktable rebuild its edit.
- *Thumbnails gone / grid empty after moving folders externally.* The
  database points at ghosts. Fix: in lighttable, re-import the moved folder —
  darktable recognises existing images by path+content and restores their edit
  histories from sidecars. Prevention: move files inside darktable, not via
  Explorer (same rule as Lightroom; §6).
- *Edits vanished after reinstall.* Database replaced but photos intact?
  Point a fresh import at your archive with sidecar reading enabled (§4) —
  every edit rides home inside the .xmp files. This is §20's backup rule
  paying out.
- *OpenCL renders differently than CPU.* Slight per-pixel differences are
  normal (different math paths); *gross* differences mean a broken GPU path —
  force CPU for that module via preferences' per-module OpenCL exclusion
  list.
- *AI features greyed out.* AI disabled in preferences (§4.3), models not yet
  downloaded, or build predates 5.6. Check the AI pane's status line.
- *Neural restore output looks wrong/flat.* You ran it on an already-
  flattened export instead of raws (§15's order-of-operations), or strength
  slider at extremes. Re-run from the original raw.

Advice generally: darktable's failure mode is almost always recoverable
because edits live in sidecars and originals are sacred. The unrecoverable
failures are hardware ones — which is what the backup rule is for.

* Appendix F. Every setting, explained

One line per option — the reference layer under the tutorial. Preferences are
grouped by what they affect; exact pane labels shift slightly between
releases, the search box finds any of them by name.

** Interface and general

- *theme* — dark or light UI skin; dark suits photo judging (surroundings stop biasing tone perception).
- *font size* — scales all module labels; raise it if sliders feel cramped.
- *GUI controls size / DPI scaling* — overall widget scale for high-resolution screens.
- *single application instance* — launching darktable again focuses the running copy instead of starting a second.
- *number of folder levels to show in film-roll names* — shortens how rolls display in collections.
- *send deleted images to the trash bin instead of erasing* — safety net for deletions; leave on.
- *ask before deleting/moving images* — confirmation dialogs; personal taste, on is safer.

** Lighttable behaviour

- *initial rating filter mode* — what the grid shows on launch (all photos vs. rated-only).
- *thumbnail size / zoom levels* — grid density steps when scrolling zoom.
- *show scrollbars / image tooltips* — cosmetic aids.
- *sort order defaults* — initial filename/date/rating sort of new collections.
- *copy/paste history dialog behaviour* — whether paste-all skips the per-module chooser.

** Darkroom behaviour

- *pen pressure control for brush opacity/size* — tablet support; maps stylus pressure onto mask painting.
- *crop guides* — which composition overlay (rule-of-thirds, golden…) appears by default while cropping.
- *hide built-in presets* — declutters module ≡ menus down to your own presets only.
- *show loading screen for full preview* — placeholder blur while a zoomed render computes; off feels snappier, on is more honest.
- *scroll behaviour around image edges* — whether panning bounces at frame borders.
- *middle-click colour picker mode* — what sample size the spot probe uses (point vs. averaged area).

** Processing

- *auto-apply pixel workflow defaults* — scene-referred (sigmoid) vs display-referred vs filmic/AgX families; §4's headline choice.
- *auto-apply chromatic adaptation defaults* — modern CAT vs legacy white-balance math.
- *pixel interpolator* — resampling quality for zoomed previews and geometry (bilinear fast, bicubic balanced, Lanczos finest).
- *module order layouts* — which grouping of the right-hand panel you use (default vs. search-friendly flat list).

** CPU / GPU / memory

- *OpenCL support* — none / on: GPU acceleration for the whole pipeline; §3's biggest speed lever.
- *OpenCL scheduling profile* — default / very-fast-GPU / multiple-GPUs / custom: how work is dispatched across devices.
- *per-module OpenCL exclusion list* — force specific modules CPU-side when one misbehaves on your driver.
- *host memory limit* — ceiling on RAM darktable may claim for tiling; raise on 48 GB machines.
- *minimum memory for tiling / buffers* — thresholds below which images process in tiles rather than whole-frame.
- *number of worker threads* — CPU parallelism; max it.

** Storage and locations

- *xmp sidecar writing strategy* — never / on import / on change / both import-and-change; §4 says set it writing.
- *cache directory* — where thumbnails and preview renders live; move to an SSD if the system disk is small.
- *database directory* — where library.db lives; include this path in backups if you skip sidecar reliance.
- *temporary directory* — scratch space for pipeline intermediates.

** AI pane (5.6+)

- *enable AI features* — master switch; off means no ONNX runtime loads at all.
- *download/update default model set* — fetches recommended models in one click.
- *object-mask active model* — SAM 2.1 tiny/small/base-plus or SegNext; tiny for 4 GB GPUs.
- *denoise active model* — NIND (default) vs NAFNet alternative tuned for small sensors.
- *upscale active model* — RealPLKSR (faithful) vs BSRGAN (denoising-while-scaling).
- *model repository URL* — where model updates come from; leave default unless self-hosting.

** The core modules' controls, line by line

- ~exposure~: *exposure* slider — whole-frame brightness in stops · *black level* — where pure black begins, lifting or sinking the floor.
- *white balance*: *temperature* — blue↔amber · *tint* — green↔magenta · *presets* — standard illuminants.
- ~color calibration~: *illuminant tab* — choose/solve the scene light · *adaptation tab* — observer adjustment (leave) · *primaries tabs* — R/G/B gamut pushes for exotic light.
- ~sigmoid~: *contrast* — midtone S-punch · *skew* — midtone brightness without moving extremes · *latitude* — compression aggressiveness at the ends.
- ~tone equalizer~: five band sliders (*darkest/shadows/midtones/highlights/brightest*) — independent gain per exposure zone · *masking iterations* — internal mask quality vs. cost.
- ~color balance rgb~: *contrast* — main global contrast · *vibrance/chroma/saturation* — three saturation philosophies, gentlest first · *brilliance* — perceptual midtone brightness · four wheels — hue shifts per tonal zone with chroma strength.
- ~local contrast~: *strength/detail/coarseness* — clarity effect depth and scale · *saturation protection* — keeps colours from going electric.
- ~diffuse or sharpen~: *mode* — sharpen vs diffuse personality · *iterations/radius* — reach of the diffusion · *anisotropy* — edge-following strength.
- ~denoise (profiled)~: *mode* — auto (metadata-matched profile) vs manual · *strength* — reduction amount · *preserve colours* — chroma-noise handling bias.
- ~retouch~: shape selector (circle/ellipse/path/brush) · *mode* — heal/clone/fill/blur semantics from §11 · *opacity/blend* — patch strength · wavelet sub-panel — frequency-separation decomposition levels.
- ~lens correction*: *auto-set* — profile lookup from EXIF · manual tabs — distortion/vignetting/TCA sliders for unlisted glass · *geometric* — rotation independent of crop.
- ~crop and rotate~: aspect drop-down · angle dial + straighten tool · guides overlay toggle.
- ~rotate and perspective~: rotation grid · perspective line guides (two verticals minimum) · *aspect* — post-correction squash compensation.
- ~haze removal~: *strength* — dehaze amount · *edge sensitivity* — halo resistance near strong edges.
- ~neural restore~: task tabs (raw denoise/denoise/ upscale) · *strength* — blend between source and result · preview picker — compute region · batch button — run over selection.

Anything not listed here is either covered in §11–12 or genuinely safe never
to touch.
