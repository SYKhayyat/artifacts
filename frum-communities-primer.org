* How to read this document

This is a *primer*, not an argument. It does not tell you where to live, does
not rank communities against each other, and does not push a thesis about
housing policy. It describes what is actually there: how many frum families,
which mosdos, which kollelim, what housing costs, what people do for a living,
and what the local pressures are.

*On hashkafa.* Communities here are described by what they contain, not by how
close they sit to some assumed center. Yeshivish, chassidish, Sephardi, Modern
Orthodox, Chabad, and mixed communities are all treated the same way. Where a
community is dominated by one tzibbur, that is stated as a fact about the
community, not as a compliment or a complaint.

*On confidence.* Numbers in this field rot fast and are collected badly. Three
markers are used:

| Marker | Meaning |
|---+---|
| *(v)* | Verified against a named source, with the date it reflects |
| *(est.)* | My estimate from indirect evidence — treat as a ballpark, not a figure |
| *(comp.)* | Computed — a verified rate applied to a figure stated in the same place |
| *(self-rep.)* | Reported by the community itself, usually in relocation marketing. Directionally useful, systematically optimistic |

Where a claim carries no marker, it is a general structural statement rather
than a measurement.

*On layout.* Parts 1–3 are cross-cutting: what makes a community work, how
kollel functions as an institution, and how people earn — §3.5 on tuition and
§3.6 on property and income tax are the two that decide most of the cost
difference between communities. Part 4 profiles the US by region, Part 5 covers
Eretz Yisroel, and the Addendum covers Canada, the UK, Europe, Australia, South
Africa, and Latin America. Parts 6–7 are the comparison tables and a checklist
for evaluating a community yourself. Appendix A is the full Ichud kollel
directory, Appendix D the shul and community-contact listings.

*If you read only three things.* *§3.5* (tuition — the largest cost difference
between communities, and a function of state law rather than of schools), *§3.6*
(property and income tax — the second tuition bill, and the one no brochure
mentions), and *§6.1* (what housing actually costs, by segment). For Eretz
Yisroel, *§5.8* on the draft law and the funding cuts, because every number in
§5.7 currently depends on it.

*On what's missing.* The two best structured datasets available — the OU's
Savitsky community directory and the Ichud HaKollelim kollel registry — both
have a coverage bias. The OU directory is built from communities that /want/
newcomers, so it over-represents small and growing places and under-represents
Lakewood, Brooklyn, Monsey, and the chassidishe centers, which have no
relocation-marketing problem. The Ichud registry covers BMG-affiliated kollelim
and therefore misses Ner Yisroel, Chofetz Chaim/RSA, YU, Chabad, Sephardi, and
independent kollelim entirely, along with the entire Israeli kollel world. Both
gaps are filled here from other sources, but the sections built on those
datasets are denser than the sections that aren't, and that reflects the data,
not the communities.

* Part 1 — The shape of the map

** 1.1 What actually makes a place livable

A frum community is an infrastructure stack. Each layer has a minimum viable
size, and the layers fail in a predictable order as a community shrinks.

*Tier 1 — Non-negotiable.* A daily minyan (three tefillos, not just Shabbos). A
mikvah in usable proximity — ideally within an eruv, otherwise a short drive.
Kosher food that does not require a two-hour round trip. A chevra kadisha or a
working arrangement with one.

*Tier 2 — Required for raising children.* Boys' elementary and girls' elementary
within a reasonable bus ride. A mesivta and a Bais Yaakov / girls' high school,
or an accepted dormitory pipeline out of town. This is the single most common
reason families leave small communities: the community can carry them through
eighth grade and then can't.

*Tier 3 — Required for a community to sustain itself.* An eruv. A kollel or beis
medrash with real learning during the day. Hatzolah or an equivalent. Bikur
cholim. Tomchei Shabbos. A vaad hakashrus or a rav whose hashgacha is accepted.
A shidduch network that doesn't require every date to start with a flight.

*Tier 4 — Quality of life.* Restaurants. Multiple shuls, so that a machlokes or
a bad fit doesn't mean leaving town. Camps. Seminary and post-high-school
options. Simcha halls. Senior care and a kosher nursing facility.

A community with Tiers 1–2 and a thin Tier 3 is a real community that will feel
small. A community with Tier 1 only is a place frum people live, not a place a
frum family builds.

** 1.2 "In-town" and "out-of-town" — what the words actually mean

The terms are geographic in form and economic in substance. "In-town" means the
New York–New Jersey corridor plus Lakewood, and by extension Baltimore, Monsey,
and the Five Towns. Everything else is "out-of-town."

What the distinction actually encodes:

|  | In-town | Out-of-town |
|---+---+---|
| *Housing* | Scarce, expensive, appreciating fast | Cheaper per square foot, more land, slower appreciation |
| *Mosdos* | Deep choice; multiple options per grade per hashkafa | One or two options; fit is not guaranteed |
| *Tuition* | High sticker, limited state aid in NY/NJ | Often dramatically lower, frequently voucher- or ESA-subsidized |
| *Parnassa* | Dense frum business ecosystem; short job search | Thinner frum ecosystem; general economy matters more |
| *Anonymity* | High. You can be a private person | Low. Everyone knows, and you will be asked to do things |
| *Communal load* | Someone else is running it | You are running it, or it doesn't happen |
| *Shidduchim* | Local pool | You are flying, or your children are boarding |

The last two rows are the ones people underweight when they move, and the ones
that decide whether the move holds.

** 1.3 The frum household cost structure

Any comparison of communities that only compares home prices is misleading, and
most of them do exactly that. Four things dominate, and the sticker price of the
house is only one.

*A note on why most published price figures are useless here.* For any given
community you can find three "median home prices" that differ by a factor of
two, because they measure different things:

- *Median listing or sale price* (Redfin, Realtor) includes condos, co-ops,
  small starter homes, and the non-frum parts of the ZIP code. Lakewood's August
  2026 median listing price was *$379K* *(v)* while new 5-bedroom construction
  there was selling at *$1.1–1.2 million* *(v)*. Both numbers are true. Only one
  describes what a frum family buys.
- *Zillow's typical home value (ZHVI)* smooths the whole housing stock, so it
  sits above the listing median and below the family-home price. Lakewood's was
  *$733,729* in October 2025 *(v)*.
- *Price per square foot* strips out the size difference — but only if it is
  measured on the same property type. A neighborhood-wide $/ft² blends condos,
  co-ops and two-to-four-family buildings in with houses, and frum families buy
  houses.
- *In a condo-heavy neighborhood the two published figures are wrong in /opposite/ directions.*
  The all-types *median price understates* houses, because it is full of
  apartments. The all-types *$/ft² overstates* the house rate, because small
  units cost more per foot. *Monsey is the proof*: the published all-types rate
  is *$580/ft²*, but actual Monsey houses run *4,700–5,000 ft²* and sell around
  *$1,019,957 on average (v)* — roughly *$215/ft²*. The published rate is nearly
  triple the real one for houses.
- *Therefore: never divide a median price by a median $/ft² to infer a house size.*
  Those are medians of two different distributions, both contaminated, and the
  quotient is meaningless. An earlier draft of this document did exactly that
  and concluded Monsey's typical house was 1,720 ft². It is closer to *5,000*.
- *Get the house range instead.* Monsey houses: *$475,000–$2,999,988 (v)*. Kew
  Gardens Hills houses: *$750,000–$2,999,000 (v)*, against an all-types median
  of $350–505K that is measuring the co-op stock. Boca Raton: all-types
  *$675–685K*, houses *$1,399,000 (v)*. The range for the right property type
  beats any single blended number.

*And always ask what share of the stock is even houses.* Detached single-family
homes are *34.46% of Lakewood's housing units (v)* — the rest is townhouses,
duplexes, apartments and 3,500-plus units of 55-plus senior housing, including
Leisure Village's 2,433 attached homes at a *$182,168* median *(v)*. A "Lakewood
median" of $399–429K is a fact about that two-thirds, not about the house a frum
family buys, which is *$750–950K* existing and *$1.1–1.2M* newly built *(v)*.
This is why §6.1 lists as many housing segments as a given community actually
has, rather than one number for all of them.

*So: always ask how big.* A $400,000 house in South Bend is roughly
*3,000 square feet* at $133/ft². A $400,000 house in West Rogers Park is roughly
*2,000 square feet* at $200/ft². A $400,000 house in Monsey is about
*690 square feet* at $580/ft², which is to say it is an apartment. Same number,
three completely different lives.

*The four cost drivers, in order of how much they actually vary:*

*1. Tuition — but not the way sticker prices suggest.* Usually the largest
single line, and it varies by state school-choice law rather than by the schools
themselves. *Do not multiply a per-child band by your number of children.*
Sibling discounts are standard, and many schools cap total family tuition at
*12–18% of AGI regardless of how many children attend (v)*; the voucher states
pay per child instead, and *Indiana does not means-test at all (v)*. The upshot
is that the community-to-community tuition gap is
*modest at a kollel or single-earner income and large at a professional one* —
see §3.5 and the two-income worked example in §3.6.

*2. Property tax.* Routinely $10,000–$20,000 a year in the frum communities of
New Jersey and New York, and it never stops — you pay it after the mortgage is
gone. It is close to a second tuition bill, it is almost never mentioned in
relocation marketing, and it does not track house prices. Cleveland Heights has
some of the cheapest frum housing in America *and* an effective rate around
*3.00%* *(v)*, among the highest anywhere in the country. See §3.6.

*3. State income tax.* Between zero and 13.3% of everything you earn, depending
on which side of a state line you live on *(v)*. Nine states levy none. Texas
and Florida trade it away for higher property tax; California and New Jersey
charge both.

*4. The housing proximity premium.* Frum families cannot arbitrage distance. You
must be inside the eruv and within walking distance of a shul, which means you
are bidding against every other frum family for the same few streets. This is
why frum-neighborhood prices decouple upward from their metro's median, and why
a metro-level figure understates what you will actually pay.

*Then the recurring items that don't vary as much but aren't small:*

- *Food.* Kosher meat, chalav Yisroel, and Pesach add a recurring premium.
  Communities where kosher is stocked in the mainstream supermarket (Kroger,
  Costco, Trader Joe's) are meaningfully cheaper than communities where
  everything ships in.
- *Yom tov and simchos.* Non-negotiable, poorly forecast, and larger in
  communities with strong social expectations.
- *The Israel year and seminary.* For most families, one to two years per child
  at roughly $25,000–$35,000 (est.), typically hitting when tuition is also at
  peak.
- *Health insurance*, if you are not covered through an employer — a large
  American number that the kollel conversation routinely omits.

*The way to actually compare two communities* is to price the same family in
both: purchase price of a house you would genuinely live in at the size you
need, plus annual property tax on it, plus state income tax on your expected
income, plus post-subsidy tuition for your actual number of children. Section
6.1 gives the inputs. Section 3.6 works an example.

** 1.4 Why frum housing demand never loosens

*Growth is endogenous.* Frum communities grow primarily from births, not from
moves. Lakewood's birth rate is among the highest of any American municipality,
and Kiryas Joel's population roughly doubled in a decade — from 33,507 in the
2020 census to about 43,863 by 2024 *(v, US Census)*, with growth continuing at
high single digits annually. This means demand for housing and school seats is
baked in years ahead of supply, everywhere, permanently.

*Turnover is low.* Frum families with four to eight children do not downsize and
move every seven years like the general market. Housing stock in frum
neighborhoods therefore trades far less often than the surrounding metro, which
tightens supply beyond what population figures alone suggest.

* Part 2 — Kollel

** 2.1 The categories

"Kollel" covers several genuinely different institutions that happen to share a
name. Conflating them produces bad decisions.

*The learning kollel.* A kollel whose purpose is Torah study, full stop. Beth
Medrash Govoha in Lakewood is the archetype and by far the largest — roughly
7,000–10,000 talmidim depending on how the count is drawn
*(v; published figures vary by source and by whether the mesivta and beis medrash divisions are included)*,
making it the largest yeshiva outside Eretz Yisroel. Similar institutions exist
at Ner Yisroel, Philadelphia, Chaim Berlin, Torah Vodaath, the Mir's American
branch, and dozens of smaller batei medrash. The yungerman's obligation is to
learn; there is no communal job attached.

*The community kollel.* A kollel planted in a community that has frum families
but not enough Torah infrastructure. The yungerleit learn a full seder, and in
exchange for the stipend they carry a communal load: night chavrusas with baalei
batim, shiurim, a morning kollel for retirees, learning programs for boys,
sometimes teaching in the local school. This is a /job/, and the community is a
stakeholder. It is the model the rest of this section is mostly about.

*The kollel horaah / dayanus kollel.* Semicha and beyond — yoreh yoreh, yadin
yadin, choshen mishpat. Aimed at producing rabbanim and dayanim. Detroit's
Kollel Hora'ah Bais Yehuda, the Choshen Mishpat kollelim in Detroit and Passaic,
and several of Baltimore's kollelim work in this space. Length is measured in
years, and the exit is a specific job.

*The kiruv kollel.* Outreach-first. The learning is real, but the mandate is to
reach Jews who are not yet frum. The Chofetz Chaim / Rabbinical Seminary of
America network, Torah Links (Lakewood-affiliated), Atlanta Scholars Kollel,
Tampa, Orlando, and the Jewish Study Network in Palo Alto operate in this space.
Different skill set, different personality fit, different metrics.

*The night kollel / baalebatishe kollel.* For men who work. Usually a small
stipend or none.

*The retiree kollel.* A growing category in relocation-oriented communities —
South Bend, San Antonio, Dunwoody, Silver Spring, Portland, Providence, and Oak
Park all list one. Not a parnassa; a reason for older people to move.

** 2.2 Ichud HaKollelim — the network, and what it tells you

*Ichud HaKollelim* is an arm of Beth Medrash Govoha. It became a formal entity
in 2022 after fourteen years as BMG's Community Development Office; the model
traces to the first community kollel, founded in Toronto in 1970. It reports
*125 kollelim and over 1,500 yungerleit* *(v, ichudhakollelim.org)*, with
offices in both the US and Eretz Yisroel. It does three things: helps
communities establish and run kollelim, supports kollel leadership and community
integration, and matches yungerleit to placements — including relocation and
acclimation help.

For anyone actually weighing a kollel position, this is the front door.

*Its published directory lists 112 kollelim across 66 cities.* The full list is
in Appendix A. Four things it shows:

1. *Concentration.* Chicago and Los Angeles each host seven listed kollelim;
   Baltimore, Cleveland, and Detroit six each. These are not "out-of-town
   communities that have a kollel" — they are Torah centers with internal
   variety, including chassidishe and Sephardi kollelim. Chicago has both, plus
   the Veitzener Kollel.
2. *Reach.* Savannah, Tucson, San Antonio, Norfolk, Rochester, Jacksonville,
   Indianapolis, and South Bend each carry one. A few dozen frum families can
   support a kollel, because the economics are a handful of stipends against a
   donor base, not a market.
3. *The in-town listings are thin by design.* Flatbush has one listing, Monsey
   three, Passaic three. That is a fact about what Ichud tracks, not about how
   much learning happens in those places.
4. *Directory quirks*, so you don't misread it: "Kollel Zichron Aryeh Leib – The
   Manhattan Beach Community Kollel" is filed under Manhattan but sits in
   Brooklyn; "Kollel Saint-Louis" is in France, not Missouri (St. Louis, MO has
   its own separate listing); "Blue Ridge Kollel" is filed under Waterbury. The
   taxonomy is loose.

** 2.3 The other networks

The OU's community directory classifies each community's local kollel by
network, which is a useful proxy for a community's center of gravity. Its
categories:
*Lakewood, Ner Yisroel, Chofetz Chaim, YU, Torah MiTzion, and unaffiliated.*

Reading across roughly sixty US community profiles:

- *Lakewood-affiliated* is the most common single label, and it appears in
  communities across the whole spectrum — including places whose shuls are
  Modern Orthodox. Denver, Boston, Pittsburgh, Houston, Southfield, Scranton,
  Northeast Philadelphia, South Bend, Bensalem, Orlando, Chesterfield, Oak Park,
  and Highland Park all list it.
- *Ner Yisroel* in Atlanta, Columbus, Cincinnati, Indianapolis, Dunwoody,
  Lincolnwood.
- *Chofetz Chaim / RSA* in Tampa, Cherry Hill, Rochester, Manalapan, Buffalo
  Grove, and behind Orlando Torah Academy and St. Louis's boys' yeshiva.
- *YU / Torah MiTzion* in Northbrook, Buffalo Grove, Lincolnwood, Silver Spring.
- *Unaffiliated* is common and does not mean weak: Memphis, Elizabeth, West
  Orange, Livingston, Providence, Norfolk, Richmond, Portland, San Antonio, San
  Diego, and Jacksonville all fall here.

Several communities list /multiple/ networks. Lincolnwood lists Lakewood, Ner
Yisroel, Torah MiTzion, YU, and unaffiliated simultaneously; Cherry Hill lists
Chofetz Chaim and Lakewood; Manalapan lists Chofetz Chaim, Lakewood, and Ner
Yisroel. That is a direct signal about how wide a community's tent actually is.

Not covered by either directory: Chabad's kollel network, the Israeli kollel
world (enormous — Part 5), most Sephardi kollelim, and the many kollelim
attached to individual yeshivos.

** 2.4 The money

Nobody publishes this cleanly, so treat everything here as a range with wide
error bars.

*US community kollel stipends* run from a few hundred dollars a month at the low
end to roughly *$1,500–$2,500/month* at established, well-funded kollelim
*(est.; synthesized from scattered community reporting — no authoritative published survey exists)*.
Older published references cite figures as low as $320/month; aspirational
benchmarks in the same discussions cite $400/week. The spread is real and it
tracks the kollel's donor base, not its Torah.

What the stipend is /for/ matters more than its size. The standard framing in
the community-kollel world is that the stipend plus low out-of-town cost of
living lets a family live on one-and-a-fraction incomes — the wife working,
often in chinuch or a profession, with the stipend covering the gap and buying
her a lower-pressure job than she'd otherwise need. It is rarely designed as a
sole family income.

So price the *package*, not the stipend:

- *Tuition remission* at the local mosdos — frequently worth more than the
  stipend itself in a high-tuition state
- *Housing* — assistance, a below-market rental, or in some communities a
  purchase subsidy
- *Health insurance* — a large US number that gets skipped in these
  conversations
- *Moving costs*
- *Term* — two years? five? open-ended? What happens at the end, and who
  decides?
- *Employer* — the kollel or the shul? Who can end it, and on what notice?
- *The wife's realistic local earning power*, which is a fact about the local
  economy, not about her résumé

*Relocation incentives* are now a real category:

- *Providence, RI* — $50,000 plus a year of free tuition and a $1,000 move-in
  stipend, for a small number of families *(v, reported 2023)*
- *New Orleans* — the "Minyan Project": housing or rental subsidy, moving
  allowance, low-cost day school tuition, free communal memberships
  *(self-rep.)*
- *Greater MetroWest NJ* — an $1,800 "Smart Move" grant plus Tuition Max,
  capping day school tuition at 18% of AGI for families earning roughly
  $150K–$300K *(v)*
- Albany, Rochester, Atlanta, Boston, Scranton, West Hartford, Lincolnwood,
  Livingston, Richmond, East Brunswick, Plano, Dayton and others list incentives
  in the OU directory *(self-rep.)*

** 2.5 What comes after

Most community-kollel yungerleit do not stay in kollel indefinitely. The
standard exits:

- *Chinuch* — rebbe, morah, menahel. Frequently the reason the kollel was placed
  where it was in the first place.
- *Rabbanus* — the local shul or one elsewhere, usually after semicha completed
  during the kollel years.
- *Klei kodesh adjacent* — kashrus and hashgacha, mikvah, chevra kadisha,
  sofrus, communal administration, mosdos development.
- *Out into the workforce* — the largest category by count, and the one the
  system prepares people for least well. Which is what Part 3 is about.

* Part 3 — Parnassa

Housing is a one-time decision. Parnassa is the thing that determines whether
the decision holds. This part covers what frum people actually do for a living,
where those jobs are, and the one variable — tuition subsidy — that swamps
salary differences between communities.

** 3.1 Klei kodesh

The chinuch and rabbanus track is the largest employer of yeshiva-educated men
in the frum world, and the compensation picture is sobering but not uniform.

*Rebbeim.* Starting salaries at stable, on-time-paying yeshivos in the New York
area run around *$28,000 for a half-day position*, with experienced rebbeim
around *$50,000 after roughly ten years* *(v, community reporting)*. National
averages for "yeshiva" positions sit around *$42,000*, typically ranging
*$35,000–$45,000* *(v, ZipRecruiter, Jan 2026)* — a figure that blends roles and
should be read loosely.

*The out-of-town advantage is real and underappreciated.* Out-of-town schools
frequently structure full-day positions — secular studies in the morning,
limudei kodesh in the afternoon — which can roughly double a rebbe's income
relative to a half-day in-town position. Out-of-town schools also tend to pay
more for degrees and certification. This inverts the usual in-town/out-of-town
intuition: for a rebbe specifically, out-of-town often pays /better/, not worse,
and the cost of living is lower on top of it.

*Moros are paid materially less than rebbeim in most schools*
*(v, widely reported; the gap is a known and openly discussed feature of the field).*

*Adjacent klei kodesh roles* with real demand: kashrus supervision and hashgacha
administration, mikvah operation, sofrus and STa"M, chevra kadisha, mosdos
development and fundraising (which pays better than teaching and is chronically
short of good people), and school administration.

** 3.2 Programming, tech, and remote work

This is the fastest-growing parnassa track in the frum world, in both directions
— frum people entering tech, and tech being the field that makes out-of-town
living work.

*Why it fits.* Software work is the rare high-income field that is genuinely
location-independent, genuinely schedule-flexible, and does not require a degree
pipeline that conflicts with a yeshiva education. Remote work removes the
specific frictions frum employees otherwise negotiate constantly: leaving early
Friday, Yom Tov clusters, kosher lunch logistics, and workplace culture built
around other people's calendars.

*Where the jobs actually are.* Two separate questions, and people conflate them:

/If you are working remotely,/ the community's local tech economy is close to
irrelevant. What matters is: reliable high-speed internet; a time zone that
works with your employer (Mountain and Pacific communities can be an asset or a
liability depending on where the company is); and — the one people miss —
whether the community /already has/ remote workers. A community where you are
the only person davening Mincha at 1:30 on a Tuesday is a lonely one, and it
usually also means no local shared-office setup, no local network, and no
informal referral chain when your contract ends.

/If you want a local tech job,/ the metros that genuinely have one, with a frum
community attached:

| Metro | Tech employers / anchors | Frum community |
|---+---+---|
| *Seattle* | Microsoft, Amazon, Google, Meta, Boeing, Costco, T-Mobile | Seward Park, Bikur Cholim–Machzikay Hadath, Sephardi community |
| *Boston* | One of the top US tech and biotech centers | Brighton, Brookline, Newton, Sharon, Malden |
| *Pittsburgh* | 1,800+ tech companies employing 86,000+ *(v, 2024)*; CMU, Pitt, Google | Squirrel Hill, Greenfield |
| *Denver / Boulder* | Broad tech sector, aerospace | Denver's East Side, ~7 shuls |
| *Dallas / Plano* | Toyota, AT&T, State Farm, UT Dallas | Dallas, Plano |
| *Columbus* | Intel fab under construction, Nationwide, Chase, Battelle, OSU | Columbus (Bexley / Eastmoor) |
| *Atlanta* | Home Depot, Coca-Cola, UPS, Delta, IBM, Cisco, Cox, Georgia Tech, CDC | Toco Hills, Dunwoody |
| *Chicago* | Broad, deep, diversified | West Rogers Park, Skokie, Lincolnwood, Northbrook, Buffalo Grove |
| *Phoenix / Scottsdale* | Semiconductor cluster, growing corporate base | Phoenix, Scottsdale |
| *Rochester / Albany* | Semiconductors, nanotech, optics, medical | Both have small established communities |
| *Baltimore / Silver Spring* | Federal, defense, cyber (NSA/Fort Meade corridor), Johns Hopkins | Very large frum communities |
| *NY metro* | Everything | Everything |

*In Eretz Yisroel*, charedi tech is now an actual sector rather than an
experiment. *KamaTech*, founded 2012, runs training and placement with Cisco,
Meta, Nokia, and others, operates the *Ampersand* co-working space in Bnei Brak,
and its portfolio companies have raised roughly $150 million and employ close to
1,000 people *(v)*. *Bizmax* plays a parallel role in Yerushalayim. Roughly
*7,000 charedi women* have come through these programs into Israeli high-tech
*(v)* — the women's track is substantially further along than the men's, which
is a real fact about the market and matters for a couple planning together.

*A realistic caution.* The entry-level software market is tighter than it was,
and "learn to code and work remotely" is now a crowded strategy rather than a
novel one. The people it works for tend to have (a) an actual portfolio or
shipped work, not just a course certificate, (b) a specialization, and (c) a
first job obtained through a personal connection. Budget for a longer runway
than the bootcamp marketing suggests, and treat the /first/ job as the hard
part.

** 3.3 The other frum-heavy fields

Worth knowing because they're the fields that already have infrastructure —
networks, mentors, and employers who understand a frum schedule.

- *Allied health and therapy* — OT, PT, speech, ABA/BCBA, social work, nursing.
  Very heavily represented, license-portable across states (with paperwork),
  strong demand in every community listed in this document, and Bensalem,
  Memphis, Louisville, Rochester and others explicitly cite therapy demand.
- *Healthcare* generally — most out-of-town communities are anchored on a
  hospital system, and it is usually the single largest local employer.
- *Real estate, mortgage, title, insurance* — frum-dense, relationship-driven,
  and one of the standard exits from kollel.
- *Accounting and finance* — the CPA path is well-worn and structurally
  compatible.
- *E-commerce, Amazon FBA, and wholesale* — high variance, low barrier, heavily
  frum, and much less reliable than its reputation.
- *Sales* — the highest-variance option, and the one that most often replaces a
  degree.
- *Trades and services* — undersupplied in almost every frum community and
  quietly lucrative in the growing ones (Lakewood, Toms River, Jackson, Monsey,
  Beitar, RBS).

** 3.4 The two-income structure

In the yeshivish and chassidish world, the normal configuration is that the
wife's income is primary or co-primary during the kollel years, and often stays
significant afterward. This makes the /local labor market for women/ a
first-order community variable, and it is almost never discussed in relocation
materials.

Questions worth asking about any community:

- What do women here actually do? (In small communities the honest answer is
  often: teach in the local school, or work remotely.)
- How many teaching positions exist, and are they filled by kollel wives
  already?
- Is there a local employer that hires professionally — a hospital system, a
  school district, a university, a large corporate?
- Is there a remote-work culture, or would she be the only one?
- Childcare: does it exist, what does it cost, and does it run on a schedule
  compatible with a frum calendar?

** 3.5 Tuition — the variable that dominates everything

*Usually the largest financial difference between American frum communities — and a function of state law rather than of the schools.*

*Read the sticker prices below as list prices, not as what families pay.* Three
mechanisms sit between the two, and an earlier draft of this document ignored
all of them:
- *Sibling discounts* are near-universal; one published model runs to a
  *40% maximum family discount (v)*.
- *Family caps on a percentage of AGI* are spreading:
  *12% under Chicago's TAP Fund*, *18% under Greater MetroWest's Tuition MAX*,
  and *15–18% at schools using that model (v)* —
  /regardless of how many children attend/. One documented case: a family at
  $150,000 AGI with four children in school paying *$13,156 per child*, not
  sticker *(v)*.
- *Vouchers and ESAs pay per child* and, in Indiana,
  *without any income test (v)*.

So a per-child band multiplied by five children overstates the real cost badly —
and overstates it /most/ in the expensive communities, which is where the caps
and scholarships do the most work.

*The high-cost baseline.* Average private-school tuition in Brooklyn runs
roughly $23,000 (elementary) and $25,000 (high school)
*(v, PrivateSchoolReview)*, though yeshivish schools in Brooklyn run far below
that — roughly *$7,000/year* for grades Pre-1A–8, with nursery and kindergarten
around $5,000 *(v, community reporting)*. Lakewood averages roughly
*$5,500–$6,500 per child*, with some schools up to $8,500 and a few outliers far
higher *(v)*. The Modern Orthodox day school world in the New York metro is
where the $25,000–$35,000 per child figures live.

New York State launched a pilot in 2025 offering *$15,000 per child per year*
(capped at $30,000 per family, and at 50% of tuition) to communal professionals
with children in one of 54 participating schools in NYC, Long Island, and
Westchester, beginning with the 2026–27 school year *(v, JTA)*. UJA-Federation
separately caps tuition at $16,350 per K–8 child, or 15% of AGI, for families
earning under $350,000 at participating schools *(v)*.

*The school-choice states are a different world.* As of 2025–26, broad ESA-style
or voucher programs operate in
*Arizona, Arkansas, Florida, Indiana, Iowa, New Hampshire, Ohio, Tennessee, Utah, West Virginia, and Wyoming*,
with *Texas launching universal access in 2026–27*
*(v, FutureEd / school-choice trackers)*. Concretely, from community sources:

- *Ohio (EdChoice Expansion)* —
  *$6,166 per child K–8 and $8,408 in high school*, available statewide, at
  *full value below 450% of the federal poverty level and prorated above it (v, Ohio Dept. of Education, 2026–27)*.
  /Community materials describing this as "regardless of income" are out of date — it is universal in availability but income-scaled in amount./
  Schools must accept the voucher as payment in full from households at or below
  200% FPL *(v)*. Cincinnati, Columbus, Cleveland and Dayton all sit inside
  this.
- *Indiana (Choice Scholarship)* —
  *income limits were removed entirely for 2026–27: 100% of families are eligible (v)*.
  The award is 90% of state per-student funding or actual tuition, whichever is
  lower — typically *$6,100–7,500 per child*, averaging *$6,536 (v)*. This is
  the only genuinely income-blind program among the states here, which makes it
  the most valuable to a high earner and the most reliable to everyone else.
  South Bend and Indianapolis both cite it; South Bend's tuition band is under
  $10K.
- *Arizona (ESA + STO tax credits)* — Phoenix and Scottsdale cite generous
  day-school scholarships at all income levels *(self-rep.)*. Phoenix's reported
  tuition band is under $10K.
- *Pennsylvania (EITC / Private School Tax Credits)* — funds school-run
  scholarships rather than paying families directly. Northeast Philadelphia,
  Bensalem, Scranton, and Harrisburg all cite it as the reason their tuition is
  low.
- *Florida* — roughly 60% of ESA-participating schools are religious, with
  recent growth among Jewish providers *(v)*. Jacksonville and Tampa report
  tuition under $10K.

*Practical translation.* Self-reported tuition bands from the OU directory,
which is the best like-for-like comparison available *(self-rep., 2026)*:

| Band | Communities reporting it |
|---+---|
| *Under $10K* | South Bend, Bensalem, Cincinnati, Rochester, Providence, Harrisburg, Jacksonville, Tampa, San Antonio, White Oak, Springfield/Longmeadow, Phoenix, Gush Etzion, Carmei Gat, Har Nof |
| *$11–15K* | Cherry Hill, Denver, Memphis, Pittsburgh, Oak Park, Houston, Las Vegas, Norfolk, Richmond, Scranton, Lincolnwood, Louisville, Indianapolis, Albany, Buffalo, Nashville, Portland, Overland Park, Omaha, Fleetwood, Livingston, West Orange, Manalapan, Hillside |
| *$15–20K* | Elizabeth, Dallas, Atlanta, Dunwoody, Chesterfield, Columbus, Southfield, Highland Park/Edison, East Brunswick, Buffalo Grove, Henderson |
| *$20–25K* | Silver Spring, Northbrook, West Hartford, San Diego, Scottsdale, Pinecrest/Miami, NE Fair Lawn |

*What the bands actually mean for a family.* They are per-child list prices.
Multiply one by five children and you get a number almost no family pays. What
matters instead:

- *In a voucher or ESA state*, the subsidy is per child and mostly income-blind,
  so a large family captures it five times over.
  *Indiana's average award is ~$6,536 per child with no income test (v)*;
  *Ohio's EdChoice Expansion pays $6,166 (K–8) and $8,408 (9–12), at full value below 450% of the federal poverty level and prorated above it (v)*.
- *In New York, New Jersey and Maryland*, there is no equivalent, so the family
  is dependent on the school's scholarship committee or an AGI cap where one
  exists. That makes the cost *income-tested*, and therefore unpredictable in a
  way a voucher is not.

*The consequence is that the tuition gap between communities grows with income.*
At a kollel or single-earner income, scholarship absorbs most of the difference
and the gap is perhaps $15,000–25,000 a year. At a two-professional income it
can exceed $40,000, because the voucher states keep paying and the scholarship
states stop. §3.6 works both cases.

*Caveat.* These are self-reported bands and they mix school types. Verify
against the actual schools you'd use, at your actual family size, with the
actual scholarship committee, before treating any of it as a number.

Three things follow from that table:

1. *Cheap houses and cheap carrying costs are not the same thing.* Cleveland
   Heights at $120/ft² with a 3.00% rate costs about
   *$10,000 a year in property tax on a $336,000 house*. Baltimore County at
   $200/ft² with a 1.07% rate costs about *$6,000 on a $560,000 house*. The
   cheaper house has the bigger annual bill.
2. *New Jersey is the outlier and it applies to most of the American yeshivish world.*
   Lakewood, Passaic, Highland Park, Cherry Hill, Toms River, Jackson,
   Elizabeth, Teaneck — every one of them sits in the highest-property-tax state
   in the country, and Passaic County's median annual bill is $7,544, with
   Clifton at $9,809 *(v)*.
3. *"No state income tax" usually means "high property tax."* Texas, and to a
   lesser degree Florida and Nevada, fund themselves this way. That is a good
   trade for a high earner and a bad one for a kollel family with a house,
   because property tax is charged on the asset regardless of income.

/Caution on published "effective rates."/ Several aggregators compute rate
against *assessed* rather than market value, which produces impossible figures
(one lists Edison, NJ at 5.92%). Where a rate looked wrong, the
*annual dollar bill* is quoted instead — Edison's median bill is *$9,568*, with
a typical range of $7,150–13,650 *(v)*.

*** State and local income tax — and why the headline rate misleads

Every table in this document that shows a "top marginal rate" is showing the
rate on the *last dollar of a very high income*. New Jersey's 10.75% starts
above *$1 million*. It says almost nothing about what a frum family earning
$120,000–$200,000 actually pays, and using it as a proxy inverts the ranking.

*Three things fall out of that, and all three contradict the headline-rate view:*

1. *Indiana costs a $150,000 family more than New Jersey does.* South Bend's
   2.95% state rate plus St. Joseph County's 1.75% comes to
   ~$7,050; Teaneck's graduated schedule comes to ~$5,500. The county add-on is
   invisible in the flat-rate figure, and Indiana's exemptions are small.
2. *The Ohio exemption is the quiet winner.* Zero tax on the first $26,050 makes
   Ohio the cheapest income-tax state with an income tax, at this income level.
3. *The two biggest local surcharges swamp the state differences.* NYC's
   *3.876%* costs a Brooklyn or Queens family about
   *$5,800 a year more than the same family in Monsey*, and Philadelphia's wage
   tax costs a Northeast Philadelphia family about
   *$5,600 a year more than Bensalem*, 25 minutes away in Bucks County. Those
   two lines are worth more than most interstate moves.

*When the top marginal rate /does/ matter:* above roughly $300,000, where New
Jersey, New York and California pull sharply away from the flat-tax states. A
$500,000 earner sees a genuine six-figure-over-a-decade difference between
Teaneck and Dallas. A $150,000 earner does not.
*Read the top-rate column in §6.1 as information about high earners, and this table as information about everyone else.*

*** A worked example — at two income levels, because the answer changes

The single biggest error in earlier drafts of this document was multiplying a
sticker-price tuition band by five children. *Almost nobody pays that.* Schools
use sibling discounts, and a growing number cap total family tuition at a
*percentage of AGI regardless of how many children attend* — 12% under Chicago's
TAP Fund, 15–18% at schools using that model, 18% under Greater MetroWest's
Tuition MAX *(v)*. Meanwhile the voucher states pay per child and
*Indiana does not means-test at all*.

That combination means the gap between communities *depends on income*, and
reverses in character as income rises.

*Family of seven, five children in school. Same house tier from §6.1.*

|  | *Teaneck, NJ* | *Cincinnati, OH* | *South Bend, IN* |
|---+---+---+---|
| Family house | $679,000–722,044 (v) | Golf Manor $280,236 (v) | $175,000–350,000 (v) |
| Property tax / yr | *$10,940 avg bill (v)* | *county median $3,898 (v)* | *~$3,000* (1% cap, v) |
| *At $150,000 income* |  |  |  |
| State + local income tax | *~$5,500* | *~$3,400* | *~$7,050* (incl. county 1.75%) |
| Tuition, 5 children | *~$22,500–27,000* if the school caps at 15–18% of AGI (v); unpredictable if it does not | EdChoice pays *$6,166/child K–8 (v)*, full award below 450% FPL → *~$30,800 of state money*; net cost modest | Indiana voucher *~$6,536/child avg (v)*, universal → *~$32,700*; often covers tuition in full |

| *Annual, before mortgage* | *~$92,000–104,000* | *~$59,000–74,000* | *~$55,000–62,000* |

*What this actually shows:*

*At $150,000 the gap is real but modest — roughly $25,000–30,000 a year, not the $85,000 an earlier draft claimed.*
Scholarship and caps absorb most of the tuition difference, and the income-tax
difference runs the /wrong/ way: Indiana costs this family more than New Jersey.

*At $400,000 the gap widens sharply, and for a structural reason.* Indiana's
voucher is *income-blind*, so a high earner in South Bend collects the same
~$32,700 as a kollel family. New Jersey scholarship is entirely income-tested,
so a high earner in Teaneck pays at or near sticker. Ohio sits between: EdChoice
tapers above 450% FPL.
*The tuition advantage of the voucher states is largest for the people who need it least.*

*The practical reading.* If you are a kollel family or a single-earner
household, the community-to-community cost difference is much smaller than the
sticker prices suggest, and *housing and property tax matter more than tuition*.
If you are a two-professional household earning well into six figures, tuition
becomes the dominant line and the voucher states pull decisively ahead.

*Caveats that matter.* AGI caps are *not universal* — "a few schools" use them
*(v)*, and most yeshivos negotiate case-by-case through a scholarship committee,
so the Teaneck figure is a /plausible/ outcome rather than a quoted price. Ohio
municipalities also levy local income tax that is not in the row above. And none
of this includes the mortgage, which on the house-price differences in §6.1 adds
its own five-figure annual gap.

** 4A — The New York / New Jersey core

This is where the majority of American frum Jews live, and it is the least
documented in relocation literature precisely because none of these places are
recruiting.

*LAKEWOOD, NJ*
/Yeshivish. The largest Torah center in the Western hemisphere./

- *Character:* Built around Beth Medrash Govoha, with a very large chassidish
  minority and dozens of distinct kehillos. Now effectively a city rather than a
  community — Westgate, Cedarbridge, Somerset, Coventry, Whispering Pines,
  Chateau and a dozen more sub-neighborhoods, each with its own feel.
- *Size:* Orthodox population estimated *over 100,000 (est.)*. Lakewood grew
  roughly *45–46% between 2010 and 2020* — the highest rate of any New Jersey
  municipality — and is now the state's fifth-largest city *(v, US Census)*.
  Births exceed 5,000 per year *(v)*, among the highest rates anywhere.
- *Mosdos:* Effectively unlimited. BMG (~7,000–10,000), scores of mesivtos, Bais
  Yaakovs, cheders, and post-seminary programs.
- *Kollel:* BMG plus a very large number of independent kollelim, night
  kollelim, and specialty kollelim. Not tracked in the Ichud community-kollel
  directory because that directory is about placing kollelim /elsewhere/ —
  Lakewood is the source, not a destination.
- *Housing — and the published medians are actively misleading here.*
  *Only 34.46% of Lakewood's housing units are detached single-family homes (v).*
  The township also contains large 55-plus communities —
  *Leisure Village alone is 2,433 attached homes at a $182,168 median*, and
  *Fairways at Lake Ridge adds 1,124 units at mid-$100Ks to mid-$400Ks (v)* —
  plus an enormous townhouse and duplex stock in the frum neighborhoods
  themselves. Every township-wide figure is dominated by that inventory:
  - Township median *list $379–399K*, median *sale $429K*, average sale
    *$465–495K* *(v, 2026)*
  - 08701 ZIP median sale *$489,900*; Zillow typical value *$560,611* *(v)*
  - *What a frum family actually buys: roughly $750,000–$950,000 (est.) existing*,
    and
    *$1.1–1.2 million for new 5-bedroom / 3.5-bath construction of 3,200–3,340 ft² (v)*
    — about *$372/ft² (v)*
  - Property tax *1.87–2.36%*, median bill *~$8,150 (v)* — roughly
    *$15,000–18,000/year* on a family house *(comp.)*
- *Why it matters:* Lakewood is no longer cheap, and the overflow it generates
  is the single largest force reshaping the American frum map.
- *Tuition:* Roughly *$5,500–$6,500 per child*, some schools to $8,500 *(v)* —
  remarkably low for the region, and a major reason the cost structure still
  works.
- *Parnassa:* Enormous internal frum economy — mosdos, real estate,
  construction, trades, healthcare, e-commerce, therapy, mortgage and title. NYC
  commute possible but long.
- *Note:* The Lakewood overflow is the origin story of Toms River, Jackson,
  Howell, Manchester, Brick, Barnegat, Bayonne, Cherry Hill's growth, and much
  of Northeast Philadelphia's.

*BROOKLYN, NY*
/Every tzibbur, at scale./

- *Boro Park* — Chassidish and yeshivish, extremely dense. The largest
  concentration of chassidishe kehillos outside Eretz Yisroel: Bobov, Satmar,
  Munkacz, Belz, Ger, Skver, Vizhnitz, Klausenberg and many more, each with its
  own shul, mosdos, and mikvah.
- *Williamsburg* — Overwhelmingly Satmar and allied chassidus, Yiddish-first,
  the most insulated large frum community in America.
- *Flatbush / Midwood / Marine Park* — Yeshivish and Modern Orthodox side by
  side, plus one of the largest Syrian and Sephardi communities in the world
  (Ocean Parkway, Gravesend). Enormous mosdos density.
- *Crown Heights* — Chabad world headquarters.
- *Size:* Brooklyn holds an estimated 600,000–800,000 Jews, the large majority
  Orthodox in the frum neighborhoods *(est.)*.
- *Kollel:* Uncountable. Ichud lists Kollel of Flatbush and the Manhattan Beach
  Community Kollel; the actual number of batei medrash with kollelim runs into
  the hundreds.
- *Housing:* Expensive and tight; multi-family, two-family, and apartment stock
  dominate. Single-family with land is a luxury product.
- *Parnassa:* The deepest frum business ecosystem anywhere. Also the highest
  cost of living.

*MONSEY / ROCKLAND COUNTY, NY*
/Chassidish, yeshivish, and everything between — in adjacent villages that are legally separate and culturally distinct./

- *Character:* Monsey proper is mixed chassidish/yeshivish. Surrounding it:
  *New Square* (Skver, ~7,000 *(v)*, entirely insular), *Kaser* (Vizhnitz),
  *Spring Valley*, *Airmont*, *Wesley Hills*, *Pomona*, *Suffern*. The OU
  directory lists *95 shuls and 6 mikvaos* in the Monsey area *(v)* — the
  highest shul count of any community in that directory by a wide margin.
- *Size:* Monsey CDP alone was *29,229 as of July 2025* *(v)*; the greater
  Rockland frum population is several times that.
- *Kollel:* Ichud lists three (Kollel Lomdei Shas, Kollel Zichron Shneur, Kollel
  Zichron Shmuel of Wesley Hills); the real count is far higher.
- *Housing — big houses on real lots, and the published $/ft² is misleading.*
  Monsey houses run *$475,000 to $2,999,988*, with a
  *median of $999,000 and an average sale of $1,019,957 (v, April 2026)*. The
  stock is large: current listings include homes of *5,000 ft²*,
  *4,828 ft² with 8 bedrooms and 6.5 baths*, and
  *4,700 ft² on a 0.25-acre lot (v)*. New 2025 construction lists at
  *$1.1–1.2M (v)*.
  - *On the $580/ft² figure (v):* that is the all-types rate, driven by Monsey's
    condo and townhouse stock. Applied to an actual Monsey house it gives
    $2.7–2.9 million, which is the /top/ of the range, not the middle.
    *For houses the real rate is closer to $215/ft²* — roughly $1.02M average
    over ~4,700–5,000 ft². Anyone comparing Monsey to another community on $/ft²
    must use the house rate, not the published one.
  - Median rent near *$2,500/month (v)*; values up over 20% since 2020 *(v)*.
    Rockland County property tax *1.77% (v)* — about *$18,000/year* on a median
    house *(comp.)*. Spring Valley and parts of Airmont are cheaper.
- *Parnassa:* NYC commute, plus a large internal economy. Extensive frum
  healthcare and services sector.

*KIRYAS JOEL / PALM TREE, NY*
/Satmar. The fastest-growing municipality in New York State./

- *Size:* *33,507 (2020 census) → ~43,863 (2024)* *(v, US Census)*, growing at
  high single digits to low double digits annually; projections put 2026 near
  48,000 *(v)*. Median age is extraordinarily low.
- *Character:* Yiddish-speaking, entirely Satmar-governed, with its own

- *Related:* Satmar satellites at Bloomingburg, and large chassidishe presences
  in Monroe, South Fallsburg, and the Catskills corridor generally.

*FIVE TOWNS & FAR ROCKAWAY, NY*
/Modern Orthodox and yeshivish, side by side, with a hard price gradient between them./

- *Character:* Lawrence, Cedarhurst, Woodmere, Hewlett, Inwood (the Five Towns)
  skew Modern Orthodox and affluent; Far Rockaway and Bayswater skew yeshivish
  and are markedly cheaper. Both have enormous mosdos density.
- *Mosdos:* HAFTR, HALB, Shulamith, Yeshiva Darchei Torah, Yeshiva of Far
  Rockaway, TAG, Bnos Bais Yaakov, and many more.
- *Kollel:* Community Kollel Avreichim of Lawrence/Far Rockaway (Ichud), plus
  multiple independent kollelim including at Darchei Torah.
- *Housing:* Far Rockaway median sale price *$701K (through May 2026)*
  *(v, Redfin)*; Five Towns listings run well above *$1M* *(v)*. The gap between
  the two, a few blocks apart, is the clearest illustration in America of
  hashkafa-driven price segmentation.
- *Parnassa:* Manhattan commute (LIRR), plus a large local professional and
  business community.

-----

*QUEENS, NY*
/Kew Gardens Hills, Forest Hills, Jamaica Estates, Hillcrest; plus large Bukharian and Sephardi communities./

- *Character:* Kew Gardens Hills is mixed yeshivish/Modern Orthodox and heavily
  Israeli and Bukharian; Jamaica Estates skews Modern Orthodox; Forest Hills has
  a very large Bukharian community.
- *Mosdos:* Yeshiva of Central Queens, Yeshiva Ketana of Queens, Bais Yaakov
  Academy, Torah Academy for Girls (Far Rockaway-adjacent), plus Yeshiva Chofetz
  Chaim (Rabbinical Seminary of America) in Forest Hills — the head of a
  national kiruv-kollel network.
- *Housing — and the published Kew Gardens Hills numbers are not house prices.*
  KGH has a very large co-op and garden-apartment stock, so the all-types median
  (*$350,000 over 12 months; $505,000 in Dec 2025; average sale $528,816*) is
  measuring apartments *(v)*.
  *Actual houses in Kew Gardens Hills range from $750,000 to $2,999,000 (v)*,
  with detached family homes comfortably over $1 million. Add NYC's
  *3.876% resident income tax* on top of the 10.9% state rate — a cost the Five
  Towns and Monsey do not carry.
- *Parnassa:* Manhattan commute, strong medical and professional presence.

-----

*STATEN ISLAND, NY*
/Willowbrook and the surrounding neighborhoods — mixed, and cheaper than Brooklyn./

- *Character:* Willowbrook is the established center, with Young Israel of
  Staten Island and the Jewish Foundation School; a substantial yeshivish and
  Sephardi population has grown alongside.
- *Housing:* Borough median *$755K, +3.4% year-over-year (2026)* *(v)*.
- *Parnassa:* Manhattan and Brooklyn commute; large healthcare sector.

-----

*PASSAIC / CLIFTON, NJ*
/Yeshivish, dense, and one of the strongest mid-size frum communities in America./

- *Character:* Predominantly yeshivish with a significant Modern Orthodox
  presence in Passaic Park and a growing chassidish population. Deep mosdos and
  a walkable, high-density layout.
- *Kollel:* Three in the Ichud directory — the Passaic Clifton Community Kollel,
  Kollel Choshen Mishpat of Passaic Clifton, and Kollel Birchas Shmuel at Bais
  Medresh L'Torah. The choshen mishpat kollel makes Passaic one of the few
  places outside Israel with a serious dayanus track.
- *Housing — the city figure and the frum figure are different markets.*
  *Passaic Park, the frum neighborhood, carries a median real estate price of $856,367 and a median sale of $645,000 (v, +24% year-over-year)*.
  Passaic /city/ reads *$543,000–$555,666 (v)* because it blends in the large,
  much cheaper multi-family stock of a city whose median household income is
  $55,371 *(v)*. Passaic's citywide $390/ft² is a higher rate per foot than
  Teaneck's $350 *(v)*. Neighboring *Clifton is $625,000 at $335/ft² (v)*.
  Property tax: *Passaic County's median annual bill is $7,544 at 1.97%*;
  *Clifton's median bill is $9,809 (v)*.
- *Parnassa:* NYC commute (bus/rail), large local professional community,
  substantial frum business base.

-----

*BERGEN COUNTY, NJ — Teaneck, Bergenfield, Fair Lawn, Englewood, Passaic-adjacent*
/Predominantly Modern Orthodox, at large scale./

- *Character:* Teaneck is one of the largest and most institutionally dense
  Modern Orthodox communities in the world — the OU directory lists
  *20 shuls and 6 schools* in the Teaneck area *(v)* — including Shaarei Orah, a
  substantial Sephardi kehilla. Bergenfield, New Milford, Fair Lawn, and
  Englewood extend it.
- *Mosdos:* TABC, Ma'ayanot, Frisch, Yavneh, RYNJ, Ben Porat Yosef, and more.
- *Housing.* Teaneck is one of the least condo-contaminated markets in this
  document:
  *73.78% of its housing units are detached single-family homes, and 61.5% are three- or four-bedroom (v)*
  — so its published figures mean roughly what they appear to mean. Median home
  cost *$722,044*, median transaction *$679,000*, single-family sale median
  *$570,000*, at *$350/ft² (v)*. Property tax is the sting: Teaneck's
  *average bill is $10,940*, ranging to *$14,700*, at a *2.05% effective rate*;
  *Bergenfield's median bill is $10,421 at 3.41%*, and
  *Bergen County averages 2.73% (v)* — among the highest municipal rates in the
  country. Stack that on NJ's 10.75% top income rate and a $20–25K tuition band
  and Bergen County is the most expensive combined package in this document.
- *Tuition:* Among the highest in the country — Fair Lawn reports *$20–25K*
  *(self-rep.)*, and NJ has no ESA program.
- *Parnassa:* Manhattan commute; very high professional density (law, medicine,
  finance).

-----

*HIGHLAND PARK / EDISON, NJ*
/Genuinely mixed — one of the few large communities where yeshivish and Modern Orthodox families share schools and shuls without friction./

- *Size:* Over *1,500 shomer Shabbos families* *(self-rep.)*; the OU listing
  shows *8 shuls, 20 minyanim, 6 schools, 1 kollel*.
- *Mosdos:* RPRY, Netivot Montessori, Yeshiva Shaarei Tzion, plus high school
  options locally and nearby.
- *Kollel:* Highland Park Community Kollel (Ichud, Lakewood-affiliated).
- *Housing:* The self-reported *$400–599K* band understates the family-home
  market. Single-family homes sold in Highland Park over the past year
  *averaged $649,579*, with an August 2026 median listing price of *$589K (v)*;
  Zillow's typical value of $494,826 and Redfin's $500K median both reflect the
  borough's condo and smaller-home stock *(v)*. Rentals
  *$1,500–2,000 (self-rep.)*. The recurring cost is the tax — Edison's median
  annual property tax bill is *$9,568*, typically ranging *$7,150–$13,650 (v)*,
  and Highland Park's rate is comparable.
- *Parnassa:* Rutgers, Johnson & Johnson, Bristol-Myers Squibb, RWJ and Saint
  Peter's hospitals; NYC rail commute.
- *Character note:* The community explicitly markets itself on "middos over
  materialism" and low-key living. That is a real cultural signal, and it is the
  main reason people choose it over Bergen County.

-----

*ELIZABETH & HILLSIDE, NJ*
/Elmora / Elmora Hills. Compact, established, institutionally strong for its size./

- *Mosdos:* Anchored on the *Jewish Educational Center (JEC)* — coed lower
  school, then separate JEC boys' and Bruriah girls' middle and high schools.
  Bruriah sits in Hillside.
- *Kollel:* Two listed (unaffiliated). Hillside lists two as well.
- *Housing:* Elizabeth *$600–799K*; Hillside notably cheaper at *$200–399K*
  *(self-rep.)*.
- *Tuition:* Elizabeth *$15–20K*; Hillside *$11–15K* *(self-rep.)*.
- *Parnassa:* Direct transit to Manhattan, Brooklyn, and NJ employment centers.
- *Position:* Short drive to Lakewood, Passaic, and Teaneck — Elizabeth's main
  structural advantage.

-----

*TOMS RIVER, JACKSON, HOWELL, MANCHESTER, BRICK, NJ*
/The Lakewood overflow belt. Growing faster than almost anywhere in America./

- *Toms River (North Dover):* Over *2,000 Orthodox families* have moved in over
  the past few years *(v)*. Predominantly yeshivish and chassidish. The
  township's first mesivta — a *Belzer mosad* for ages 14–17 — received
  unanimous zoning approval *(v)*. Median home price
  *$543K, +8.5% year-over-year* *(v, Redfin)*.
- *Jackson:* Several thousand frum families *(v)*. Agudath Israel settled a
  seven-year land-use lawsuit against the township; Jackson's first yeshiva with
  a dormitory was approved in 2023 *(v)*.
- *Character:* These are new communities in the literal sense — infrastructure
  is being built now, zoning fights are live and ongoing, and the relationship
  with long-time residents is contentious in a way that shows up in local
  politics.
- *Who it's for:* Families who want Lakewood's ecosystem at a lower price and
  are willing to live somewhere still under construction.

-----

*BAYONNE, NJ*
/Small, deliberate, and being built./

- *Character:* Explicitly recruiting — "ground floor" positioning.
  Lakewood-style kollel with daily minyanim; diverse hashkafa; walkable town.
- *Mosdos:* Local options thin; schools are in Elizabeth (JEC, Bruriah, 15 min)
  and Passaic (20 min).
- *Housing:* Affordable by NJ metro standards, with substantial rental stock
  *(self-rep.)*.
- *Parnassa:* 20 minutes to NYC.

-----

*CHERRY HILL, NJ*
/South Jersey. Mixed, growing, and structurally underrated./

- *Character:* The West Side is the frum center. Genuinely mixed — the OU
  listing shows Ashkenaz /and/ Sefard nusach, a Young Israel, a Torah Links
  branch, and a Chabad, with *Chofetz Chaim /and/ Lakewood* kollel affiliations
  *(v)*. That combination in one small community is unusual.
- *Mosdos:* Politz Day School (elementary and middle) locally; Philadelphia
  yeshivos and day schools a short commute away. The OU listing counts
  *10 schools* in reach and *4 shuls, 2 mikvaos, 1 kollel* locally.
- *Housing:* *$450,000–$600,000* typical *(self-rep., 2026)*; rentals
  *$2,000–2,500*.
- *Tuition:* *$11–15K* band *(self-rep.)* — and Politz benefits from
  Pennsylvania-adjacent options plus NJ's absence of a voucher program is a real
  constraint here.
- *Position:* About an hour to Lakewood, 90 minutes to New York, under two hours
  to Baltimore. Multiple weekly food deliveries from Lakewood establishments
  *(self-rep.)*.
- *Parnassa:* Philadelphia's hospitals, universities, and professional sector;
  Center City is a straightforward commute.

*WATERBURY, CT*
/Yeshiva-first. A community that exists because a yeshiva was put there./

- *Character:* Built around *Yeshiva Ateres Shmuel of Waterbury* and its
  associated mosdos, with a strong baal teshuva and "second-chance" reputation
  in the mesivta world. Yeshivish.
- *Kollel:* Kollel Avreichim of Waterbury, plus the Blue Ridge Kollel listing.
- *Housing:* Cheap by Northeast standards *(est.)*.
- *Note:* Its identity is tied to the yeshiva's; evaluate the yeshiva, not just
  the town.

*MANALAPAN, EAST BRUNSWICK, WEST ORANGE, LIVINGSTON, FLEETWOOD (Westchester), NJ/NY*
/Mid-size suburban communities, mostly Modern Orthodox, mostly commuter./

- *Manalapan* — Surprisingly deep for its size:
  *5 shuls, 11 schools in reach, 1 kollel*, listing
  *Chofetz Chaim, Lakewood, and Ner Yisroel* affiliations. Housing *$400–599K*,
  tuition *$11–15K* *(self-rep.)*. 20–30 min to the Jersey Shore and Deal's
  Syrian community.
- *East Brunswick* — Young Israel of East Brunswick, ~200 families, one-shul
  community. Housing *$600–799K*, tuition *$15–20K* *(self-rep.)*. Rutgers,
  Princeton, J&J, BMS nearby.
- *West Orange* — AABJ&D and Ohr Torah anchor it, with a growing Sephardi
  kehilla. Kushner/JEC schools. Housing *$400–599K* *(self-rep.)*.
- *Livingston* — Suburban Torah and Etz Chaim; strong special-needs
  infrastructure (Friendship Circle, LifeTown). Housing *$600–799K*
  *(self-rep.)*.
- *Fleetwood, NY* — Small Westchester community, 30 minutes to Grand Central by
  Metro-North, at a fraction of typical Westchester cost. Mikvaos and kosher
  food in Riverdale/New Rochelle, 10–15 min. Housing *$600–799K* *(self-rep.)*.

** 4B — The Mid-Atlantic

-----

*BALTIMORE, MD*
/Yeshivish at scale, with real internal variety. One of the three or four most important Torah centers in America./

- *Character:* Centered on Park Heights, Greenspring, Cheswolde, and Pikesville.
  Predominantly yeshivish, with substantial Modern Orthodox, chassidish, and
  Sephardi communities and a famously wide range of shuls. Baltimore's
  self-image as a place where different types of frum Jews coexist without
  ideological warfare is largely earned.
- *Mosdos:* *Ner Yisroel* (Yeshivas Ner Yisroel) anchors the town and seeds
  kollelim nationally. Plus Talmudical Academy, Torah Institute, Bais Yaakov of
  Baltimore, Bnos Yisroel, Ohr Chadash, Beth Tfiloh, and many more.
- *Kollel:* *Six in the Ichud directory alone* — Baltimore Community Kollel,
  Kollel Avreichim of Baltimore, Kollel D'Baltimore, Kollel Nachlas Yosef,
  Kollel Zichron Yehoshua Heschel, Kollel of Greenspring — plus Ner Yisroel's
  own kollel and others outside that registry. One of the deepest kollel
  ecosystems in the country.
- *Housing:* Baltimore city median *$285,000, +11.8% year-over-year*;
  Baltimore–Columbia–Towson metro *$380,000 (Oct 2025)* *(v)*. Frum
  neighborhoods run above both — Greenspring and upper Park Heights meaningfully
  so *(est.)*.
- *Tuition:* Mid-range. Maryland's BOOST program is small relative to Ohio or
  Indiana; Baltimore's affordability comes from scale and low housing cost, not
  from state subsidy *(est.)*.
- *Parnassa:* Federal, defense, and cyber (the Fort Meade / NSA corridor), Johns
  Hopkins and the medical sector, plus a very large internal frum business
  economy. DC commute possible.
- *Why it matters:* Baltimore is the standard American answer to "I want a big
  yeshivish community and I cannot afford Lakewood or Brooklyn."

-----

*SILVER SPRING / KEMP MILL, MD (Greater Washington)*
/Mixed, professional, and expensive./

- *Character:* Kemp Mill and White Oak. Silver Spring Jewish Center and Young
  Israel Shomrai Emunah anchor it. The community's self-description emphasizes
  range of background and openness; the OU listing counts
  *7 shuls, 2 mikvaos, 8 schools, 1 kollel*.
- *Kollel:* Greater Washington Community Kollel — listed as
  *Torah MiTzion and unaffiliated*, which correctly signals a different flavor
  from a Lakewood-model kollel.
- *Housing:* *$600–799K* band, rentals *$2,000–2,500* *(self-rep.)*.
- *Tuition:* *$20–25K* *(self-rep.)* — among the highest bands in this document.
- *Parnassa:* Federal government, contractors, NIH, law, policy. Extremely high
  professional density and correspondingly high salaries, which is the trade for
  the tuition.

-----

*PHILADELPHIA, PA*
/Three distinct frum geographies, and one of the best tuition situations in the country./

- *Northeast Philadelphia* — Yeshivish and growing hard. Over
  *50 families in recent years* *(self-rep.)*. The OU listing counts
  *6 shuls, 3 mikvaos, 15 schools in reach, 1 kollel*, Lakewood-affiliated.
  Housing — the community's own figures are
  *rent a 2–3 bedroom home for $1,500–2,000/month; buy for $350K with about $3,000/year in property taxes*
  *(self-rep.)*. /The rent holds. The purchase price does not describe a house a
  growing family buys./ Bustleton and Somerton run
  *$389,500–444,000 all-types median at $267/ft² (v, 2026)*, and that median is
  itself carried by the rowhouse and twin stock; detached four-bedrooms on the
  frum blocks list at *$500,000–550,000 (v, Aug 2026)*. The *$3,000* tax bill is
  real but is a lag rather than a rate — Philadelphia charges *1.3998%* on
  assessed value less a *$100,000 homestead exemption (v)*, so $3,000 implies an
  assessment near $314,000 against a market price well above it, and the
  citywide revaluation lands for *tax year 2027 (v)*. Carrying costs here are
  still genuinely low for the Northeast corridor. They are not $350K-and-$3,000
  low. 90 minutes to NYC, just over an hour to Lakewood, 2 hours to Baltimore;
  Trenton station gives a 50-minute train to Penn Station.
- *Overbrook Park / Lower Merion / Bala Cynwyd* — Older established community,
  mixed Modern Orthodox and yeshivish, home to *Yeshivas Philadelphia*
  (Philadelphia Yeshiva), one of the historically most influential American
  yeshivos.
- *Somerton* — Smaller, its own kollel.
- *Kollel:* Three in the Ichud directory: Philadelphia Community Kollel,
  Northeast Community Kollel, Somerton Community Kollel.
- *Tuition:* Pennsylvania's *EITC / Private School Tax Credit* program funds
  large school-run scholarships. Northeast Philadelphia describes tuition as
  "astonishing" in the good direction *(self-rep.)* — this is the single biggest
  reason the region is growing.
- *Parnassa:* Philadelphia's universities, hospitals, and Fortune 500 base; the
  community runs a resume-matching service *(self-rep.)*.

-----

*BENSALEM, PA (Bucks County)*
/Small, warm, and cheap. A satellite done deliberately./

- *Character:* Under Rabbi and Mrs. Moshe Travitsky. One shul, one kollel
  (Lakewood-affiliated), mikvah, eruv, preschool, and an active outreach
  organization. Schools, kosher shopping, and dining are a 15–20 minute drive
  (Northeast Philadelphia).
- *Housing:* Homes from *$350K*, apartments within the eruv *(self-rep.)* —
  that is the entry rung; the township's all-types median is
  *$409,777–450,000 (v, 2026)* against a Bucks County median of *$379,000 (v)*.
  The community reports Bucks County taxes lower than surrounding areas
  *(self-rep.)*; the median Bensalem bill is *$4,977 (v)*, which beats Lakewood
  and Passaic comfortably but is roughly what a Northeast Philadelphia detached
  house will cost once its assessment catches up. /What Bensalem actually buys
  you is the missing 3.74% wage tax, not a cheaper house./
- *Tuition:* *Under $10K* band, via PA EITC *(self-rep.)*.
- *Position:* 25 minutes to Northeast Philadelphia, under an hour to Lakewood,
  under 90 minutes to Brooklyn.
- *Parnassa:* Broad professional market; the community runs a local Jewish job
  network of business owners and reports heavy demand for therapists
  *(self-rep.)*.

-----

*SCRANTON, PA*
/Yeshivish, institutionally dense for its size, and inexpensive./

- *Mosdos:*
  *3 shuls, 2 elementary schools, 3 girls' high schools, a yeshiva, and a kollel*
  *(v, self-rep.)* — an unusually complete stack for a town this size, driven by
  the yeshiva's dormitory population.
- *Kollel:* Scranton Community Kollel, Lakewood-affiliated.
- *Housing:* Spacious homes in the Hill Section around *$400K*; the OU band is
  *$200–399K*, rentals *$800–1,500* *(self-rep.)*.
- *Tuition:* *$11–15K* band, with PA EITC *(self-rep.)*.
- *Position:* Two hours to NY/NJ and Philadelphia; near the Poconos.

-----

*HARRISBURG, PA*
/Small, Modern Orthodox, very affordable./

- *Mosdos:* Kesher Israel (daily minyanim, mikveh, eruv); The Silver Academy,
  PreK–8, sharing a new campus with the JCC.
- *Housing:* *$200–399K*, rentals *$800–1,500* *(self-rep.)*.
- *Tuition:* *Under $10K* *(self-rep.)*.
- *Limits:* No local high school. Plan for that.

-----

*PITTSBURGH, PA*
/Squirrel Hill and Greenfield. Urban, walkable, genuinely mixed, and a real tech city./

- *Character:* Over 80 years of continuous Jewish life in Squirrel Hill. Full
  spectrum — yeshivish, Modern Orthodox, Chabad, Sephardi. The OU listing counts
  *6 shuls, 2 schools, 1 kollel*.
- *Kollel:* Kollel Jewish Learning Center (Beacon Street), Lakewood-affiliated.
- *Housing:* *$400–599K* band, rentals *$1,500–2,000* *(self-rep.)*.
- *Tuition:* *$11–15K*, with PA EITC *(self-rep.)*.
- *Housing — and note which Squirrel Hill.* The frum community lives mainly in
  *Squirrel Hill South* and *Greenfield*, not Squirrel Hill North.
  *Squirrel Hill South's median single-family sale is $525,000 at $333/ft²; Squirrel Hill North's is $835,000 at $341/ft² (v, 2026)*
  — the North figure is an affluent sub-neighborhood and should not be read as
  the community's price. Allegheny County assesses at a common level ratio of
  about 50%, so the 2.22%-of-assessed rate works out near
  *1.1% of market value (v)*.
- *Parnassa:* Carnegie Mellon, University of Pittsburgh, world-class hospital
  systems, and *over 1,800 tech companies employing 86,000+ people as of 2024*
  *(v)* — Google, BNY Mellon, PNC. One of the best programming markets on this
  list relative to cost of living.

*WHITE OAK, PA*
/20 minutes from Pittsburgh. Very small, very cheap./

- *Housing:* Homes from about *$180,000* *(self-rep.)*; rentals under *$800* —

- *Mosdos:* One shul, one kollel, two Orthodox day schools in Pittsburgh with

- *Tuition:* *Under $10K* *(self-rep.)*.
- *Who it's for:* Someone who wants Pittsburgh's job market at a fraction of
  Pittsburgh's housing cost, and is comfortable in a very small kehilla.

*NORFOLK / VIRGINIA BEACH, VA*
/Small, walkable, and near a Navy-and-research economy./

- *Character:* The frum community lives mostly in Norfolk's *Ghent* neighborhood
  — walkable, urban, near downtown and major hospitals including a well-regarded

- *Kollel:* The Norfolk Kollel (unaffiliated).
- *Housing:* Homes within the eruv in the *$400–600Ks* *(self-rep.)*.
- *Tuition:* *$11–15K* *(self-rep.)*.
- *Parnassa:* The world's largest naval base, NASA Langley, universities, and
  hospitals.

*RICHMOND, VA*
/Small and growing, with a kollel-style program./

- *Mosdos:* Multiple minyanim, an active Va'ad Hakashrus, a new mikvah, a large

** 4C — The Midwest

The Midwest is where the out-of-town model works best: real cities with real
economies, deep frum infrastructure built over generations, cheap housing, and —
in Ohio and Indiana especially — the best tuition situation in the country.

-----

*CHICAGO, IL*
/West Rogers Park, Peterson Park, Lincolnwood, Skokie, Northbrook, Buffalo Grove. A major Torah center with genuine internal variety./

- *Character:* The West Rogers Park / Lincolnwood / Skokie corridor is one
  continuous eruv-linked Jewish hub. The OU listings count
  *30–34 shuls and 18–23 schools* across the corridor *(v)*. Every tzibbur is
  represented: yeshivish, chassidish (the Veitzener Kollel and Kollel Zichron
  Yitzchok Zev / Chicago Chassidishe Kollel), Sephardi (Sephardic Community
  Kollel of Chicago), Modern Orthodox, and Chabad.
- *Kollel:* *Seven in the Ichud directory* — Chicago Community Kollel, Kollel
  Ateres Ami, Kollel Tiferes Yisroel, Kollel Zichron Eliyahu, Kollel Zichron
  Yitzchok Zev, Sephardic Community Kollel, Veitzener Kollel — plus Kollel Torah
  MiTzion. Lincolnwood alone lists
  *Lakewood, Ner Yisroel, Torah MiTzion, YU, and unaffiliated* kollel
  affiliations simultaneously, which is about as wide a tent as exists anywhere.
- *Mosdos:* Telshe Chicago, Hebrew Theological College, Arie Crown, Joan Dachs
  Bais Yaakov, Ida Crown, Hanna Sacks, Yeshivas Tiferes Tzvi, and many more.
- *Housing.* The published medians mislead badly, and in both directions. West
  Ridge's /all-types/ median sale is *$236,500–$262,000 (v, 2026)* — that figure
  is dominated by the neighborhood's large condo, co-op and two-to-four-flat
  stock. *Detached single-family homes run about $500,000–$535,000*, with larger
  four- and five-bedroom houses at or just under $500K *(v)*. The cross-check
  that proves the point: neighboring
  *Skokie prices at $272–276/ft² and Lincolnwood at $261/ft² (v)*, both /above/
  West Ridge's all-types $189–208/ft² — which means the low figure is measuring
  apartments, not houses. On a single-family basis a 2,800 ft² house on the frum
  blocks is roughly *$730,000–$770,000 (est.)*. Nearby:
  *Peterson Park's median sale is $520,000*, Sauganash averages *$541,752 (v)*.
  Property tax: *Cook County's effective rate is 1.91%* with a median bill of
  *$6,191*; the City of Chicago runs 1.8–2.0% with a median bill of $4,814 *(v)*
  — call it *$12,000–14,000/year* on a family house.
- *Tuition:* Lincolnwood *$11–15K*; Northbrook and Buffalo Grove *$15–25K*
  *(self-rep.)*. Illinois has no broad ESA program.
- *Suburbs:* *Northbrook* (YU-affiliated kollel, $400–599K homes),
  *Buffalo Grove* (Torah Academy PreK–8, Northern Lights Kollel, listing Chofetz
  Chaim/Lakewood/YU affiliations, $600–799K), *Skokie* (large, established,
  mixed).
- *Parnassa:* Deep, diversified metro economy — finance, healthcare, logistics,
  tech, professional services.

-----

*CLEVELAND, OH*
/Cleveland Heights, University Heights, Beachwood, South Euclid. Deep, old, and cheap./

- *Character:* One of the strongest yeshivish communities in the Midwest,
  alongside a substantial Modern Orthodox community. The OU listing shows
  *13 shuls* in Cleveland Heights alone *(v)*; the frum population is spread
  across Cleveland Heights, University Heights, Beachwood, Shaker Heights, and
  South Euclid.

* Part 5 — Eretz Yisroel

Israel is not "America with better food." The housing market, the school system,
the kollel economy, and the legal status of a ben Torah all work on entirely
different principles. This part covers the structure, the numbers, and — because
it currently dominates everything else — the political situation.

-----

** 5.1 How the map is organized

Israeli frum geography sorts into five categories, and mixing them up produces
bad expectations.

1. *Yerushalayim* — the largest and most internally varied charedi population in
   the world, spread across a dozen neighborhoods with distinct characters and
   price levels.
2. *The charedi cities* — Bnei Brak, Modiin Illit (Kiryat Sefer), Beitar Illit,
   Elad, and Beit Shemesh/RBS. Municipally charedi, purpose-built or effectively
   so, with the full infrastructure and the full social norms.
3. *Charedi neighborhoods inside mixed cities* — Ashdod, Netanya, Petach Tikva,
   Rechovot, Haifa, Tzfas, Tveria, Kiryat Gat, Netivot, Rechasim. Cheaper,
   smaller, more variable.
4. *Religious Zionist and Anglo towns and yishuvim* — Efrat, Gush Etzion,
   Modiin, Chashmonaim, Ma'ale Adumim, Ra'anana, Karnei Shomron, and dozens of
   smaller yishuvim.
5. *The periphery* — the Negev, the Galil, and deep Yehuda and Shomron. Cheapest
   housing in the country, thinnest infrastructure, longest commutes.

-----

** 5.2 Yerushalayim

*Price context.* Four-room apartments in Yerushalayim range roughly
*₪2.5 million to ₪5.5 million* depending on neighborhood *(v)*. Neve Yaakov, one
of the more affordable areas, starts around *₪27,000 per square meter*
*(v, 2026)*. The central and prestige neighborhoods run far above that.

*Rental figures by neighborhood (2026)*
*(v, aggregator data — but read with care; some of these figures are inconsistent with what the neighborhoods are generally understood to cost, which usually means the sample is skewed by apartment size rather than by price per meter):*

| Neighborhood | Average monthly rent |
|--------------+----------------------|
| Sanhedria    | ₪4,533               |
| Har Nof      | ₪5,977               |
| Ramot        | ₪7,596               |
| Neve Yaakov  | ₪7,966               |
| Ramat Eshkol | ₪10,317              |

*Har Nof* is the reference case for Anglo charedi Yerushalayim. The community
reports
*over 50 kollelim in the neighborhood, more than 20 of which have English representation or run in English*
*(v, community report via OU)*. There are well over 30 shuls and minyanim, an
active neighborhood municipality with a dedicated coordinator for
English-speaking olim, and Project Connect, a grassroots organization running
English shiurim and chesed. Both charedi and national-religious populations. OU
housing band: *over ₪1 million equivalent*, rentals ₪2,000–2,500 equivalent,
tuition under $10K.

*Other neighborhoods, in brief:*
- *Ramot* — Large, mixed charedi and national-religious, sub-neighborhoods
  (Ramot Aleph, Bet, Gimmel, Polin) with distinct characters. Relatively
  affordable for Yerushalayim.
- *Sanhedria / Sanhedria Murchevet* — Charedi, central, older stock, walkable to
  Geula.
- *Ramat Eshkol / Ma'alot Dafna / Arzei Habira* — Heavily Anglo, mixed charedi
  and national-religious, expensive.
- *Neve Yaakov / Pisgat Ze'ev* — Northern, most affordable, mixed populations,
  growing Anglo charedi presence.
- *Geula / Meah Shearim / Beis Yisroel* — The historic charedi core.
  Chassidish-dense, old housing, expensive per meter for what you get.
- *Bayit Vegan* — Established, charedi-yeshivish, home to major yeshivos.
- *Ramat Shlomo* — Charedi, newer, north Jerusalem.
- *Rechavia / Talbiya / Old Katamon / Baka* — National-religious and Anglo,
  expensive to very expensive.
- *Givat Ze'ev* — Just outside the city, charedi neighborhoods, cheaper.
- *Telzstone (Kiryat Ye'arim)* — Small charedi town 10 minutes west of
  Yerushalayim, heavily Anglo, purpose-built, with its own mosdos.

** 5.3 The charedi cities

*BNEI BRAK*
- The densest and most institutionally important charedi city in the world.
  Ponevezh, Slabodka, Chevron-adjacent, Vizhnitz, Lelov, and dozens of other
  yeshivos and chassidus centers.
- Extremely dense; very little green space; old housing stock in much of the
  city.
- *Average rent ₪4,397/month (early 2026)* *(v)*.
- *Ampersand*, the KamaTech charedi co-working space, is here — the center of
  the charedi tech sector *(v)*.
- Anglo presence exists but is smaller proportionally than in Yerushalayim or
  RBS.

*MODIIN ILLIT (KIRYAT SEFER)*
- *Population 89,627 (2024)* *(v)* — the largest Jewish community in Yehuda and
  Shomron.
- Purpose-built charedi, primarily Lithuanian-yeshivish with a large chassidish
  population. Sub-neighborhoods: Kiryat Sefer, Achuzat Brachfeld, Green Park,
  Kiryat HaRabanim.
- Among the more affordable serious charedi options; comparable to or below RBS
  on price *(v)*.
- 30–40 minutes to both Yerushalayim and the Tel Aviv area, which is its
  structural advantage.

*BEITAR ILLIT*
- *Population 72,964 (February 2026), with 55.4% under the age of 18* *(v)* —
  one of the youngest populations of any city on earth.
- Heavily chassidish (Ger, Belz, Vizhnitz, Sanz, Toldos Aharon and others)
  alongside a Lithuanian population.
- *5,000 new housing units approved August 2025 — nearly doubling the city — plus 230 more approved December 2025*
  *(v)*. Expect substantial construction and substantial disruption.
- *Average rent ₪4,592/month* *(v)*.
- 20 minutes to Yerushalayim.

*ELAD*
- Purpose-built charedi city near Rosh Ha'ayin, notable for a large Sephardi
  charedi population alongside Ashkenazi.
- Closer to the Tel Aviv job market than Beitar or Modiin Illit.

*BEIT SHEMESH / RAMAT BEIT SHEMESH*
- *Population surpassed 170,000 as of 2026* *(v)* — now one of Israel's larger
  cities, and the single most important destination for Anglo frum aliyah.
- *The neighborhoods are genuinely different places:*
  - *RBS Aleph* — Established, strong Anglo presence, mixed charedi and
    national-religious, the widest range of hashkafa.
  - *RBS Bet* — Predominantly Israeli charedi, including significant kanoi
    elements. Not the default Anglo landing spot.
  - *RBS Gimmel* — Newer, heavily Anglo yeshivish. Described by residents as
    "Lakewood transplanted to Israel," with streets named after gedolim and a de
    facto communal dress standard; families from Passaic, Monsey, or the Five
    Towns frequently report that it feels immediately familiar
    *(v, community reporting)*.
  - *RBS Daled / Hey / Vav* and further phases — under construction and
    expanding.
  - *Old Beit Shemesh* — Cheaper, more Israeli, more mixed.
- *Prices:* RBS Gimmel runs roughly *₪19,000–₪24,000 per square meter*; a recent
  3-bedroom listing at *₪2,390,000 for ~105 sqm* works out to about ₪22,762/sqm
  *(v, 2026)*. City-wide, a 4-room apartment recently sold at
  *₪2,415,000 (~$775,000)* and a 3-room at *₪2,140,000 (~$693,000)* *(v)*.
- *Trajectory:* Beit Shemesh prices rose *66.5% over seven years*, driven by
  charedi demand against previously low prices; Q2 2025 showed a slight decline
  of 0.42% *(v)*. Newer neighborhoods including Gimmel were projected to rise
  8–10% in 2025 *(v)*.
- *Anglo infrastructure:* Dozens of Anglo WhatsApp groups, women's groups,
  English shiurim, and chesed networks *(v)*. This is the practical reason RBS
  works for people who don't yet have Hebrew.

** 5.4 Mixed cities and the periphery

- *Ashdod* — Large charedi population, notably Ger and other chassidus, plus a
  large Sephardi charedi community and a substantial French population.
  *Average apartment ₪2.09 million (Q1 2026), 14th nationally; average rent ₪4,402/month*
  *(v)* — considerably cheaper than the Jerusalem corridor, with beach access.
- *Netanya* — Large Anglo and French communities, mixed religious and charedi.
  *Average apartment ₪2.87 million (Q1 2026), 6th nationally; average rent ₪4,729/month*
  *(v)*.
- *Petach Tikva, Rechovot, Rehovot-adjacent* — Established mixed cities with
  real charedi neighborhoods and better access to the Tel Aviv job market.
- *Haifa and Rechasim* — Rechasim is a small charedi town near Haifa with a
  strong yeshiva presence; Haifa itself is inexpensive and has a small but real
  frum community.
- *Tzfas* — Small, distinctive, heavily breslov and chassidish alongside a
  national-religious population and a well-known Anglo baal teshuva community.
  Cheap by Israeli standards. Limited parnassa.
- *Tveria* — Cheap, growing charedi population, limited economy.
- *Kiryat Gat / Carmei Gat* — *Carmei Gat* is a new Anglo-oriented neighborhood
  positioned on transit:
  *38 minutes by train to Tel Aviv, 35 minutes' drive to Modiin*, next to
  Highway 6 *(v, developer)*. New construction with 334 units in one recent
  project. OU band: *₪600–799K equivalent* with rentals ₪1,500–2,000 equivalent;
  tuition under $10K *(self-rep.)*. Worth watching, and worth remembering that
  developer marketing is marketing.
- *Netivot, Ofakim, Yerucham, Arad, Dimona* — Negev towns with growing charedi
  communities, often kollel-anchored, very cheap housing, very thin job markets.
- *Emanuel* — Charedi town in the Shomron, inexpensive, isolated.

** 5.5 Religious Zionist and Anglo communities

- *Gush Etzion* — Suburban communities
  *15–45 minutes from Yerushalayim, Beit Shemesh, and Beitar* *(v)*.
  *Five religious elementary schools*, special-education resources described as
  among the country's best, very active Bnei Akiva, and high schools experienced
  with olim *(v)*. Sub-communities differ sharply:
  *Neve Daniel, Elazar, Alon Shvut* are religious-Zionist suburban;
  *Tekoa and Nokdim* are mixed religious and secular with a desert view 15
  minutes from Yerushalayim; *Ma'ale Amos and Meitzad* are growing charedi
  yishuvim with large English-speaking populations, Ma'ale Amos guided by an
  English-speaking rav *(v)*. OU band: *₪400–599K equivalent*, rentals
  ₪800–1,500 equivalent, tuition under $10K *(self-rep.)*. Dedicated local
  aliyah staff.
- *Efrat* — The largest Anglo religious-Zionist town, full infrastructure,
  expensive by regional standards.
- *Modiin and Chashmonaim* — Modiin is a large modern city with a substantial
  Anglo religious population; Chashmonaim is a religious yishuv nearby, heavily
  Anglo. Both are commutable to Tel Aviv and Yerushalayim, and both are
  expensive.
- *Ma'ale Adumim, Ra'anana, Karnei Shomron, Ginot Shomron, Beit El, Kochav Yaakov, Tel Zion*
  — Each with its own character; Tel Zion and parts of Kochav Yaakov are
  charedi.
- *Deep Yehuda and Shomron* — Shilo, Eli, Ateret, Ma'aleh Levona, Itamar, Elon
  Moreh, Har Bracha, Otniel, Susya. The lowest cost per square meter in the
  country for detached homes with land, and the trade is distance,
  infrastructure, and a security and legal environment that is materially
  different from inside the Green Line.

** 5.6 The housing and mortgage reality

Several structural facts that surprise Americans.

*The state owns the land.* The *Israel Land Authority controls roughly 93%* of
the country's land *(v)*, releasing it through tenders. Supply is
administratively throttled rather than market-driven, which is the single
biggest structural difference from the US.

*Construction is concrete and stone*, partly for security — every apartment
requires a *mamad*, a reinforced room. Build times run to years, not months.

*Apartments are the normal family home.* A *4-room* apartment is roughly 90–110
m² and houses a family. Detached houses exist mainly in yishuvim and the
periphery.
*The national average apartment price was ₪2.33 million in Q1 2026 (v)*;
Jerusalem averages *₪36,000/m²*, median *₪33,000/m² (v)*.

*Mortgages work differently, and the difference is not cosmetic.* There is no
30-year fixed-rate mortgage of the American kind. An Israeli *mashkanta* is
assembled from several tracks at once, and as of 2026 *(v)*:

| Track                               | Rate     |
|-------------------------------------+----------|
| Fixed, non-indexed (/kalatz/)       | 5.3–6.1% |
| Fixed, CPI-indexed (/kevua tzmuda/) | 4.5–5.2% |
| Floating (/mishtana/)               | 4.8–5.5% |
| Prime                               | 5.25%    |

The Bank of Israel rate was *3.75% in June 2026* with inflation at 2.6%, and
further cuts toward 3.5% were expected by year-end *(v)*. Terms run to
*30 years*, and payments are *capped at 50% of net monthly income (v)*.

*The loan-to-value rules are the part olim should notice:*

| Buyer                     | Maximum LTV | Minimum down payment |
|---------------------------+-------------+----------------------|
| *New oleh*                | *90%*       | *10%*                |
| Israeli citizen           | 75%         | 25%                  |
| Non-resident / investment | 50%         | 50%                  |

*(v)* — that is a materially better borrowing position than a citizen gets, and
it is time-limited, so it belongs in the aliyah timing conversation.

*The CPI-indexed tracks are the trap.* On an indexed track the /principal/ rises
with the cost-of-living index, so the balance can grow even while you pay. Model
the downside before committing, not the headline payment.

*Mechir LaMishtaken*, the subsidized-housing lottery for eligible buyers, is a
major channel into new construction — and access to it is one of the benefits
currently entangled in the political situation in §5.8.

*Arnona is not American property tax.* Israel's municipal tax is charged
*per square meter of the apartment*, not as a share of its value, so it does not
scale with the market. Jerusalem runs *₪40.40–113.22 per m² per year*; Beit
Shemesh *₪41.26–47.48*, roughly *₪3,500–5,000/year on a 100 m² apartment (v)*.
Rates rose *1.626% nationally in 2026 (v)*. Against the $10,000–27,000 annual
bills in §6.1, arnona is close to a rounding error — and §6.2 covers the
discounts, including the rule change that specifically hurts avreichim.

*Prices moved with the war.* Israeli housing dipped, then rose for two
consecutive months after the ceasefire, then dipped again
*(v, Times of Israel housing snapshots, Feb–Mar 2026)*. Anyone buying should
look at the latest CBS index rather than any figure in this document.

** 5.7 Kollel in Eretz Yisroel — the economics

This is a completely different institution from an American community kollel.

*Scale.* At the start of 2024 there were roughly
*169,366 yeshiva and kollel students in Israel* *(v, IDI)*. Between 2013 and
2023 that number grew *83%* *(v)*. Learning full-time into adulthood is the
statistical norm in Israeli charedi society, not an elite track.

*The income stack.* A typical avreich's household income is assembled from
several small sources rather than one salary:

- *Government stipend* — roughly *₪800–900/month* from the Education Ministry,
  with an additional *₪1,000–2,000/month* for those with at least three
  children, no car, and a non-working wife, subject to conditions *(v)*.
- *Kollel stipend* from the institution itself, varying enormously by kollel.
- *Keren Lomdei Torah* — a fund providing
  *₪14,000/year in twelve monthly payments* to avreichim whose wives earn no
  more than ₪1,200/month *(v)*.
- *The wife's salary* — usually the largest single component. Charedi women's
  employment stands at *81%* *(v, 2025)*, versus *53%* for charedi men.
- *Family support*, from parents and in-laws, which in the Israeli system is
  often formalized at the time of the shidduch and can include the apartment
  itself.
- *Bituach Leumi* child allowances.

*The outcomes.*
*33% of charedi families live below the poverty line, yet 75% own an apartment*
— slightly /higher/ than the rest of Jewish society *(v, IDI)*. Both halves of
that sentence are true and they explain a great deal: the system produces low
cash income alongside high asset ownership, because apartments come through
family and subsidy channels rather than through earnings.
*47% of charedi children were below the poverty line after transfers (2022)*
*(v)*. Charedi men who leave learning for work earn about
*49% of non-charedi wages*, largely for lack of training *(v)*.

*For an Anglo.* Anglo kollelim exist throughout Yerushalayim (over 20 in Har Nof
alone with English representation), RBS, Telzstone, and elsewhere. Practical
differences from an American community kollel: there is usually no communal job
attached, the stipend is smaller, the cost of living is lower, and the standing
of a full-time learner is normative rather than exceptional. What you give up is
the American safety net — health insurance is via the kupot cholim rather than
an employer, and the American communal-kollel package (tuition remission,
housing help) generally does not exist in the same form.

** 5.8 The political situation — and why it is not background noise

*This is currently the single most important variable for anyone planning a charedi life in Israel, and it is unresolved.*

*The legal chain of events:*

- *June 2024* — the High Court ruled that with the relevant law and government
  decision expired, there was no longer any legal framework for charedi
  military-service exemptions, and that
  *state financial support for exempt students was also illegal* *(v)*.
- *April 2024 onward* — yeshivos and kollelim have been
  *denied funding in respect of draft-eligible students*: roughly
  *₪5,000/year per unmarried yeshiva bochur* and
  *₪10,000/year per married kollel student*, totaling about *₪650 million/year*
  *(v, IDI)*.
- *August 2024* — the Attorney General ordered daycare subsidies cut for
  children of yeshiva students who disobey draft orders *(v)*.
- *March 2025 onward* — kollel students subject to the draft have been
  *denied daycare subsidies*, worth roughly
  *₪26,400/year per family on average*, about *₪250 million/year* in total
  *(v, IDI)*.
- *Additional sanctions* — loss of access to
  *discounted housing deals (Mechir LaMishtaken)*, cancellation of after-school
  program subsidies, and cancellation of discounts on arnona (municipal tax) and
  public transportation *(v)*.
- *November 19, 2025* — the High Court required the government to formulate an
  effective enforcement plan; a contempt motion followed *(v)*.
- *December 2025* — the Bank of Israel published its assessment of the economic
  cost of non-enlistment *(v)*.
- *April 26, 2026* — the High Court ordered the government to enforce the draft,
  including
  *withholding state benefits and opening criminal proceedings against draft evaders*.
  Critically, it extended the daycare-subsidy denial to
  *all draft-subject yeshiva students, including those who work* — not only
  those learning full time *(v)*.
- *July 17, 2026* — the Knesset *dissolved without passing a draft law*. In its
  final week it instead passed a
  *Basic Law declaring Torah study a foundational state value*, and a
  *temporary law freezing enforcement against qualifying yeshiva students*
  *(v)*.
- *Also in 2026* — the Knesset Finance Committee advanced a UTJ-sponsored bill
  to *restore daycare subsidies* by barring the government from considering
  yeshiva-student status in eligibility decisions *(v)*.

*What this actually means, practically:*

1. *The subsidy stack in §5.7 is not stable.* Several of its components have
   already been cut for draft-eligible men, restored, re-cut, or frozen. A
   family budget built on the historical numbers is building on sand. Anyone
   modeling a move should model the *post-sanction* case, not the pre-sanction
   one, and treat any restoration as upside rather than as baseline.
2. *The legal exposure is real and now includes criminal proceedings*, not only
   financial pressure. For a young man of draft age, or a family with sons
   approaching it, this is a first-order consideration and it requires actual
   current advice from someone qualified — not a document like this one.
3. *The Basic Law and the enforcement freeze are recent and provisional.* A
   Basic Law is a strong statement of principle; a temporary enforcement freeze
   is a temporary enforcement freeze. Their durability depends entirely on the
   composition of the next government.
4. *The Knesset is dissolved and elections are ahead.* Every number in §5.7 is,
   in effect, a political variable pending that outcome. This is the least
   predictable moment in decades to model charedi household economics in Israel.
5. *Second-order effects.* Sanctions on subsidies push more charedi men toward
   the workforce, which is a large part of why the charedi tech track (§3.2) has
   grown. Whatever one's view of the underlying machlokes, the labor-market
   direction of travel is clear, and the training infrastructure — KamaTech,
   Bizmax, charedi vocational programs — is where the growth is.

*How to use this section.* Not as a prediction. As a list of things to check the
/current/ status of before making a decision, because every one of them has
changed at least once in the last twenty-four months.

** 5.9 Chinuch in Israel

The differences from America that catch olim off guard:

- *Registration is not automatic.* Charedi schools screen, and acceptance
  depends on the family's standing, the rav's recommendation, and the school's
  assessment of fit. A family that would be unremarkable in an American
  community can find itself negotiating for placement.
- *Anglo children need a landing plan.* Some schools and neighborhoods handle
  new olim well — RBS, Har Nof, parts of Gush Etzion which explicitly cites high
  schools experienced with olim — and some do not.
- *Tuition is dramatically lower* than the US. Most Israeli communities in the
  OU listing report *under $10K equivalent*, and much of the system is
  state-funded.
- *The tzibbur question is sharper.* In America, the range within a single
  school is often wide. In Israel, schools are more finely sorted by kehilla,
  chassidus, and hashkafa, and switching is harder.
- *Boys' and girls' tracks diverge earlier*, and the secular-studies content in
  charedi boys' schools is substantially thinner than in American yeshivos —
  which is directly connected to the wage gap in §5.7.

** 5.10 Aliyah practicalities

- *Nefesh B'Nefesh* handles most Anglo aliyah logistics and runs
  community-specific guidance, employment support, and post-aliyah programming.
- *Sal klita* — the absorption basket, paid over the first months, plus tax
  benefits, customs exemptions, and reduced arnona for a period.
- *Health insurance* via the kupot cholim (Clalit, Maccabi, Meuchedet, Leumit) —
  coverage from arrival, and structurally very different from and generally far
  cheaper than the American system.
- *Professional licensing* — medicine, law, therapy, and accounting all require
  conversion processes of varying difficulty. Software does not, which is a
  large part of why it is the standard Anglo profession in Israel.
- *Time zone* — Israel is 7 hours ahead of US Eastern. A US-employer remote job
  from Israel means afternoon-to-evening working hours, which collides with
  seder, with family time, and with the school day. This is the most common
  failure mode of "I'll just keep my American job," and it is worth planning
  around honestly before rather than after.

* Addendum — Beyond the US and Eretz Yisroel

Roughly 85% of the world's frum Jews live in the United States or Israel. The
rest — call it the third world of frum life — is not a rounding error: it
includes the largest yeshiva in Europe, one of the densest chassidish
communities anywhere, and several communities with better cost structures than
anything in the American Northeast. It also includes communities under real
demographic and political pressure.

This addendum is shallower than Parts 4 and 5, because the data is thinner and
less standardized. Treat it as orientation.

*One cross-cutting fact.* The Ichud HaKollelim directory reaches all of this:
kollelim in
*Toronto (4), Thornhill, Montreal (4), London, Borehamwood, Leeds, Antwerp, Amsterdam, Basel, Berlin, Milan, Rome, Saint-Louis (France), Gibraltar, Cape Town, Johannesburg, Melbourne, and Mexico City*.
There is also a separate *European Community-Kollel Network (ECKN)* operating in
the same space. Wherever there is a mid-sized frum community outside the US and
Israel, there is usually a kollel in it, and it is usually the community's Torah
engine.

** Canada

*TORONTO and THORNHILL, ON*
/The largest frum community in Canada, and the birthplace of the community-kollel model./

- *Geography:* Everything runs along *Bathurst Street*, and Torontonians locate
  themselves as "down south" or "up north" along it. The older communities sit
  in North York (Bathurst/Lawrence, Bathurst/Sheppard); the growth has moved
  steadily north into *Thornhill* and, most recently, *Thornhill Woods* — which
  grew *614% between 2001 and 2011* *(v)*. That northward migration is driven by
  exactly what drives it in America: newer houses, larger lots, lower prices.
- *Mosdos:* *Yeshivas Ner Yisroel of Toronto* (est. 1959, Bathurst Glen,
  Thornhill), *Netivot HaTorah Day School* (PreK–8, 600+ students, Thornhill)
  *(v)*, Eitz Chaim, Bnos Bais Yaakov, TanenbaumCHAT, Yeshivat Or Chaim /
  Ulpanat Orot, and a substantial chassidish school network.
- *Kollel:* *Kollel Ohr Yosef* (601 Clark Avenue West, Thornhill) serves
  Thornhill and northern Toronto *(v)*; Ichud also lists
  *Forest Hill Community Kollel, Kollel Avreichim of Toronto, Kollel Oholei Yom Tov, and Toronto Sephardic Kollel*.
  Toronto's historical significance is worth stating plainly:
  *the first community kollel in the world was established here in 1970* *(v)*,
  and the entire model in Part 2 descends from it.
- *Character:* Full spectrum — a large yeshivish community, a substantial
  chassidish population, a large Modern Orthodox community, a Sephardi community
  with its own kollel, and Chabad.
- *Housing:* Expensive by North American standards; the Greater Toronto market
  is among the costliest on the continent *(est.)*.
- *Parnassa:* Canada's financial and corporate capital. Note for Americans:
  cross-border work, tax, and immigration questions are non-trivial and require
  actual advice.

*MONTREAL, QC*
/Two communities that barely overlap, and the most dramatic demographic split in North America./

- *Geography:* *Côte Saint-Luc* holds the largest Jewish population in the metro
  at *18,180*; *Hampstead* has the highest density at *63.8% Jewish*, Côte
  Saint-Luc at *53.9%* *(v)*. These are the Modern Orthodox and traditional
  communities. Separately, *Outremont and Parc Extension* hold the chassidish
  concentration — Satmar, Belz, Skver, Vizhnitz, Klausenberg — plus
  *Tosh in Boisbriand*, a self-contained chassidish village of
  *over 2,400 people* *(v)*.
- *The demographic split is stark.* Between 2011 and 2021,
  *Outremont gained 1,560 Jews while Côte Saint-Luc lost 930 and Hampstead lost 890*
  *(v)*. The median age of Montreal's Jewish community overall is *39.6*; the
  median age in the Outremont Jewish community is *17.6* *(v)*. Charedim are now
  roughly *a fifth of Greater Montreal's Jewish population* *(v)*. One half of
  Montreal Jewry is aging and shrinking; the other is very young and growing
  fast.
- *Mosdos:* Hebrew Academy (Côte Saint-Luc, 521 students), Yeshiva Gedola Merkaz
  Hatorah, Beth Jacob, Skver girls' school in Outremont, Bais Yaakov, and a
  dense chassidish school network *(v)*.
- *Kollel:* Four in the Ichud directory — Kollel Kesser Torah (Montreal
  Community Kollel), Kollel Petach Tikvah, Kollel Pe'er HaTorah, and the
  *Sephardic Kollel Avrechim Foundation – Kehillas Chouva Yisrael*, reflecting
  Montreal's large Moroccan and Sephardi community.
- *Cost:* Housing and tuition are materially cheaper than Toronto or the
  American Northeast *(est.)* — the standing trade-off is Quebec's language
  politics and tax load.

*Smaller Canadian communities:* Ottawa, Vancouver, Winnipeg, Hamilton, Calgary,
Edmonton, and Halifax each have Orthodox shuls and, in several cases, day
schools. All are small; verify the K–12 stack before considering any of them.

** The United Kingdom

*The demographic story first, because it dominates everything.* Britain's
strictly Orthodox population is growing at roughly
*3.5% per year against 0.2% for the rest of British Jewry* *(v, JPR)*. Estimates
of current share vary — JPR has framed it as
*one in seven of all British Jews, rising to one in five by 2040*, while other
reporting cites *around 80,000 charedim, about one in four*, and projects
*40% by 2040*
*(v — the sources disagree, and the discrepancy is about definitions of "strictly Orthodox," not about the direction)*.
Strictly Orthodox births rose
*35% between 2007 and 2015 (1,431 to 1,932 per year)* *(v)*, on an estimated TFR
around *7 children per woman* *(v)*. On current trends,
*around half of Jewish children born in the UK by 2031 will be charedi* *(v)*.
Charedi growth has already reversed the overall decline of British Jewry.

*STAMFORD HILL, LONDON*
/The largest strictly Orthodox community in Europe./

- *Size:* Approximately *25,000 charedim*, about
  *10% of the Borough of Hackney*, growing at roughly *5% per annum* *(v)*.
- *Character:* Overwhelmingly chassidish — Satmar, Belz, Bobov, Vizhnitz, Ger,
  Skver, and many more — plus a Litvishe minority. Yiddish is widely spoken.
  Extremely dense, with old and expensive housing stock.
- *Mosdos:* Yesodey Hatorah, Beis Chinuch, Lubavitch, and a very large network
  of chadarim and yeshivos.
  *Upwards of 1,500 boys aged 13+ learn in yeshivos that are not registered as schools*
  and therefore fall outside Ofsted inspection *(v)*.
- *The live issue:* UK schools legislation targeting unregistered settings is an
  active and serious conflict. The head of Gateshead Yeshiva has publicly told
  the strictly Orthodox community to *leave the UK if the Schools Bill passes*
  *(v)*. Anyone considering a move to a British charedi community should treat
  the regulatory status of its yeshivos as a first-order question, not a
  background detail.

*GOLDERS GREEN, HENDON, EDGWARE, BOREHAMWOOD, LONDON*
/The Litvishe and Modern Orthodox side of London./

- *Character:* Golders Green and Hendon are the established centres — a mix of
  yeshivish, Modern Orthodox, and a growing chassidish presence. Edgware and
  Borehamwood are the suburban expansion.
- *Mosdos:* Hasmonean, Menorah, Beis Yaakov, Immanuel College, plus the yeshivos
  and seminaries.
- *Kollel:* Ichud lists *The London Chaburah* and the
  *Borehamwood Kollel (Kollel Toras Chanoch)*.
- *Housing:* Flats in Golders Green Central run roughly *£450,000–£700,000*
  depending on size and condition; borough-level annual house price growth hit
  *4.6% to early 2026* *(v)*. Houses cost considerably more. London is expensive
  and the frum streets are the expensive part of it.

*MANCHESTER — Broughton Park, Prestwich, Salford, Whitefield*
/The second-largest UK frum community, and cheaper than London./

- *Character:* *Broughton Park is predominantly Litvishe charedi* *(v)*, with a
  large chassidish presence alongside; Prestwich and Whitefield are more mixed.
  A very substantial and self-sustaining community.
- *Mosdos:* Manchester Yeshiva, Manchester Mesivta, Beis Yaakov Manchester,
  Broughton Jewish Cassel Fox, King David, and many more.
- *Cost:* Materially cheaper than London on housing, which is the main reason
  for its growth *(est.)*.

*GATESHEAD*
/A yeshiva town, in the fullest sense of the term./

- *Gateshead Talmudical College is the largest yeshiva in Europe* and among the
  most prestigious advanced yeshivos in the Orthodox world *(v)*. The Gateshead
  Seminary (the "Sem") is equally significant on the girls' side.
- *The community itself is small — roughly 350–400 families* *(v)*. Almost
  everything about the town is downstream of the mosdos.
- *Cost:* Housing in the North East of England is among the cheapest in Britain
  *(est.)*. Parnassa is the constraint.

*Also:* *Leeds* (Ichud lists the Leeds Community Kollel), *Liverpool*,
*Birmingham*, and *Gibraltar* — a small, ancient, and complete community with
its own kollel in the Ichud directory.

** Continental Europe

*ANTWERP, BELGIUM*
/"The Jerusalem of the North." The densest chassidish community in continental Europe./

- *Size:* A Jewish community of roughly *20,000–25,000*, of whom about
  *15,000 are charedi, many of them chassidish* *(v)*.
- *Infrastructure:*
  *30 of Belgium's 45 active synagogues are Orthodox and in Antwerp* *(v)*;
  directories list around 20 shuls, 13 kosher restaurants, 4 kosher stores, 4
  caterers, and 3 mikvaos *(v)*.
- *Character:* Historically built around the diamond trade, which has contracted
  significantly — a real economic question for the community's future. Belz,
  Satmar, Ger, Vizhnitz and Machzikei Hadass all present. Belgium's Jewish
  schools are concentrated here and in Brussels.
- *Kollel:* *Kollel Antwerp HaChodosh* (Ichud).

*AMSTERDAM, NETHERLANDS*
- Roughly *15,000 Jews* *(v)*. The *NIHS*, an Orthodox Ashkenazi kehilla dating
  to 1635 with about *2,000 members*, supports the full infrastructure —
  kashrus, shuls, mikveh, rabbanus, social services, cemeteries, and school
  subsidies *(v)*. *Buitenveldert* holds an Orthodox shul, a Jewish elderly
  home, and three Jewish schools *(v)*.
- *Kollel:* Kollel Rabonim Rabbeinu Tzvi Ashkenazi (Ichud).

*SWITZERLAND — Zurich, Basel, Geneva*
- *Zurich ~6,800 Jews, Geneva ~4,400, Basel ~2,600* *(v)*. Zurich holds three
  distinct communities each around its own shul: the Orthodox
  *Israelitische Religionsgesellschaft (IRG)*, the Reform Or Chadasch, and a
  sizable chassidish community around *Agudas Achim* *(v)*. A school, library,
  mikveh, and two kosher stores *(v)*.
- *Kollel:* *Kollel Basel* (Ichud).
- *Note:* Very high cost of living, very high salaries, and a small but complete
  frum infrastructure.

*VIENNA, AUSTRIA*
- The *IKG* has nearly *8,000 members*; roughly *12,000 Jews* live in Vienna
  *(v)*. *Four Jewish schools*, kosher restaurants, and full availability of
  what a frum life requires *(v)*.
  *The charedi community operates independently with a separate school system and institutions*
  *(v)* — largely Bucharian, Georgian, and chassidish.

*BERLIN, MILAN, ROME*
- Small but real Orthodox communities, each with an Ichud kollel —
  *The Berlin Kollel*, *Kollel Milano – Mesilat Yesharim*, and *Kollel Roma*.
  Milan and Rome both have long-established Italian and Sephardi kehillos with
  their own minhagim.

*FRANCE*
- *Europe's largest Jewish community at roughly 450,000* (estimates range
  453,000–600,000) *(v)*.
  *Modern Orthodoxy is the predominant organized religious stream*; roughly
  *7% are charedi* *(v)*. Heavily Sephardi, largely North African in origin.
- *Paris* has *more than 20 Jewish day schools* plus kindergartens and
  seminaries; there are also Jewish schools in
  *Strasbourg (a community of 16,000), Nice, Toulouse, Marseille, Bordeaux, Metz, and Aix-les-Bains*
  *(v)* — the last of which is a yeshiva town in the Gateshead mould.
- *Kollel:* *Kollel Saint-Louis* (Alsace) in the Ichud directory.
- *The trajectory matters.*
  *3,300 French Jews made aliyah in 2025, a 45% increase over 2024*, and surveys
  suggest
  *38% of French Jews — roughly 200,000 people — are actively considering leaving*
  *(v)*. Israel's Aliyah Ministry closed 2025 with 21,900 olim overall, with
  growth specifically from France and the UK *(v)*. Any assessment of a French
  community has to account for the possibility that a meaningful share of it
  will not be there in a decade.

** Australia

*MELBOURNE*
/The largest and most institutionally complete frum community in the southern hemisphere./

- *Character:* Centred on *East St Kilda, Caulfield, and Ripponlea*. Chabad is
  the dominant organized presence — the *Yeshivah Centre* operates
  *Yeshivah College* (boys, est. 1955, ~420 students),
  *Beth Rivkah Ladies College* (est. 1956), *Kollel Menachem Lubavitch*, and
  *Ohel Chana* seminary *(v)*. Alongside it sits *Adass Israel*, a substantial
  and separate chassidish/charedi kehilla with its own mosdos *(est.)*, plus
  Mizrachi, Yeshivah Gedolah, and a Sephardi community.
- *Kollel:* *Kollel Beth HaTalmud – Yehuda Fishman Institute* (Ichud), a serious
  Litvishe beis medrash.
- *Considerations:* Extremely far from everywhere else — flights to Israel, New
  York, or London are long and expensive, which matters for shidduchim, for
  family, and for sending children out to yeshiva or seminary. Housing in
  Melbourne's Jewish suburbs is expensive.

*SYDNEY*
- Centred on *Bondi* and the eastern suburbs. The *Yeshiva Centre* (Chabad, est.
  1956, Flood Street, Bondi) and *Yeshiva College Bondi* *(v)*, plus Moriah
  College, Kesser Torah, and the *Sydney Community Kollel* *(v)*. Smaller and
  less charedi than Melbourne.

** South Africa

- *Total Jewish population roughly 52,300* — *Johannesburg ~30,000 (57.5%)* and
  *Cape Town ~12,500 (23.9%)* *(v)*.
- *Johannesburg* is one of the most religiously observant Jewish communities in
  the diaspora by percentage. *Yeshiva College*, founded in the mid-1950s, was
  South Africa's first and is now its largest Modern Orthodox day school;
  several more religious schools followed in the 1970s and 1980s, and by the
  2020s Johannesburg had
  *multiple yeshivos and kollelim across the charedi spectrum* *(v)*.
  *Kollel Yad Shaul*, Ohr Somayach, Aish, and Chabad have all been major forces
  in the community's shift toward observance since the 1970s *(v)*.
- *Kollel:* *Kollel Toras Chaim* (Johannesburg) and the *Cape Town Kollel* (both
  Ichud).
- *Cape Town's Orthodox community is contracting, and not primarily through emigration.*

** How to read this addendum

Three patterns run through all of it:

1. *The charedi share is rising everywhere, and fast.* Britain at 3.5% annual
   growth against 0.2%, Montreal's Outremont at a median age of 17.6, Stamford
   Hill at 5% a year. Communities that look stable in aggregate are usually two
   communities — one shrinking and one growing — and the aggregate hides it.
2. *Kollel is the transplant mechanism.* Almost every one of these communities
   has a kollel, and in most of them the kollel is the reason there is still
   Torah infrastructure. The Ichud/BMG model that began in Toronto in 1970 has
   been exported to five continents.
3. *The specific risks are different from America's, and they are not financial.*
   In Britain it is school regulation. In France it is security and a population
   that is actively considering leaving. In South Africa it is emigration and
   infrastructure. In Latin America it is security and currency. In Australia it
   is distance. None of these show up in a housing comparison, and all of them
   matter more than one.

* Part 6 — Comparative tables

** 6.1 US communities — housing by segment, plus the taxes

*Why each community lists a different number of prices.* A community's housing
stock is not one market.
*Detached single-family homes are only 34.46% of Lakewood's units (v)* — the
rest is townhouses, duplexes and 3,500-plus units of 55-plus senior housing, so
any "Lakewood median" is mostly measuring that two-thirds. Meanwhile
*Passaic Park, the frum neighborhood, runs a median real estate price of $856,367 against Passaic city at $543–555K (v)*.
One number per community cannot describe either place, and forcing every
community into the same number of tiers is the same mistake in a different
shape. So each row below lists *only the segments that genuinely exist there*.
Lakewood needs five. Brookline and South Bend need one.
/The count is itself information./

*Labels:* *Entry* = cheapest realistic way in · *Attached* =
townhouse/duplex/co-op · *All-types*
= a blended figure, flagged because it understates houses · *Family* = detached
4–5BR on the frum blocks, *the number that matters* · *New*
= new construction · *Upper* = top of the local market.

*Confidence:* *(v)* verified against a named market source, reading date given
· *(comp.)* computed from a verified rate in the same row · *(est.)* inferred
from local $/ft² · *(s-r)* self-reported — the community's own recruitment copy,
undated and not independently checked.

*The (s-r) tier is wrong in a known direction, which is the useful part.* Asked
what homes cost, a community answers with the cheapest realistic way in, because
that is the answer that attracts families; the figure then lands in a *Family*
column that asked a different question. It also carries no date, where every
*(v)* reading here does, so it ages downward in a market that has risen.
*Twelve (s-r) prices have now been checked against the market: five were materially low, five held, two were bands too wide to be wrong — and not one came back high.*
The failures were the point estimates and the narrow bands — Northeast
Philadelphia's $350,000 against $389–444K, Omaha's $155,400 against $289–307K,
Nashville's $800K–1M against a $1.1M ZIP median, Plano's $400–500K against the
verified $/ft² printed beside it. The ones that held were bands wide enough to
span most of their own level: South Bend's $175–350K has a top double its
bottom, and a band that wide contains the answer without asserting anything —
while the reader anchors on the bottom, which is the number the community chose
to lead with. San Antonio's $200–399K illustrates both failures at once, since
its top turns out to be the market median exactly.
*So read an (s-r) point estimate as a floor, and an (s-r) band as its top.*
Earlier drafts wrote these cells as /(v, self-rep.)/, which asserted
verification of a source Appendix B disqualifies; see
[[*Known weaknesses in this document][weakness 15]].
*If you know a market directly, your number beats mine.*

*Read the income tax column carefully.* It shows the *top marginal rate*, which
applies to the last dollar of a very high income — New Jersey's 10.75% starts
above $1 million. *At $150,000 the ranking is different and sometimes reversed*:
Indiana costs such a family ~$7,050 and New Jersey ~$5,500, because Indiana adds
a county tax and New Jersey's graduated schedule never reaches its top band.
*§3.6 has the effective rates at real frum-family incomes; use that table for anything below roughly $300,000.*
Property tax, by contrast, is directly comparable — it is charged on the asset
regardless of income.

*** What the segmented view shows that one number hid

- *Lakewood is expensive, and the published medians conceal it.* With only
  34.46% of the stock detached, every township figure is a statement about
  townhouses and senior housing. The family house is *$750–950K*, and new
  5-bedroom construction is *$1.1–1.2M (v)*.
- *The frum neighborhood is almost always dearer than its town.* Passaic Park
  *$856,367* vs Passaic city *$543–555K*. Bexley *$556K* vs Columbus *$304K*.
  Memphis 38120 *$442K* vs the citywide $117/ft². Never take a city-wide figure
  as a proxy for the frum blocks.
- *The number of segments is itself information.* Baltimore's Cheswolde runs
  from a $270,000 median to $1.35M — a genuinely wide market you can enter
  cheaply and trade up inside. Brookline has one number. South Bend has one
  number. Those are three very different propositions and no uniform table can
  say so.
- *Cheap entry with a punishing rate is the trap.* Cleveland Heights: *$120/ft²*
  and *3.00%*. Rochester: *$230,000* and *2.43%*.
- *Some of the cheap communities are cheap because nobody measured them.* Brooklyn,
  Monsey, Los Angeles and Brookline were priced off dated market medians with
  sale counts and a $/ft² cross-check. Omaha, Bensalem, Northeast Philadelphia,
  South Bend and Norfolk were priced off the community's own webpage. So
  *measurement quality correlates with price*, and that manufactures part of the
  very spread this table exists to show. Twelve of those cells have since been
  checked: *five were materially low* — Omaha by *97%*, Northeast Philadelphia's
  family segment by *43–57%* — *five held, two were bands too wide to be wrong,
  and none came back high.* The in-town versus out-of-town gap is real and
  large. It is *narrower* than an unaudited table made it look, and the
  correction runs one way.
- *The tax columns are the honest comparison.* Denver at 0.44% and Maricopa at
  0.47% against Cleveland Heights at 3.00% and Bergenfield at 3.41% is a real,
  statutory, seven-fold spread — and unlike the price columns, it is not an
  artifact of which properties happened to sell that quarter.

*Arnona in one paragraph.* Israel's municipal tax is charged
*per square meter of the apartment*, not as a percentage of value — so it does
not scale with the market the way American property tax does, and it is far
cheaper. Jerusalem runs *₪40.40 to ₪113.22 per m² per year*; Beit Shemesh
*₪41.26 (older neighborhoods) to ₪47.48 (newer)*, which is roughly
*₪3,500–5,000/year on a 100 m² apartment (v)*. Rates rose
*1.626% nationally in 2026 (v)*. Compare that to $10,000–27,000 a year in the
American communities in §6.1:
*arnona is close to a rounding error by US standards.*

*Two discount points that matter for a kollel family.* New olim receive a
*90% arnona discount for 12 months (v)*, and Jerusalem, Beit Shemesh and Modiin
Illit run local top-up programs reaching *90–95% for large families (v)*. Kollel
students can apply on income grounds — but the rules changed: eligibility is now
assessed on
*average income over the past year rather than three months of payslips*, which
specifically disadvantages avreichim whose income is irregular *(v)*. That is a
small, concrete instance of the broader squeeze described in §5.8.

* Part 7 — How to actually evaluate a community

Marketing materials answer the questions that are easy to answer well. Here are
the ones that decide whether a move holds.

** Before the visit

1. *The K–12 question.* Not "is there a school" —
   /is there a school for each of my children, in each of the next fifteen years, that I would actually choose?/
   Get the enrollment numbers per grade, not the total. A school with 200
   students across PreK–8 has about 22 per grade, which means one class, which
   means no fit alternative if it goes badly.
2. *The high school question, specifically.* If there is no local mesivta or
   Bais Yaakov, where do people send? Is dorming normal here, and at what age?
   What does that cost?
3. *The tuition question, in dollars.* Ask the school, not the community: what
   does a family with my income and my number of children actually pay, after
   scholarship, after voucher or ESA? Get a number.
4. *The parnassa question, for both of you.* Named employers, not sectors. If
   remote: who else here works remotely, and can I speak to them?
5. *The exit question.* If this doesn't work out in three years, what does
   leaving cost? A house in a small community is far less liquid than a house in
   Lakewood.

** On the pilot Shabbos

6. *Go to a weekday Maariv, not just the Shabbos davening.* Shabbos always looks
   good. Tuesday night tells you what the community actually is.
7. *Count the young families in shul.* Then ask how many arrived in the last
   three years and how many left. Communities always report the first number and
   rarely the second.
8. *Ask who is carrying the load.* In a small community, a handful of people run
   everything. Find out who, how old they are, and what happens when they stop.
9. *Ask about the machlokes.* Every community has one. A community that claims
   otherwise is either hiding it or hasn't noticed it. What you want to know is
   whether it's the kind you can live around.
10. *Ask the women.* Separately, and about their own lives — childcare, work,
    isolation, whether they have friends here or just neighbors. This is the
    variable that most often determines whether a family stays, and it is the
    least represented in any relocation material.
11. *Check the shidduch reality.* For a community under a few hundred families:
    where do the children go to date, who pays for it, and does the community
    have a functioning network or just good intentions?

** For a kollel position specifically

12. *Get the whole package in writing.* Stipend, tuition remission, housing,
    health insurance, moving costs, term length, and who your employer actually
    is.
13. *Talk to the yungerman who left.* Not the one who stayed. The kollel will
    introduce you to the latter; find the former yourself.
14. *Ask what the community expects of you* beyond learning — how many
    chavrusas, how many shiurim, whether you're expected to teach in the school,
    and what happens if you say no.
15. *Ask what the exit looks like.* Do yungerleit here transition into local
    jobs, or do they leave town? If they leave, the community is a waystation,
    and that's fine — as long as you know it going in.

** For Israel specifically

16. *Verify the current status of every subsidy in §5.7 and every sanction in §5.8*
    before you model anything. All of it has moved in the past two years, and
    the Knesset is dissolved.
17. *Get real advice on draft status* for yourself and for sons approaching
    draft age, from someone qualified and current. Not from a document, and not
    from a forum.
18. *Confirm school placement before you confirm the apartment.* In that order,
    and in writing where possible.
19. *Model the mortgage under inflation and rate stress*, not at today's
    payment.
20. *If you plan to keep an American job*, sit down and actually write out a
    weekday: what time you start, what time you finish, where seder goes, when
    you see your children. Then decide.

* Appendix B — Sources, and what to distrust

** Primary structured sources

- *Ichud HaKollelim* (ichudhakollelim.org) — kollel directory, organizational
  statistics, and placement services. Directory extracted August 2026.
- *OU Communities / Savitsky Communal Growth Initiative* (communities.ou.org) —
  approximately 80 community profiles covering shuls, mikvaos, schools, kollel
  network affiliation, tuition bands, housing bands, tax treatment, and retiree
  and special-needs amenities. Extracted August 2026.
- *US Census Bureau* — population and growth figures for Lakewood, Kiryas Joel,
  Monsey, and New Square.
- *Israel Democracy Institute* — charedi society statistical reports
  (employment, poverty, yeshiva/kollel enrollment) and legal analysis of the
  conscription rulings and sanctions.
- *Israel Central Bureau of Statistics*, via Times of Israel and Ynet housing
  coverage — Israeli price and rent indices.
- *Zillow, Redfin, PropertyShark* — US metro and neighborhood housing medians,
  2026 readings.
- *Institute for Jewish Policy Research (JPR)* — British strictly Orthodox
  population, growth rates, and projections.
- *Federation CJA / Canadian census analysis* — Montreal Jewish demographics by
  borough, 2011–2021.
- *World Jewish Congress, European Jewish Congress, Jewish Virtual Library* —
  community size figures for Europe, South Africa, Australia, and Latin America.
- *SA Jewish Report* — Cape Town marriage and birth-rate data.
- *Israel Aliyah and Integration Ministry, via JPost* — 2025 aliyah figures by
  country of origin.
- *Redfin, Zillow, Movoto, PropertyShark, Homes.com* — 2026 median sale price
  per square foot by neighborhood; the anchor of §6.1.
- *Ownwell, njtaxcalculator, county assessors, Tax Foundation reporting* —
  effective property tax rates and median annual bills by municipality and
  county.
- *Tax Foundation, Visual Capitalist, state revenue departments* — 2026 state
  income tax rates, flat-tax conversions, and no-income-tax states.

** Known weaknesses in this document

1. *OU profiles are self-authored recruitment copy.* Every community in that
   dataset is trying to attract families. Amenity lists are accurate (they're
   checkboxes); the narratives are optimistic; the housing and tuition bands are
   what the community believes is achievable, not the average outcome.
2. *The Ichud directory undercounts.* It lists 112 against a reported 125
   members, and it covers only one network.
3. *Neither dataset covers Lakewood, Brooklyn, Kiryas Joel, or the chassidishe world properly*,
   because those communities have no reason to appear in relocation directories.
   Those sections here are correspondingly thinner and lean more on general
   reporting.
4. *The housing figures were rebuilt three times, and the final structure is the admission.*
   Version one quoted the OU directory's /"Affordable Homes/Co-ops"/ bands — the
   entry rung — in a column headed "Homes." Version two replaced them with
   neighborhood-wide price-per-square-foot, still too low, because such figures
   blend condos, co-ops and two-to-four-family stock in with houses. Version
   three used single-family figures where they existed but
   *mixed units of analysis* — comparing a township that contains retirement
   villages (Lakewood) against a frum neighborhood (Passaic Park) against an
   affluent sub-neighborhood that is not where the local frum community lives
   (Squirrel Hill North) — and produced the absurd result that Lakewood was
   cheaper than both.
   *The underlying problem is that no single number can describe a community's housing, because the stock is not one market*:
   detached single-family homes are only *34.46%* of Lakewood's units. §6.1
   therefore now lists *however many segments genuinely exist in each community*
   — entry, attached, family house, new, upper — each labelled with what it
   measures. Lakewood needs five; Brookline and South Bend need one. The
   family-house column is the one that matters and the one public aggregators
   are worst at isolating; several of its cells are marked (est.) and are the
   weakest figures in this document. Anyone with direct knowledge of a local
   market should trust their own number over mine.
5. *An earlier draft inferred house sizes by dividing a median price by a median $/ft². That is invalid*
   — they are medians of two different, differently-contaminated distributions,
   and the quotient means nothing. It produced the claim that Monsey's typical
   house is about 1,720 ft²; actual Monsey listings run *4,700–5,000 ft²*, and
   the published $580/ft² is the attached/condo rate, not the house rate
   (*houses are nearer $215/ft²*). All derived square footages have been
   removed; where a size appears now, a source stated it.
6. *An earlier draft multiplied a per-child tuition band by five children.* That
   is not how schools price. Sibling discounts are standard, and many schools
   cap total family tuition at
   *12–18% of AGI regardless of how many children attend (v)*. The error
   inflated the expensive communities specifically — because that is where caps
   and scholarship do the most work — and it drove a headline claim of an
   "$85,000 a year" gap that was roughly three times too large at a $150,000
   income. §3.5 and §3.6 are rebuilt; the corrected finding is that the gap is
   *modest at kollel-family incomes and large at professional ones*, because
   voucher states do not means-test and scholarship states do.
7. *An earlier draft used top marginal income tax rates to describe families earning $150,000.*
   New Jersey's 10.75% applies above $1 million. At $150,000 the effective
   ranking is different and partly reversed:
   *Indiana ~$7,050 (state plus county), New Jersey ~$5,500, Ohio ~$3,400*. The
   top-rate column survives in §6.1 because it is correct for high earners, but
   §3.6 now carries effective rates at real frum-family incomes and §6.1 points
   to it.
8. *US housing medians are metro- or neighborhood-level, not frum-street-level.*
   Frum blocks generally price above their surrounding median. Some
   year-over-year swings quoted (West Rogers Park at +50%, Amberley Village
   swinging between $435K and $802K within 2026, Pico-Robertson at −35%) reflect
   thin transaction volume rather than real market moves, and are flagged in
   place.
9. *Property tax figures mix verified bills with computed estimates.* Where a
   rate is marked (v) it is sourced; where a dollar figure is marked (comp.) it
   is that rate applied to the home price in the same row; where marked (est.)
   the rate itself is my estimate. Several commercial aggregators compute
   "effective rate" against assessed rather than market value and produce
   impossible numbers — one lists Edison, NJ at 5.92% — so verified
   *dollar bills* are preferred over rates throughout.
10. *The 2,800 ft² reference house is a convention, not a claim.* Where it
    appears, it means "a house of that size, at this community's verified rate,
    would cost X" — nothing more. It is /not/ an assertion that local houses are
    that size, and in several communities they plainly are not.
11. *Kollel stipend figures are the weakest numbers in this document.* No
    authoritative survey exists. Treat the ranges in §2.4 as orientation, not as
    data.
12. *Israeli policy figures have a short shelf life.* Everything in §5.7 and
    §5.8 has changed at least once in the past two years, the Knesset dissolved
    in July 2026, and elections are ahead. Re-verify before relying on any of
    it.
13. *The Addendum is thinner than Parts 4 and 5, and knowingly so.* Diaspora
    community statistics outside the US and Israel are collected irregularly, by
    different bodies, on different definitions. Where UK sources disagree on the
    charedi share of British Jewry (one in seven versus one in four), that
    disagreement is reported rather than resolved, because it turns on
    definitions this document is not equipped to adjudicate.
14. *Population figures for frum communities specifically are almost never measured.*
    Municipal census data captures the whole town; Jewish federation studies
    capture the whole Jewish community. Where a frum-specific number appears
    here, it is an estimate unless marked otherwise.
15. *The self-reported housing figures were never held to the standard the rest of §6.1 was, and they are either low or unfalsifiable.*
    Weakness 4 describes the fix: stop printing the entry rung in a column headed
    /Family/. That fix was applied wherever a market source was pulled to replace
    the community's own number — and *only* there. Where no market source was
    pulled, the version-one figure stayed in place and inherited the
    version-three label, carrying the composite marker /(v, self-rep.)/, which
    asserts verification of exactly the source weakness 1 disqualifies. The
    problem was never the method. It was that the method was applied to a
    convenience sample.
    *The bias is one-directional, and four things push the same way.* A community
    asked what homes cost answers with the cheapest realistic way in, because
    that is what attracts families. The answer is undated, in a market that has
    risen. It carries no $/ft², and $/ft² is this document's contamination
    detector everywhere else — it is how Monsey's condo rate and Southfield's
    small houses were caught, and it is absent from precisely the rows that
    needed it. And where the price is wrong the property tax cell, computed from
    it, is wrong by the same factor while being marked as though independently
    sourced.
    *All twelve have now been checked, and the result corrects a claim an earlier version of this appendix made.*
    /Materially low (five):/ Northeast Philadelphia
    *$350,000 → $389–444K all-types, $500–550K detached*; Bensalem
    *"from $350,000" → $409,777–450,000*; Omaha *$155,400 → $289,000–306,750*, a

    Indianapolis *(ZIP 46260 at $296–326K)*, South Bend *(city $185–190K)*,

    combined Jacksonville/Orlando/Tampa *$200–599K*, which spanned three metros
    and every segment at once.

    had. Of twelve, five did, five held, and two were unfalsifiable.
    The direction never reversed — *nothing came back high* — and the pattern
    in the failures is sharper than the original claim was. The cells that broke

** What this document deliberately does not do

It does not rank communities, recommend one, or argue about how the housing
market ought to work. A previous version of this material included proposals for
restructuring the Israeli land and construction system; those have been removed
as out of scope. This is a description of what exists.

* Appendix C — Corrections to the original /Orthodox Real Estate Matrix/

This primer replaces an earlier document,
/The Orthodox Real Estate Matrix: Comparing the US and Israeli Markets/. Its
factual claims were checked; most of the descriptive material held up. The
following did not, and is corrected or dropped here.
/(Corrections to this document's own earlier drafts are in Appendix B, not here.)/

*1. The Israeli price conversion is arithmetically wrong.*
The earlier draft stated a national baseline of "roughly
*$770 per sq. ft. (₪24,000 per sq. meter)*." Those two figures are not the same
number. ₪24,000/m² ÷ 10.764 ft²/m² = *₪2,230 per sq. ft.*, which at recent
exchange rates of roughly 3.6–3.7
₪/$ is about *$600–620 per sq. ft.* The stated $770 implies an exchange rate near 2.9 ₪/$,
which has not been the rate. *The dollar figure overstates by roughly 25%*, and
every US-vs-Israel comparison built on it inherits the error. The ₪24,000/m²
side of it is a reasonable national ballpark; the dollar side is not.

*2. "Willowbrook is the most affordable established Modern Orthodox enclave within the five boroughs" — no.*
Staten Island's 2026 median is *$755K* *(v)*; Kew Gardens Hills' average home
value is *$467,848* *(v, Zillow 2026)*. Kew Gardens Hills is substantially
cheaper and is an established community by any measure.

*3. The out-of-town price-per-square-foot figures are stale.*
The draft gave Cleveland at $115–160/ft² and Detroit at $115–155/ft². Cleveland
Heights' median is now *$223K, up 21.6% year-over-year*, and Oak Park, MI is
*$266K, up 26.1%* *(v, 2026)*. On typical house sizes those medians land above
the quoted bands. The Midwest is still cheap; it is not as cheap as those
figures suggest, and it moved sharply in the last year.

*4. The ILA figure is correct.*
The Israel Land Authority does control roughly *93%* of Israel's land *(v)*.
That part stands, and it is retained in §5.6.

*5. Linden, NJ was over-weighted.*
It appears in the draft as one of three named "high-density budget commuter
hubs." It is a real but small community; Passaic, Elizabeth, Hillside, Bayonne,
and Highland Park/Edison are all larger and more institutionally complete, and
are covered here instead.

*6. The framing treated "Orthodox" as meaning "Modern Orthodox."*
The draft's US section was organized around Modern Orthodox strongholds — Five
Towns, Surfside, Pico-Robertson — with yeshivish and chassidish communities
appearing only as parenthetical notes ("while sections are strictly Yeshivish or
Chassidic..."). Lakewood, Brooklyn, Monsey, Kiryas Joel, Baltimore, and Passaic
— collectively the large majority of American frum Jews — were absent or
marginal. That framing is not used here.

*7. Parts 3 and 4 of the draft are dropped as out of scope.*
Those sections proposed mechanisms to bring Israeli housing prices down toward
American levels: breaking the ILA land monopoly, subsidizing modular
construction, high-speed rail decentralization, corporate build-to-rent, and
applying sovereignty in Yehuda and Shomron. Whatever their merits, they are
policy argument rather than community reference. The underlying factual
observations that are useful to a family — the ILA's control of land supply, the
concrete-and-stone construction requirement and its effect on build times, and
the mortgage structure — are retained in §5.6 without the accompanying
proposals.

*8. The draft's deep-periphery price range is retained qualitatively but not numerically.*
Its figure of $175–240/ft² (₪6,800–9,000/m²) for Itamar, Elon Moreh, Har Bracha,
Shilo, Eli, Otniel and Susya could not be independently verified for 2026. The
structural point — that the deep periphery is the cheapest detached housing in
the country, and that the discount reflects distance, infrastructure, and legal
and security conditions — is retained in §5.5 without the specific numbers.
