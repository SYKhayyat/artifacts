* Start here

*What GIMP is.* A free, open-source pixel editor — the closest free equivalent
to Photoshop. Layers, masks, selections, brushes, compositing, text, healing,
content-aware fill. If a job requires manipulating individual pixels or stacking
separate images, GIMP is the free tool that can do it.

*What GIMP is not.* A raw editor. No catalogue, no raw develop module worth
using, no batch library management, no link back to your original negative.
Exposure, contrast, colour, white balance, dust spots, cropping — none of that
belongs here; it belongs in darktable (or Lightroom Classic, which does the same
job with more money involved). The moment an image enters GIMP, everything is
flattened into pixels and editing becomes destructive-by-default. GIMP 3.2's
non-destructive features soften that considerably, but the principle holds:
*raw work happens upstream.*

*How often you'll open it.* Once or twice a month, if that — when your raw
editor hits a wall (section 2 lists exactly those walls). That frequency is
correct, not a failure. This guide is mostly about recognising those moments
and executing precisely once inside.

*Time to set up: 15 minutes*, including the two plugins that make the whole
installation worth having.

* 1. Concepts you need first

Seven ideas carry the entire program. Each takes a minute; together they make
every later section obvious.

** Pixels and resolution

An image is a grid of coloured squares. "Resolution" talk confuses two
independent things:

- *Pixel dimensions*: how many pixels across × down (6000 × 4000).
- *PPI setting*: a number stored in metadata claiming how densely those pixels
  should print. It changes nothing on screen and nothing about the pixels
  themselves.

For screens, only pixel dimensions matter. For print, dimensions ÷ PPI = size
(6000 px at 300 ppi prints 20 inches wide). Anyone who says "web images are 72
dpi" has confused a default with a law.

** Bit depth

8 bits per channel = 256 levels each; enough for viewing, marginal for editing.
16 bits per channel = 65,536 levels; edits land smoothly instead of banding.
GIMP also offers *32-bit floating point* internal precision — headroom above
white and below black so aggressive edits don't clip irreversibly.

Rule: bring files in at 16-bit (export from darktable accordingly), leave
~Image → Precision~ at its default high setting, and never let an 8-bit file
near a curve.

** Colour spaces

sRGB: the universal space every screen and browser expects. Adobe RGB: wider
gamut some print workflows want. A file tagged with one space shown as if it
were another looks wrong (usually flat or oversaturated) — this is what colour
management prevents. GIMP reads embedded profiles and converts on request;
section 4 sets the defaults once.

** Layers

A layer is a transparent sheet with something painted on it; the stack composes
top-down. Every non-destructive technique in this guide is layers wearing
costumes: adjustments on their own layer, retouching on a separate sheet,
composites as stacks of photographs. Section 8 is the full treatment.

** Masks

A mask is a greyscale transparency map attached to a layer: white reveals the
layer, black hides it, grey partially shows it. Painting on the mask changes
where the layer appears without ever touching its pixels. Reversible forever.
This asymmetry — pixels sacred, mask disposable — is the whole trick of
professional-looking work.

** Selections

A selection marks a region of the canvas; tools then act only inside it.
Selections and masks are two views of one thing — ~Select → Save to Channel~
stores one, right-click → *Add Layer Mask* → *from selection* converts one to
the other. Fluent users convert between them constantly.

** Destructive vs non-destructive

Destructive: the operation rewrites pixels (painting, direct filter application
on old GIMP). Non-destructive: the operation is stored as instructions
re-applied live — adjustment layers elsewhere, *non-destructive filters* here,
text/vector/link layers. GIMP 3.x made real progress on the second category
(section 9); prefer it wherever possible, because future-you will want to
change things, and future-you is the customer.

* 2. When to open GIMP, and when not to

The most useful thing this guide tells you is when to stay away.

** Stay in your raw editor when...

Exposure, contrast, colour, white balance, sharpening, noise reduction, dust
spots, straightening, cropping, even local masking — all belong upstream in
whatever develops your raws. Those edits are non-destructive, sensor-deep, and
revisable years later. In GIMP the same operations flatten onto rendered
pixels and lose all of that.

- Global tone/colour moves — done upstream, better
- Sharpening and noise reduction — raw editors tune these to your specific
  sensor; GIMP's are generic
- Dust, blemishes, small distractions — darktable's ~retouch~ handles these on
  the raw, including frequency-separation skin work
- Culling, rating, organising — not even remotely GIMP's job
- Anything involving several photos' *library management*

*The rule: if your raw editor can do it, do it there.*

** Open GIMP for these

- *Removing large objects* — people, cars, power lines across a sky. Raw
  editors heal by sampling nearby patches; past a certain size that smears.
  GIMP + Resynthesizer synthesises plausible new texture wholesale (section
  13.1).
- *Combining photographs* — sky replacement, exposure blending, swapping faces
  in group shots. Raw editors process one image at a time; full stop.
- *Adding text, logos, graphic elements* — typography is not a raw editor's
  job at all.
- *Pixel-precise mask work* — hand-painting an edge at 400% zoom. Raw-editor
  masks are parametric/vector; excellent, but not per-pixel brushes.
- *Anything needing a genuine layer stack* — raw editors have pipelines, not
  layer stacks. Different model entirely.
- *Heavy warping* — mesh warp and cage transforms live here (section 12).

If you know Lightroom: GIMP is nothing like it, and darktable is. If you know
Photoshop: GIMP 3.2 covers the photographer-relevant core of it — sections 8–13
are the translation dictionary.

* 3. Install

*Download:* https://www.gimp.org/downloads/ — take the 64-bit Windows
installer from gimp.org itself, not a mirror site dressed like one.

1. Run installer, accept defaults.
2. Launch. First start builds font caches — slower than subsequent ones.
3. Note where plugins live for section 15: ~Edit → Preferences → Folders →
   Plug-ins~ shows the folder list; the user-level one under your AppData is
   where manual installs go.

Version notes: 3.0 broke the plugin API (see section 15's warning); the 32-bit
Windows build ended with 3.2 — irrelevant on modern hardware, but it explains
old download links dying. Updates within 3.x install over the top cleanly;
your settings survive.

* 4. Preferences that matter

Open ~Edit → Preferences~. Four changes, then done:

- *System Resources → Tile cache size:* set 8–16 GB. The tile cache is
  GIMP's working memory for image data; the default is timid. With 48 GB RAM
  you afford generosity, and big layered files stop stuttering.
- *System Resources → Number of threads:* set to maximum/available. No reason
  to leave cores idle.
- *Color Management → Monitor profile:* set to use the system profile ("from
  display profile" / pick your monitor's). Otherwise GIMP and darktable show
  you different colours for the same file and you lose an evening deciding who
  lies. While there, note RGB workspace default (sRGB is correct for delivery;
  leave it).
- *Image Import & Export:* keep the 32-bit float preservation option enabled
  where offered — protects headroom on round trips.

Two non-preference settings worth doing once:

- *Windows → Single-Window Mode* if panels ever float loose. Nobody wants
  floating panels.
- ~Image → Precision~ on any opened photo confirms bit depth. A 16-bit TIFF
  should arrive as 16-bit integer or higher. If it says /8-bit/, something
  upstream exported wrong — go back and re-export rather than edit a cripple.

* 5. The interface in ten minutes

Single-window mode assumed.

- *Centre: canvas.* Zoom with ~+~/~−~ or Ctrl-wheel; ~Ctrl+0~ fits window,
  ~Ctrl+1~ jumps to 100% (the only honest judging zoom). Hold *space* and drag
  to pan.
- *Left: toolbox* — the tool icons — with *Tool Options docked beneath*. The
  options panel changes per tool and is half the program; glance at it every
  time you switch tools, because defaults live there (feather edges! opacity!
  brush size!).
- *Right, upper: Brushes/patterns/fonts tabs.*
- *Right, lower: Layers panel* with Channels, Paths and Undo history tabs.
  You will live in Layers; the others matter (Channels for masks by colour,
  Paths for precise selections, Undo because nobody is perfect).
- *Top: menus.* Everything has a menu home; when lost, hunt menus before
  hunting forums.
- *Menus → Filters → Filters... / GEGL Filter browser (new in 3.2):* searchable
  catalogue of every effect with parameters described — the discovery tool the
  filter menu itself never was.

Keyboard-first tip: pressing ~Tab~ hides docks for distraction-free viewing;
~F11~ full-screens.

* 6. Getting images in and out

** The reliable way: export a TIFF from your raw editor

1. Finish the edit completely upstream. Get it /right/ — nothing flows back
   from GIMP to the raw side.
2. Export *TIFF, 16-bit*, uncompressed or losslessly compressed (darktable:
   section 16 of its guide; Lightroom: export dialog, format TIFF, 16-bit,
   ProPhoto or sRGB per destination).
3. Colour profile: *sRGB* if bound for screen/web, *Adobe RGB* if a print shop
   asks for it. When unsure: sRGB.
4. Open the TIFF in GIMP (drag onto the window works), work, then produce the
   final via Export (section 7).

*16-bit is not negotiable.* An 8-bit file bands visibly the first time a curve
bends, and the camera's dynamic range was thrown away at export for nothing.

** The convenient way: open raw files directly

With *darktable* or *RawTherapee* installed, opening a raw (~.NEF~, ~.CR3~,
~.ARW~) from GIMP auto-launches that program as the loader: develop there,
close, and the processed result lands in GIMP. Genuinely slick when it works.

It has broken across version updates before — documented history, repeatedly.
If it stops working after an update, do not spend an evening debugging plugin
plumbing; export a TIFF (above) and get on with your day.

** One-way trip, stated plainly

Whatever GIMP produces is a new, separate file. Nothing syncs back to the raw
editor's library or history. To keep the result near its siblings, import the
exported file alongside the original in your catalogue. This is why finishing
the raw edit properly first matters: upstream mistakes cannot be fixed
downstream, only papered over.

* 7. Save vs Export — read this before you lose work

This distinction confuses every single newcomer. Learn it now rather than at
1 a.m. with a finished composite and no JPEG in sight.

- ~Ctrl+S~ — *Save.* Writes ~.xcf~, GIMP's own layered working format. An XCF
  is /not a photograph/: browsers can't show it, printers choke on it, your
  family won't know what it is. What it is: the complete editable project —
  layers, masks, text, filters.
- ~Ctrl+Shift+E~ — *Export As…* Writes JPEG/PNG/TIFF/WebP/etc. *This is how an
  actual image leaves GIMP.*
- ~Ctrl+E~ — *Export.* Re-export over the last exported path/format — the
  repeat button for iteration loops.
- ~File → Overwrite~ appears after opening e.g. a PNG directly: writes back
  over that exact file. Convenient; also the fastest way to clobber a source
  image. Respect it.

Working rhythm: save ~.xcf~ whenever the layer stack is worth keeping (it
compresses well), export whenever you want a picture. Doing only the first and
closing means you own a project file and no photograph. There is no built-in
autosave; save early, save often.

* 8. Layers, completely

** Mechanics

The Layers panel lists sheets top-down; the *top* layer paints over those
beneath. Per-layer: visibility eye, opacity slider, mode drop-down, lock
switches (pixels/position/alpha), name (double-click to rename — do rename;
"Layer #47 copy" is how projects die). Right-click a layer for the full menu:
duplicate, delete, flatten, mask operations.

Drag layers to reorder. Drag onto the "create new layer" icon to duplicate.
Ctrl-click several for group moves; ~Ctrl+G~ groups selected layers into a
folder-within-the-stack.

** Blend modes worth knowing

Dozens exist; photographers need six:

- *Normal* — plain stacking. 95% of work.
- *Multiply* — darkens by combining: white passes through unchanged, black
  wins. Classic use: multiply a texture/frame overlay onto a photo so only its
  dark marks show.
- *Screen* — multiply's opposite; black vanishes, light things lighten. Two
  differently-exposed exposures screened together fake a brighter composite.
- *Overlay / Soft Light* — contrast blending; the grey-layer dodge & burn in
  section 12 runs on Overlay.
- *LCh Lightness / LCh Color* (3.x additions) — blend only brightness or only
  colour between layers; the honest way to steal one property without the
  other.
- *Pass through* — technically a group-mode: the group's internal blend modes
  keep affecting layers below the group as if it weren't there. Essential to
  the adjustment-layer recipe (section 9).

Opacity is global per layer; note also the *Fill* vs *Opacity* distinction if
you add layer effects later (fill scales content contribution, not effects).

** Layer masks

Right-click a layer → *Add Layer Mask* → initialise:

- *White*: layer fully visible; paint black on the mask to hide regions.
- *Black*: layer hidden; paint white to reveal. Better default for composites
  (reveal gradually rather than hide accidentally).

Painting rules: soft round brush, low hardness for organic edges; ~[~ and ~]~
resize; press ~X~ to swap black/white as you work both directions; mistakes
paint the opposite colour — nothing is permanent until you apply the mask.
Grey = partial strength. Alt-click a mask thumbnail views it full-screen
greyscale — the honest check of what your brush actually did. Shift-click a
thumbnail disables/enables the mask for before/after comparison. Apply
permanently via right-click → *Apply Layer Mask* only at the end, if ever;
masks cost almost nothing to keep live.

Masks can also be initialised *from selection* (section 10) — the standard
bridge from "I selected the sky" to "this adjustment affects only the sky".

** Non-destructive layer types (3.x)

Four layer kinds now protect themselves from accidental pixel damage:

1. *Text layers* — always re-editable text; font/size/spacing stay adjustable.
2. *Link Layers* (~File → Open as Link Layer~) — like Photoshop's Linked Smart
   Objects: points at an external file which auto-updates in place when that
   file changes elsewhere. The reusable watermark/logo mechanism (section 14).
3. *Vector Layers* (new in 3.2) — the Path tool now creates real vector shapes
   with editable fill and stroke; scale, rotate, transform them losslessly,
   drag-and-drop colours/patterns onto them, edit path nodes any time.
4. Ordinary *pixel layers*, unprotected by design.

All three protected types show a *Rasterize* command in ~Layer~ menu; painting
on them requires rasterizing first (deliberate friction), and *Revert
Rasterize* undoes even that. This killed the classic accident of brushing over
text and losing editability forever.

** Groups

~Ctrl+G~ with layers selected. Groups nest, collapse, move as units, and take
their own masks and filters. Set a group's mode to *Pass through* (next
section) when stacking adjustments inside.

** Merge and flatten, used sparingly

*Merge down* combines one layer into the one beneath; *Flatten image*
collapses everything (respecting a single visible composite). Both destroy
editability of what they consume — useful for exporting steps or taming
hundred-layer files, poisonous by habit. Duplicate the whole image first
(~Image → Duplicate~, or save an .xcf checkpoint) whenever flattening feels
convenient.

* 9. Non-destructive filters, and the adjustment-layer recipe

GIMP 3.0 introduced non-destructive filters; 3.2 made them genuinely useful.

** How NDE filters behave

Apply any GEGL filter (everything under ~Filters~) to a layer and it attaches
to that layer instead of baking in:

- An ~fx~ icon appears on the layer; clicking it lists every applied filter.
- Re-open any filter to change parameters — no undo-history archaeology.
- Toggle filters' eyes on/off for before/after; drag to reorder their math.
- Filters apply to layer *groups* and even *channels*, not just single layers.
- To bake permanently: *rasterize* the layer (or merge down). Until then,
  everything stays live — including across save/close/reopen of .xcf files.

** The adjustment-layer recipe

Photoshop users live on adjustment layers — a recolourable, re-editable effect
sitting above the artwork, affecting everything below. GIMP has no such layer
type. 3.2 makes the workaround exact:

#+begin_quote
*The adjustment-layer recipe*

1. Put your working layers inside a *layer group* (~Ctrl+G~).
2. Set the group's mode to *"Pass through"*.
3. Apply a non-destructive filter *to the group itself*.

Because the group passes through, the filter sees everything beneath it —
and because the filter is non-destructive, its settings remain editable
forever. Stack several filters on the same group for compound treatments.
This is an adjustment layer, constructed rather than granted.
#+end_quote

Concretely: group contains your photo; apply ~Curves~ to the group; the curve
now grades the photo; reopen and retune it next week at will. Write this on a
sticky note; it is completely undiscoverable from the interface and it is the
single most valuable trick in GIMP 3.2.

Two adjacent 3.2 features worth ten minutes:

- *GEGL Filter Browser* (~Filters~ menu): searchable list of every filter
  including ones absent from menus, with parameter docs.
- *CMYK colour selector* with Total Ink Coverage readout — a selector only.
  GIMP still cannot *edit* in CMYK; genuine CMYK output remains Scribus/print-
  shop territory (section 16).

* 10. Selections, completely

Selections restrict where tools act. The craft is choosing the fastest tool
that produces the needed precision — not the most precise tool that exists.

** The tools, by job

- *Free Select* (~F~) — lasso: freehand outline; also polygon mode and
  magnetic-ish edge snapping. Fastest tool for rough organic regions.
- *Rectangle / Ellipse Select* (~R~ / ~E~) — hold Shift to constrain square/
  circle; the Tool Options' "Fixed" toggle makes exact ratios/ sizes.
- *Fuzzy Select* ("magic wand", ~U~) — clicks a colour region by similarity;
  *Threshold* slider in Tool Options is everything. Click-and-drag adjusts
  threshold interactively before releasing.
- *Select by Color* (~Shift+O~) — like Fuzzy but grabs *every* matching pixel
  image-wide, not just contiguous ones. Sky across tree gaps: this one.
- *Scissors* (~I~) — click vertices along an edge; it snaps to contrast.
  Semi-automatic outlining of subjects against busy-but-contrasty backgrounds.
- *Foreground Select* — paint roughly over subject, paint a line across
  background, engine computes the matte. Underrated for people-shapes.
- *Paths* (~B~ on the Paths context) — bezier curves; the precision instrument.
  Convert any path to selection via right-click in Paths panel → *Path to
  Selection*. Slowest, sharpest, never fooled.

** Refining any selection

The ~Select~ menu is where selections get finished:

- *Feather* — softens edges (see the feathering gospel below).
- *Grow / Shrink* — expands/contracts by pixels. Grow-by-5 before healing
  work gives synthesis algorithms breathing room (section 13).
- *Border* — converts selection to a ring of chosen width (frame/border jobs,
  section 14).
- *Invert* (~Ctrl+I~) — flip selected/unselected. Constantly used: select the
  easy thing (sky), invert to have the hard thing (subject).
- *None* ~Ctrl+Shift+A~ / *All* ~Ctrl+A~.

** Quick Mask: paint your selection

Press ~Q~: the image reddens except the selected zone; paint with black to
*remove* from selection, white to add, soft brushes give feathered results
directly. Press ~Q~ again to return to normal view with your painted selection
active. For hand-built masks at high zoom this is faster and saner than any
dialog. (Red overlay colour and its meaning are configurable in preferences.)

** Feathering gospel

#+begin_quote
*A selection's default edge is one hard pixel wide, and it looks like a
ransom note wherever it lands.* Feather every selection destined to be
visible as an edge — 1–3 px for crisp product edges, 5–15 px for anything
organic, more for glow-like blends. Antialiasing (Tool Options checkbox)
smooths stair-stepping but does not blend; feather blends.
#+end_quote

** Selections become masks

Right-click layer → *Add Layer Mask* → initialise from selection, or ~Select →
Save to Channel~ to bank a selection for later recall (channels panel,
right-click → channel to selection). Banked channels turn repeated complex
selections into one-click recalls.

* 11. Colour and tone tools

All live under ~Colors~, all work through the NDE-filter mechanism when applied
to layers/groups (section 9), so every one of these stays re-editable.

- *Curves* — the master control. Drag points to remap input→output tones:
  gentle S-curve = contrast; pull lower-mid down for richness; per-channel
  tabs grade colour (blue-channel-down warms highlights classically). Click
  two points to lock range while bending between them. If you learn one tool
  here fully, make it this one.
- *Levels* — curves reduced to three arrows (black point, gamma, white
  point). Setting black/white points just inside the histogram's true ends is
  the honest first tonal move on scans and flat exports.
- *Exposure* — scene-referred-style stop-based brightness; matches how your
  raw editor thinks.
- *Color Balance* — three wheels (shadows/midtones/highlights): push shadows
  slightly cool, highlights slightly warm for instant "graded" depth. Small
  values only.
- *Hue-Saturation* — per-primary-colour saturation/hue/lightness sliders.
  Taming an oversaturated red jacket without touching the rest lives here.
- *Saturation* and *Scale...* — global desaturation; for monochrome prefer
  *Desaturate → Luminance* (perceptually even) over "lightness" modes.
- *Brightness-Contrast* — exists, is blunt, has no place in finished work.
  Curves does both, better, with control.
- *Colour picker / sample points* — Windows → Dockable Dialogs → Pointer:
  read exact pixel values while grading; trust numbers over eyes for
  neutrality checks (RGB equal = neutral).

Workflow order for photo finishing inside GIMP (rarely needed after good raw
work, but when needed): Levels or Curves for tone → Color Balance/Hue-Sat
for cast and pop → local work (next section) → sharpen last, lightly.

* 12. The retouching toolbox

** Heal (~H)

Ctrl-click a clean source area, then dab the flaw. Heals transfer texture
while blending colour/luminance with surroundings — blemishes vanish without
the waxy smear cloning leaves. First choice for skin, sensor dust that made it
past raw editing, small scratches. Size the brush barely larger than the flaw;
oversized heals sample too much context and go mushy.

** Clone (~C)

Ctrl-click source, paint verbatim copy. For structure, not blending: removing
something by extending real pattern (fence wires, brick rows). Watch alignment
— clone stamp follows relative offset from your source pick; re-pick sources
often to avoid visible tiling.

** Dodge & burn, non-destructively

The darkroom technique for shaping light on faces and landscapes:

1. New layer, filled 50% grey (~Edit → Fill with~ after setting foreground to
   #808080), mode *Overlay*. The layer turns invisible.
2. Paint on it with a large soft brush at 3–8% opacity: *white brush lightens*
   (dodge), *black darkens* (burn).
3. Overshot? Paint back with 50% grey, or drop layer opacity. The underlying
   photograph is untouched; the shaping remains adjustable forever.

Judge at arm's length; dodge&burn at full strength looks like a bad spray tan.

** Warp Transform (~W)

Push/pull/twist pixels through a mesh — fix a bulging horizon, unstick a
poking branch, gentle jawline adjustments. *Undo warp* button reverts strokes;
*Remove dots* relaxes the mesh. Restraint is the skill: viewers feel warped
geometry before they can name it. The *Cage* deform option wraps an object in
a cage of control points — for deforming whole objects coherently rather than
local smudges.

** Perspective and Unified Transform

~Tools → Transform Tools~: straighten keystoned buildings (perspective tool's
guides onto known-verticals), free transforms with rotation/scale/shear in one
gizmo (unified). Crop afterwards; transformed edges leave transparency to fill
or crop away.

* 13. Five jobs, step by step

Complete walkthroughs of the five canonical "this is why GIMP exists" jobs.
Each assumes a 16-bit TIFF arrived from darktable (section 6).

** Remove a large object (Resynthesizer)

Requires the Resynthesizer plugin installed (section 15).

1. *Free Select* (~F~) a loose outline around the object — a person, car, or
   power line. Go *wider* than the object, not tight to it.
2. ~Select → Grow~ by 5–10 px. Synthesis needs surrounding context to sample.
3. ~Filters → Enhance → Heal Selection~.
4. In its dialog: *sampling radius* ≈50 px is a sane start; *fill order*
   random for amorphous texture (grass, gravel), directional/inward for
   structured surroundings. Run.
5. Inspect at 100%. Residual ghosts: smaller second pass, then finish seams
   with Heal (~H~) dabs and Clone (~C~) for structure.
6. Expectations: grass/gravel/sand/water/foliage — excellent. Repeating
   architecture — good if source pattern is regular. Faces and hands — do not;
   heal manually from a donor region instead.

** Sky replacement

1. Base image open; select the old sky: *Select by Color* (~Shift+O~) on blue,
   threshold-tuned, then Q-mask-paint fixes around trees/rooftops.
2. Open replacement sky as new layer (~File → Open as Layers~); drag it
   *beneath* the main layer in the stack.
3. Add layer mask to the *main* layer from your sky selection (mask initialised
   white, then paint the sky area black on the mask — or invert logic: mask
   black, paint white; either way sky region of main layer becomes
   transparent, revealing the new sky below).
4. Scale/move the sky layer (unified transform) until light direction agrees
   with the subject.
5. *Unify the grade:* curves-on-group (section 9 recipe) over both layers —
   nudge the composite's shadows toward one temperature until subject and sky
   belong to the same planet. Mismatched colour temperature outsells every
   other tell.
6. Feather check at 200% around every tree twig; soften mask edges there.

** Exposure blend (two frames, e.g. bright sky + dark ground)

1. Open ground frame; ~File → Open as Layers~ the sky frame, perfectly aligned
   (tripod shots align themselves; handheld needs ~Layer → Transform →
   Offset~/warp nudging against a 50%-opacity ghost).
2. Mask the top layer black-initialised; reveal its good half with soft white
   strokes along the natural boundary (horizon, roofline).
3. Vary mask opacity gradient instead of a hard seam; verify at multiple zooms.
4. Optionally flatten and run a gentle global curve to marry the halves.

** Head swap in a group shot

One person blinked; a near-identical frame has them perfect.

1. Both frames open; copy the good head (~Ctrl+C~ after selecting loosely
   around head+neck in the donor).
2. Paste into base (~Ctrl+V~); the paste lands as a floating selection —
   ~To New Layer~ it immediately.
3. Move/rotate/scale onto the sleeping victim; match scale via ear-to-chin
   distance, rotate via shoulder line.
4. Mask the pasted layer: reveal only the head, feathered generously through
   hairline; align jawline seams along natural shadow boundaries.
5. Grade-check: curves-on-group until skin tones agree (they rarely arrive
   agreeing).

** Clean up skin without destroying texture

First resort: don't come to GIMP — darktable's ~retouch~ wavelets do frequency
separation on the raw file (its guide, section 11). When the file is already
here anyway:

1. *Frequency separation*, manual: duplicate layer twice. Bottom copy:
   ~Filters → Blur → Gaussian~, radius ≈ blur-out all pores (try 4–8 px).
   Top copy: ~Colors → Components → Decompose~... simpler route: set top copy
   mode to *Overlay/Linear light* after applying ~Filters → Enhance → High
   Pass~ with same radius — GIMP's pragmatic approximation of the classic
   technique.
2. Heal (~H~) blemishes *on the blurred copy only*: colour/tones fixed, pore
   detail in the high-pass copy rides through untouched.
3. Merge when satisfied. Result: blemish-free skin that still shows real
   texture — the difference between retouched and rubber.

* 14. Text, logos, borders, watermarks

** Text that stays text

*Text tool* (~T~), click, type. The layer remains a live text layer: font,
size, kerning, colour all re-editable forever (section 8). *Text along path*
turns type onto any bezier curve; 3.2's vector-layer work also improved how
type interacts with shapes and paths. For graphics destined for reuse,
rasterize only at export.

** Reusable logo/watermark via Link Layers

The mechanism 3.2 added for exactly this job:

1. Design the watermark once as its own small file (PNG with transparency or
   SVG — SVG scales perfectly).
2. In any composition: ~File → Open as Link Layer~ → pick it.
3. Position/opacity once per composition. Edit the source file next month in
   any editor; every composition using it updates live.

** Borders and frames

1. ~Image → Canvas Size~, unlock chain-link, add symmetric pixels
   (~Ctrl+Resize by~ offsets centre) — canvas grows without resampling.
2. Background colour shows through; or select-all → ~Select → Border~ of
   chosen width → fill with white/black on a new layer beneath.
3. Flatten at export. Classic white gallery border: 2–4% of long edge.

** Watermarking exports in bulk

GIMP is not a batch watermark engine — one-offs yes, hundreds no. For bulk:
darktable's export module has a watermark tab (its guide, section 16);
Lightroom's export dialog likewise. GIMP's role is designing the mark those
tools then apply.

* 15. Plugins: the two worth installing

#+begin_quote
*A warning that saves hours.* GIMP 3.0 broke the plugin API. Most plugins
written for 2.10 do not work anymore, and the majority of GIMP plugin tutorials
online predate the break and are now fiction. *Check dates on everything.* If
it does not explicitly say GIMP 3, assume dead. Script-Fu scripts mostly
survived; binary plugins did not.
#+end_quote

** G'MIC-Qt

https://gmic.eu/download.html — take the *Windows EXE installer*, which locates
your plugin folder automatically. Restart GIMP → new ~Filters → G'MIC-Qt~
entry: hundreds of filters — effectively a second application bolted on. The
photographer-relevant subset:

- *Repair & denoise group*: several advanced denoisers beyond GIMP's own;
  smooth-but-keep-detail variants worth an evening's comparison.
- *Local contrast enhancement* — another clarity engine, different failures
  than darktable's, useful as second opinion.
- *Sharpening range*: including deconvolution-style sharpeners that genuinely
  out-resolve naive unsharp masking on motion-soft images.
- Film emulation / black&white conversion presets — taste-dependent; try with
  restraint.

Everything runs locally, preview-first, applied to the active layer (and via
NDE when invoked on layers/groups).

** Resynthesizer 3

https://github.com/bootchk/resynthesizer — you need the *resynthesizer3 branch*,
tagged v3.0+. Not the old 2.x most links still target.

This is *content-aware fill* and the main reason GIMP stays installed:
~Filters → Enhance → Heal Selection~ synthesises convincing new texture from
surroundings — the thing spot-removal tools fundamentally cannot do (job 13.1).
Windows installation is fiddlier than it should be: prebuilt 64-bit binaries
circulate on GIMP Chat and the project's wiki installation page; drop them into
the user plug-ins folder shown in preferences (section 3), restart. Reported
working on 3.2.

** Discipline clause

*Install nothing else until a specific problem demands it.* A GIMP full of
half-working plugins is worse than a bare one — crashes cost more time than
features return.

* 16. Exporting, printing, soft-proofing

** Export formats, decided once

- *JPEG* (~Ctrl+Shift+E~): quality 90+ for delivery; "optimize" and
  "progressive" on; embed colour profile checked. Universal.
- *PNG*: transparency or screenshot-sharpness needs; 8-bit fine for web.
- *TIFF*: print-shop interchange or returning to a raw-editor library;
  16-bit, lossless compression (LZW/Deflate — nobody still uses uncompressed).
- *WebP*: smaller than JPEG at similar quality; use when destination is known
  modern.
- *PSD export* exists for handing layered files into Photoshop workflows —
  improved layer-style import in 3.2, though round-trips are never perfect.

Export dialog's *export as* filename field picks format via extension. The
advanced options fold out per-format settings — the defaults are sane except
JPEG quality, which defaults low-ish: raise it and GIMP remembers.

** Scaling down properly

~Image → Scale Image~ with *LoHalo*/NoHalo interpolation for downscaling
photographs (visibly cleaner than the old cubic default). Scale in one step to
target size, then export — repeated JPEG re-saves are what make web images
look chewed.

** Printing

~File → Print~ hands off to Windows' print system with GIMP's colour management
applied. For serious home printing: enable ~Image → Color Management → Soft
Proofing~ against your printer/paper ICC profile first (~View → Color
Management~ toggles), so you preview gamut loss before ink hits paper. Genuine
CMYK separation remains outside GIMP (Scribus or the print shop handles it);
sRGB TIFF + their profile request covers virtually every online lab.

** Soft-proofing generally

View → Color Management enables simulation of how the image renders under a
chosen profile — proof-print look on an sRGB screen, and the honest answer to
"why does my print look duller than my screen".

* 17. Gotchas

1. *Save is not Export.* Section 7. It gets everybody at least once; some
   people twice.
2. *The plugin API break of 3.0.* Pre-2025 plugins and tutorials alike are
   suspect until proven current (section 15).
3. *No real CMYK editing.* 3.2 has a CMYK selector with ink-coverage readout,
   not a CMYK workflow.
4. *The raw-loader hand-off can break on updates.* darktable/RawTherapee
   auto-loading is lovely until it isn't; TIFF fallback always works (section
   6).
5. *8-bit files band under curves.* Check ~Image → Precision~ whenever
   gradients posterise; the cure is upstream re-export, not here.
6. *Unfeathered selections announce themselves.* Feather by default
   (section 10); hard edges belong to graphic design, not photography.
7. *Nothing flows back upstream.* GIMP output is a new file; finish raw edits
   first (section 6).
8. *Floating selections after paste.* ~Ctrl+V~ creates a floating selection
   that must be anchored or promoted (~To New Layer~) before other tools behave.
   Confusion here generates most "GIMP is frozen" reports.
9. *PSD round-trips are lossy in both directions.* Layer styles/effects
   translate imperfectly; keep originals in native formats.
10. *Undo has memory limits.* Set generous tile cache (section 4); for huge
    multi-layer files save .xcf checkpoints rather than trusting history depth.

* Appendix A. Brushes, gradients, and painting

Mask-painting and retouching run on the brush engine; ten minutes here pays
out across every other section.

** Brush anatomy

Tool Options for ~P~ (Paintbrush): *brush* (shape preset), *Size*, *Aspect
ratio*, *Angle*, *Spacing* (stamps per stroke length — raise above 50% for
dotted effects, lower for solid), *Hardness* (edge softness — 0.5 is the
mask-painting sweet spot), *Force/Opacity* semantics per tool. Dynamics
(size/opacity varying with pressure or velocity) matter on tablets only.

Advice: build a personal top-five brush set (hard round, soft round, large
soft for dodging, textured brush for masks, pencil-thin for cleanup) instead
of browsing the hundred shipped presets you will never use again.

** The three paint tools that matter

- ~P~ Paintbrush — standard.
- ~N~ Pencil — hard aliased pixels; pixel-art and one-px mask surgery.
- ~E~ Eraser — same engine, removes to transparency (layer-bound; erasing a
  mask thumbnail isn't a thing — paint black there instead).

Airbrush (~A~) keeps depositing while held — GIMP's flow analogue; use it
where Photoshop habits reach for flow.

** Gradients

~G~: drag on canvas to place. The shape family matters more than colours:

- *Linear/Radial* — masks' graduated filters.
- *Conical/Spiral/Starburst* — graphic work.
- Shape variants (spherical, dimpling) — rarely photographic.

*Fg→Bg (RGB)* with pure black/white is the mask gradient; *Fg→Transparent*
paints colour fades over photos without obliterating them. Advice: gradient
on a layer mask is GIMP's honest answer to raw editors' graduated filters —
drag once, re-drag anytime by re-running from the mask edge.

** Pattern fill and bucket

~Shift+Ctrl+B~ fills with background; bucket ~Shift+B~ fills by region
similarity (its threshold behaves like fuzzy-select). Patterns tile from the
patterns dock; useful for textures behind cutouts, not for photographs.

* Appendix B. Channels, paths, and luminosity masks

The panels next door to Layers, and the techniques they unlock.

** Channels panel

One greyscale channel per stored selection plus R/G/B of the image itself.
Ctrl-click any channel = load its luminance as a selection. This is the
raw material of manual precision masking:

- Ctrl-click *Red/Green/Blue* → selections keyed to that channel's brightness
  (blue channel selects skies ruthlessly on daylight shots).
- ~Select → Save to Selection~ banks any selection as a new channel — your
  library of hard-won masks, recallable with one click forever after.
- Advice: before hand-painting any complicated subject mask, spend thirty
  seconds ctrl-clicking each RGB channel looking for one that nearly isolates
  it already. Frequently one does, and painting shrinks to touch-up.

** Luminosity masks, manually

No plugins needed:

1. ~Layer → Duplicate~ the base image twice.
2. Top copy: ~Colors → Desaturate~ (Luminance) then ~Colors → Invert~;
   blend mode *Multiply* — now darkens only what was bright (a shadows-
   protecting darkening layer).
3. Variants: skip the invert for brightening-brights; add a Gaussian-blurred
   copy in Overlay for glow-free contrast.
4. Mask further with channel-loaded selections from Appendix B.1.

This is the whole trick professional "luminosity masking panels" automate.
Three layers, no cost, fully inspectable.

** Paths deep dive

~B~ switches to Pen within the Paths context: click vertices (straight),
click-drag for bezier handles. The Paths panel stores every path permanently —
unlike throwaway selections. Right-click a path:

- *Path to Selection* (also Enter) — with feather dialog via Select menu
  afterwards.
- *Stroke Path* — paint a chosen brush along it (hairline cleanup along
  perfect curves).
- *Fill Path*.

Advice: paths are the only selection method that survives save/close/reopen
with full fidelity. For recurring product/client shapes, path libraries beat
re-selecting every time.

* Appendix C. Script-Fu and batch processing

GIMP's scripting is where photographers' patience usually ends and engineers'
begins. The middle path:

** The Script-Fu console

~Filters → Script-Fu → Console~: a live REPL driving the whole program.
Useful one-liners to feel it:

#+begin_example
(let* ((image (car (gimp-file-load RUN-NONINTERACTIVE "in.tiff" "in.tiff"))))
  (gimp-image-flatten image)
  (file-jpeg-save RUN-NONINTERACTIVE image (car (gimp-image-get-active-drawable image)) "out.jpg" "out.jpg" 0.92 0 1 1 "" 0 1 0 0))
#+end_example

Loads, flattens, exports at quality 92 — without dialogs. Every menu action
has such a procedure; the *Procedure Browser* (~Help~) documents them with
signatures.

** Batch converting a folder without opening files

Script-Fu server or console loops work; the pragmatic Windows route is the
BIMP plugin (Batch Image Manipulation Plugin) — check its GIMP-3
compatibility before installing (section 15's warning applies). For pure
format conversion at scale, honest advice: ImageMagick from PowerShell beats
any in-GIMP solution:

#+begin_example
# every TIFF in a folder to JPEG q92, full width preserved
magick mogrify -format jpg -quality 92 *.tiff
#+end_example>

** When GIMP batch is genuinely worth it

Operations needing layers/masks/selection logic per file (watermark placement
relative to content, conditional framing). Otherwise: raw-editor export
presets handle volume, ImageMagick handles conversion; GIMP scripting handles
the structural middle case.

* Appendix D. Working with PSD files

Photoshop files arrive from clients/colleagues; GIMP 3.x opens them well and
writes them imperfectly:

- Opening: layer structure, masks, text (converted), blend modes mostly
  survive; layer *styles* (strokes/shadows as live effects) import improved in
  3.2 but never perfectly; adjustment layers flatten into their effect.
- Saving back to PSD: possible (~File → Save As~ .psd); expect round-trip
  drift on anything fancy. Advice: keep edits native-XCF while in your hands,
  export PSD only at hand-back, flattened copy included for safety.
- Smart objects import as raster snapshots (their live link is Adobe-side);
  text layers become editable-but-reformatted. Budget re-typesetting for
  type-heavy PSDs.

The professional courtesy rule: tell Photoshop-using collaborators you're on
GIMP *before* they send layered masters, not after.

* Appendix E. One composite, end to end

Continuing the exact photograph developed in ~DARKTABLE-README.org~ §17: the
reception frame whose TIFF just arrived, 16-bit, fully graded — with one
problem no raw editor should fix: a glowing green EXIT sign over the subject's
head, and a blown-out window behind her worth improving.

What I would actually do, in order, with reasons:

1. *Precision pass before surgery*: zoom to 100% and walk the frame once.
   Two sensor spots survived upstream (darktable healed three; these hide in
   shadow), plus a waiter's elbow at frame edge. Heal ~H~ dabs, thirty
   seconds. Advice: always do this sweep *before* big work — composites make
   you forget dust until it's flattened in forever.
2. *The exit sign.* Free Select (~F~) loosely around glow included,
   ~Select → Grow~ 6 px, ~Filters → Enhance → Heal Selection~, sampling
   radius 50, fill order random (wall texture is amorphous). Result: 90%
   convincing. Advice: judge synthesis at 100% AND at arm's length — each
   catches artefacts the other forgives. Residual ghosting: second smaller
   pass, then two Heal (~H~) dabs sampling from clean wall.
3. *The window.* Decision point, stated honestly: replacing this window's
   view means sourcing a matching exposure — more work than value for an
   album shot. What I would do instead: Curves adjustment-layer-recipe group
   (§9) masked to the window via Select-by-Color + feathered quick-mask
   touch-up, pulling highlights −0.4 and warming +200K equivalent. The
   "improvement" is tonal, not synthetic — five minutes, invisible seams by
   construction. Advice: prefer edits that can't seam to edits that must not.
4. *Local finish*: 50%-grey Overlay layer (§12), white strokes at 5% opacity
   lifting her face half a stop relative to the room; black at 4% calming the
   tablecloth's hot fold. Arm's-length checks every few strokes.
5. *Output sharpening last*: duplicate flattened copy, High Pass radius 1.5,
   Linear Light, masked to eyes/jewellery only. Advice: on already-raw-
   sharpened 16-bit files, less is almost always more — sharpen decisions,
   not surfaces.
6. *Export discipline*: ~Ctrl+S~ the .xcf first (the layered master lives in
   the project folder beside the TIFF), then ~Ctrl+Shift+E~ JPEG q92 sRGB
   2048px for the album, full-size TIFF back to darktable's folder where its
   guide's workflow imports it beside the original raw as the delivered
   variant.

Total time: eleven minutes. Every layer named by intent, nothing flattened,
master archived — future-you inherits an editable decision tree, not a
flattened apology.

* Appendix F. Every preference and option, explained

The reference layer under §4: the full preferences tree, then every tool's
options bar — one line each.

** Preferences → System Resources

- *minimal number of undo levels* — guaranteed undo depth per image (raise from 5).
- *maximum undo memory* — RAM ceiling for history stacks; raise on 48 GB machines.
- *tile cache size* — working memory for image data (§4's 8–16 GB).
- *maximum new image size* — guard rail against accidental giant documents.
- *number of threads* — CPU parallelism; max it.

** Preferences → Color Management

- *mode of operation* — none / display-managed / soft-proofing; leave managed.
- *RGB workspace profile* — default document space (sRGB is right).
- *monitor profile* — system-provided vs manual ICC pick (§4's honesty switch).
- *display rendering intent* — perceptual default; relative colorimetric for punchier proofing.
- *soft-proofing profile + intent + black point compensation* — printer-simulation trio for §16.

** Preferences → Image Import & Export

- *default export quality/format memory* — remembers last JPEG quality etc.
- *metadata policies (EXIF/XMP/IPTC)* — which metadata rides along on export.
- *32-bit float import/export where offered* — headroom preservation (§4).

** Preferences → Tool Options / Default Image / Grid

- *default interpolation* — transform resampling quality; NoneHalo/LoHalo best for photos, Cubic safe general.
- *marching ants speed* — selection-dash animation rate.
- *default new-image template/size* — what ~Ctrl+N~ opens.
- *default grid spacing/colour* — canvas alignment grid cosmetics.

** Preferences → Interface, Theme, Window Management

- *theme / icon theme* — skin choices; dark themes flatter photo work.
- *keyboard shortcut behaviour (save on exit)* — whether customised keys persist automatically.
- *window hints/docking behaviour* — panel snapping style.
- *use single-window mode by default* — §4's tidiness, settable as default.

** Preferences → Display / Input Devices / Folders

- *transparency type (checkerboard size/colours)* — how transparency renders on screen.
- *display resolution (calibrate)* — DPI truthfulness for print-size views.
- *extended input devices* — tablet configuration home.
- *mouse-wheel controllers* — remappable wheel behaviours (zoom, opacity…).
- *folders: plug-ins/scripts/brushes/patterns/gradients/palettes/fonts* — search paths §15 installs into.

** Tool options bars, line per option

- *Move (~V~): pick a layer/path toggle · move layer/selection/path* — what gets dragged when you drag.
- *Rectangle/Ellipse (~R~/~E~): mode replace-add-subtract-intersect · feather edges+radius · antialiasing · rounded corners · expand from centre · fixed ratio/size · numeric position/size* — selection geometry discipline in one bar.
- *Free Select (~F~): polygonal toggle · feather · antialiasing* — lasso refinement.
- *Fuzzy Select (~U~): threshold · select-by channel · sample merged · diagonal neighbours · antialiasing/feather* — magic-wand sensitivity plumbing.
- *Select by Color (~Shift+O~): same family minus contiguity* — image-wide colour grabbing.
- *Scissors (~I~): interactive boundary display · feather* — edge-snapping outline controls.
- *Paintbrush/Pencil (~P~/~N~): opacity · paint mode · brush picker · size · aspect ratio · angle · spacing · hardness · force · dynamics · incremental · jitter · smoothing* — Appendix A's engine, exhaustively.
- *Eraser (~E~): anti-erase to background vs transparency · hardness family* — what erasing reveals.
- *Airbrush (~A~): rate · flow* — continuous deposition tuning.
- *Clone (~C~)/Heal (~H~): alignment none-aligned-fixed · source · sample merged · offset fields* — donor mechanics (§12).
- *Blur/Sharpen: rate · convolve type toggle* — local focus effects.
- *Smudge: rate · no-erasing option* — pixel smearing strength.
- *Dodge/Burn (~Shift+B~): exposure · type shadows-midtones-highlights · mode dodge-burn* — zone-targeted lightening/darkening (grey-layer method preferred, §12).
- *Gradient (~G~): FG-BG/FG-transparent etc. · shape linear-radial-conical-spiral-starburst · repeat policy · dithering · adaptive supersampling · instantaneous preview* — Appendix A's gradient machinery.
- *Bucket Fill (~Shift+B~): fill similar-colours vs fill-selection · threshold · sample merged · fill transparent areas · supersampling* — region filling semantics.
- *Text (~T~): font/style/size · antialiasing · hinting · justification · colour · box dynamic-vs-fixed* — live-text controls (§14).
- *Paths (~B~): design vs edit mode · polygonal · create-selection/stroke/fill buttons* — Appendix B's precision instrument.
- *Crop (~Shift+C~): current-layer-only · allow growing · expand from centre · fixed options · size fields* — framing mechanics.
- *Unified Transform (~M~)/Rotate/Scale/Shear/Perspective: interpolation dropdown · clipping policy · show preview · guides options* — geometry suite defaults.
- *Warp Transform (~W~): behaviour buttons (move-grow-shrink-swirl-erase-smooth) · size · strength · averaging · adaptive spacing* — §12's warp arsenal.
- *Zoom (~Z~): direction · resize-window toggle* — navigation only.
